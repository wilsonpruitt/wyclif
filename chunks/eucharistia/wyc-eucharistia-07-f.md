# *De eucharistia*, chapter 7, part 6: enigmatic vision, and the three rejoinders that the body of Christ is seen in the host

## ¶1 · 209.24–210.8

> [p. 209] Tercia autem visio intellectualis est enigmatica, que est de pure credito infusa obscura noticia, modo quo loquitur Apostolus I. Cor. XIIIᵒ, 12: Videmus nunc per speculum in enigmate, tunc autem facie ad faciem. Et noticiam istius modi habent tam boni quam mali in quolibet inicio noticie rei insensibilis, sic quod quandoque simul currunt iste tres noticie. Et communiter precedit visio enigmatica, cum oportet nos procedere ab imperfecto ad perfectum. Sic ergo novem modis satis equivoce sumitur visio in scriptura. [p. 210] Quandoque autem sumitur visio aggregative pro multis visionibus; unde in Deo distinguitur visio intuicionis qua Deus videt existencia presencia, preterita et futura. Omnia enim illa intuetur, cum existunt sibi presencia, sed possibilia que non existunt videt Deus noticia simplicis apprehensionis. Unde quamvis oporteat illa esse, non tamen oportet illa existere (sicut patet alibi diffuse).

And the third intellectual vision is enigmatic, which is an infused, obscure knowledge of what is purely believed, in the way the Apostle speaks, 1 Cor. 13:12: "We see now through a glass in a dark manner: but then face to face." And knowledge of this kind both good and bad men have at every beginning of knowledge of an [imperceptible]{g:insensibilis} thing, so that sometimes these three knowledges run together. And commonly [enigmatic vision]{g:visio-intuitiva} goes first, since we must proceed from the imperfect to the perfect. So therefore vision [is taken]{g:sumitur-pro} in scripture in nine ways, [equivocally]{g:equivoce} enough.[^1] [210] And sometimes vision is taken [aggregatively]{g:aggregative} for many visions; hence in God there is distinguished the vision of intuition, by which God sees existing things, present, past and future. For he intuits all those things, since they exist present to him; but possible things which do not exist God sees by the knowledge of simple apprehension. Hence although it must be that they are, yet it is not necessary that they exist (as is clear elsewhere at length).[^2]

## ¶2 · 210.9–210.17

> [p. 210] Et per istam distinccionem potest intelligi scriptura que videtur contradicere sibi ipsi. Nam Exodi XXXIIIᵒ, 20 capitulo dicit Deus: Non me videbit homo et vivet, hoc est, non tunc vivet vitam naturalem, dum deitatem fuerit intuitus, ut fuit de Moyse et Paulo in raptibus, et ut evenit de beatis; vivunt enim tunc aliam vitam spiritualem mortificati mundo. Et hec est racio quare debemus mundum relinquere et non implicare nos terrestribus sed spiritualibus inhiare.

And by this distinction scripture can be understood which seems to contradict itself. For in Exodus 33:20 God says: "Man shall not see me, and live"; that is, he will not then live the natural life while he has intuited [the Godhead]{g:deitas}, as it was with Moses and Paul in their raptures, and as happens with the blessed; for they then live another, spiritual life, mortified to the world.[^3] And this is the reason why we ought to leave the world and not entangle ourselves in earthly things but gape after spiritual ones.

## ¶3 · 210.18–211.20

> [p. 210] Et sic intelligitur illud Johannis Iᵒ, 18: Deum nemo vidit unquam. Et illud Apostoli I. Cor. IIᵒ, 9: Nec oculus vidit nec auris audivit nec in cor hominis ascendit cum eis similibus. Notandum tamen quod tales negative quandoque excedunt claram intuicionem divine essencie et quandoque equalitatem visibilitatis divine. Quamvis enim beati clare videbunt divinam essenciam, non tamen secundum ultimum sue visibilitatis, cum hoc soli Deo sit proprium. Necesse itaque est tales distincciones cognoscere, nam propter talem ignoranciam et alias malicias dicitur Manassen regem Juda impetere Isaiam super heretica pravitate et ipsum usque ad mortem serrare. Dicit enim (ut supra [p. 211] exposui): Vidi dominum sedentem super solium excelsum. Et Deus dicit Moysi: Non videbit me homo et vivet. Sed concordantur per hoc quod propheta vidit Dominum visione abstractiva cum visione alia quam habuit de ymagine vel figura; per hoc enim quod Ysaias vidit talem ymaginem oculo corporali et visio deitatis fuit sibi infusa cum visione abstractiva quam Deo movente elicuit, Deum super alios intellexit, et sic generaliter in lege veteri, quando dicitur quod videt vel audit Dominum, intelligitur de sensacione in signo cum noticia abstractiva divine essencie, non quod tunc intuitus est Deum, ut sepe declarat Augustinus et specialiter in libro Sermonum Sermone primo: Apparere, inquit, per substanciam suam, sicuti est, nonnisi mundis cordibus dignatur, ut dicit evangelium; oculis autem corporalibus sanctorum. Si quando Deus voluit apparere, non per se ipsum, sed per creaturam sensibilem, quantum ista carne sentiri potest apparuit. Et post diffuse declarat istam sentenciam.

And so is understood that of John 1:18: "No man hath seen God at any time." And that of the Apostle, 1 Cor. 2:9: "Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard: neither hath it entered into the heart of man", with the like. Yet it is to be noted that such negatives sometimes exceed clear intuition of the divine essence and sometimes the equality of the divine visibility.[^4] For although the blessed will clearly see the divine essence, yet not according to the utmost of its visibility, since this is proper to God alone. It is therefore necessary to know such distinctions; for on account of such ignorance, and other malices, Manasses king of Judah is said to have attacked Isaiah on [a charge of] [heretical depravity]{g:heretica-pravitas} and to have sawn him through to death.[^5] For he says (as I expounded [211] above): "I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high." And God says to Moses: "Man shall not see me, and live." But they are reconciled by this: that the prophet saw the Lord by abstractive vision together with another vision which he had of an image or [figure]{g:figura}; for by this, that Isaiah saw such an image with the bodily eye, and the vision of the Godhead was infused into him together with the abstractive vision which he elicited by God's moving, he understood God above others. And so generally in the old law, when it is said that one sees or hears the Lord, it is understood of [sensation]{g:sensacio} in a sign together with [abstractive]{g:abstractive} knowledge of the divine essence, not that he then intuited God, as Augustine often [makes clear]{g:declarare}, and especially in the book of Sermons, Sermon 1: "To appear," he says, "through his substance, as he is, he deigns only to the clean of heart, as the gospel says; but to the bodily eyes of the [saints]{g:sancti}, if ever God willed to appear, he appeared not through himself but through a [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} [creature]{g:creatura}, so far as he can be [perceived]{g:sentire} by this flesh."[^6] And afterwards he makes this [teaching]{g:sentencia} clear at length.

## ¶4 · 211.21–212.6

> [p. 211] Et patet quod sicut nemo sufficit Deum videre oculo corporali, sic nec corpus Christi in hostia consecrata, quia si sufficeret videre sic illud corpus ibi, sufficeret videre sic illud corpus esse ibi, et discernere cuiusmodi est ibi, quod est contra experienciam et determinacionem Urbani quinti cum suis cardinalibus. Videt, inquam, fide et intellectu corpus Christi in hostia consecrata, et sic per istam distinccionem de visione intelligende sunt alie scripture ut Math. Vᵗᵒ, 8, ubi promittitur pro finali premio beati mundo corde quoniam ipsi Deum videbunt. [p. 212] Ibi patet quod loquitur de intuicione in patria. Sed Joh. XIVᵒ, 9 dicit: Philippe, qui videt me, videt et patrem meum; ubi videtur loqui de visione abstractiva et visione infusa. Qui enim videt Dei filium naturalem sub racione qua talis, videt abstractive Deum patrem; et illa visio Christi pertinet ad fidelem supra bestiam.

And it is clear that, just as no one is able to see God with the bodily eye, so neither [can he see] [the body of Christ]{g:corpus-christi} in the [consecrated host]{g:hostia-consecrata}; for if he were able to see that body there in this way, he would be able to see in this way that that body is there, and to discern of what kind it is there, which is against experience and the [determination]{g:determinacio} of Urban V with his cardinals.[^7] He sees, I say, the body of Christ in the consecrated host by faith and understanding; and so by this distinction about vision other scriptures are to be understood, as Matt. 5:8, where there is promised as the final reward: "Blessed are the clean of heart: they shall see God." [212] There it is clear that it speaks of intuition in [the homeland]{g:patria}. But John 14:9 says: "Philip, he that seeth me seeth my Father also"; where it seems to speak of abstractive vision and infused vision.[^8] For he who sees the natural Son of God under the aspect by which he is such sees God the Father abstractively; and that vision of Christ belongs to the believer, above the beast.[^9]

## ¶5 · 212.7–212.16

> [p. 212] Sed replicatur contra istud primo per hoc quod oculus corporalis est causa visionis Dei a nobis, ergo nos videmus Deum oculo corporali. Sed sic arguens remittendus est ad grammaticos pro cognoscendo regimine ablativi casus, ut dixi sepius. Illi enim talo et pedibus senciunt et intelligunt quidquid apprehenderint sicut fingunt; ideo oportet volentes loqui in ista materia notare grammaticam, loycam et philosophiam cum distinccione visionis predicta, et aliter sunt a disputacione istius materie exsufflandi.

But against this [it is rejoined]{g:replicatur}, first, by this: that the bodily eye is a cause of the vision of God by us; therefore we see God with the bodily eye. But one who argues so is to be sent back to the grammarians to learn the government of the ablative case, as I have said more than once.[^10] For they, as they feign, perceive and understand with the ankle and the feet whatever they have apprehended; therefore those who wish to speak in this matter must attend to grammar, logic and philosophy, with the distinction of vision given above, and otherwise they are to be [blown out]{g:exsufflare} of the disputation of this matter.

## ¶6 · 212.17–212.19

> [p. 212] Secundo replicatur per hoc quod iuxta sepe dicta hostia consecrata est corpus Christi, et illa ibi videtur oculo corporali, ergo et corpus Christi.

Secondly it is rejoined by this: that, according to what has often been said, the consecrated host is the body of Christ, and it is seen there with the bodily eye; therefore the body of Christ [is seen] too.

## ¶7 · 212.20–212.28

> [p. 212] Sed patet quod concedentes recte assumptum sunt sic glosandi: Hostia sacra figurat sacramentaliter corpus Christi; sed supposito quod ecclesia admittat loycam istam ut olim, patet quod propter equivocacionem perit illacio, cum sic arguitur: Hostia figurat corpus Christi et ipsa sentitur ibidem, ergo et corpus Christi. Sic enim argueretur quod deitas sit sensibilis quolibet quinque sensuum quandocunque aliquid sentiretur, omnis namque creatura figurat et dicit deitatem.

But it is clear that those who rightly grant [the assumption]{g:assumptum} are [to be glossed]{g:glosare} thus: "The [sacred]{g:sacer} host [sacramentally]{g:sacramentaliter} [figures]{g:figurare} the body of Christ";[^11] but, supposing that the [church]{g:ecclesia} admits this [logic]{g:loyca} as formerly, it is clear that through [equivocation]{g:equivocacio} the [illation]{g:illacio} perishes, when it is argued thus: "The host figures the body of Christ, and it is perceived there; therefore the body of Christ [is perceived] too." For so it would be argued that the Godhead is perceptible by any of the five senses whenever anything were perceived; for every creature figures and [signifies]{g:dicere} the Godhead.[^12]

## ¶8 · 212.29–213.16

> [p. 212] Tercio replicatur per hoc quod nulla substancia videtur oculo corporali nisi quia suum accidens videtur oculo corporali per se, cum ergo indumentum vel [p. 213] armatura corporis Christi per se videtur in hostia, videtur quod corpus Christi videbitur consequenter. Confirmatur ex predicta sentencia Augustini dicentis quod Deus in ista carne potest sentiri oculis corporalibus, sicut dicimus parietem colorari et videri per spissas picturas parieti collinitas. Istam autem arguciam magnificant ydiote, asserentes quod solum accidens vere sentitur. Sed ignorant philosophiam quomodo nullum accidens potest sentiri nisi in virtute substancie sustentantis, cum nullum accidens sit nisi substanciam accidentari, hoc est, modus accidentalis substancie. Accidens tamen videtur per se et subiectum per accidens; cum ergo nullum accidens corporis Christi in hostia videtur oculo corporali, manifestum est quod ipsum non videtur sic per accidens sed ipsa hostia.

Thirdly it is rejoined by this: that no [substance]{g:substancia} is seen with the bodily eye except because its [accident]{g:accidens} is seen [of itself]{g:per-se} with the bodily eye; since, therefore, the garment or [213] [armament]{g:armatura} of the body of Christ is seen of itself in the host, it seems that the body of Christ will be seen in consequence. This is confirmed from Augustine's teaching mentioned above, who says that God can be perceived in this flesh by bodily eyes, just as we say that a wall is coloured and seen through thick paintings [smeared on]{g:linire} the wall.[^13] And this [quibble]{g:argucia} the [unlettered men]{g:ydiota} magnify, asserting that only the accident is [truly]{g:vere} perceived. But they do not know philosophy, how no accident can be perceived except in the power of the sustaining substance, since no accident is anything but substance's being accidented, that is, the accidental mode of substance. Yet an accident is seen of itself and the subject [by accident]{g:per-accidens}; since, therefore, no accident of the body of Christ is seen in the host with the bodily eye, it is manifest that it is not seen so by accident, but the host itself [is].[^14]

## ¶9 · 213.17–213.20

> [p. 213] Et quantum ad Augustinum, patet quod ipse extendit sensacionem ad omnem noticiam intuitivam, iuxta illud Apostoli Rom. XIᵒ, 34: Quis enim cognovit sensum Domini?

And as to Augustine, it is clear that he extends sensation to all intuitive knowledge, according to that of the Apostle, Rom. 11:34: "For who hath known the mind of the Lord?"[^15]

- 210.9: These distinctions explain apparent contradictions in Scripture;
- 210.19: e. g. as to man seeing God.

- 213.1: and that in seeing the accidents of the host we see Christ's body.
- 213.12: But the accidents that we see are not accidents of Christ's body.

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** Nine ways: three of bodily, three of imaginary and three of intellectual vision (07-e, 207–209).

[^2]: **C.** *Esse* against *existere*: possible things must "be" (in God's knowledge) without "existing". "Elsewhere" is not identified in the Latin.

[^3]: **S.** Exod. 33:20; Wyclif's order *Non me videbit homo* (the Vulgate *non … videbit me homo*), the same sense. Paul's rapture: 2 Cor. 12:2–4. D *vitam animalem*; ABCE *et raptibus*.

[^4]: **C/T.** *Excedunt* "exceed" is the print, with no variant; the sense required, "sometimes exclude clear intuition … sometimes [only] equality with the divine visibility", suggests a corruption (*excludunt*?), unresolved and translated as printed.

[^5]: **S.** The tradition that Manasses had Isaiah sawn asunder, with the charge that Isaiah's "I saw the Lord" contradicted Moses' "Man shall not see me", is not named in the Latin; Loserth gives only Isa. 6:1 (LEDGER K07f-1). D *sarrare*.

[^6]: **S/T.** Wyclif's "Sermon 1"; Loserth: correctly *Sermo* XVII *De diversis* = *Sermo* VI, *De eo quod apparuit Dominus*, Opp. V (LEDGER K07f-2). Loserth stops after *sanctorum*; the sentence is read across the stop. ABCE *Sed quando*; C *Debet quando voluerit*; ABC *sentire*. "The clean of heart": Matt. 5:8.

[^7]: **R.** Urban V (pope 1362–70) "with his cardinals": the determination is not identified in the Latin or by Loserth (LEDGER K07f-3).

[^8]: **S.** John 14:9: Wyclif adds *meum* ("my Father"), translated; the Vulgate has *videt et Patrem*. ABC *Philippo*.

[^9]: **C.** *Sub racione qua talis*: under the aspect of being the natural Son. "Above the beast": the believer's sight, unlike an animal's, goes beyond the bodily eye.

[^10]: **C.** The ablative *oculo corporali* names the organ by which one sees; a cause of the vision (the eye moving the mind) is not thereby the means of seeing God.

[^11]: **C.** *Recte* may go with *concedentes* ("those who rightly grant") or with *glosandi* ("are rightly to be glossed"). *Assumptum*: the premiss "the consecrated host is the body of Christ" (212.18).

[^12]: **C.** *Illacio*, the drawing of the conclusion, rendered "illation" to keep it apart from *consequencia* "inference". *Ut olim*: as the church once admitted this manner of speaking.

[^13]: **S.** "Augustine's teaching mentioned above" is the sermon at 211.14–19; Loserth also cites Opp. V, 34, 313; VIII, 866 (LEDGER K07f-4). *Indumentum vel armatura*: the accidents of the host as the body's clothing.

[^14]: **C.** *Accidentari*: "substance's being accidented", the verb beside *accidentatus*. The subject seen by accident is the host, whose accidents are seen of themselves.

[^15]: **S/C.** Rom. 11:34. The Douay–Rheims "mind" renders *sensum*, the word for sense; Wyclif cites the verse because it calls knowledge *sensus*.
