# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 8, part 1: against Christ's identity of species, the first two objections

## ¶1 · 123.0–123.0

<!-- the editor's bracketed argument at the chapter head: a Latin summary, then his own English
     summary in the same bracket (see the volume CARD for what is translated) -->

> [p. 123] [Obicit tripliciter contra ydemptitatem specificam Christi cum aliis, et dissolvit. Examination of three objections to the statement that Christ is man in the same sense of the word as other men: i.e. that Christ is of the same species as other men.]

[It objects in three ways against the [identity of species]{g:ydemptitas-specifica} of Christ with others, and resolves the objections.]

## ¶2 · 123.2–123.12

> [p. 123] Ex istis patet solucio argumentorum factorum communiter ad probandum quod Christus non sit univoce homo cum aliis. Primo per hoc, quod nichil reponitur in specie per aliquod sibi accidentale: sed humanitas Christi est sibi accidentalis: ergo non est in nova specie propter illam: sed ante incarnacionem non fuit eiusdem speciei nobiscum: ergo nec¹ post propter² adventiciam unionem. Confirmatur per locum a simili. Nam si homo de novo acquirat albedinem vel quamcumque formam aliam accidentaliter³ inherentem, non ex hoc⁴ est disparis speciei: ergo per idem nec Christus propter humanitatem tam adventiciam.

From these things the solution is clear of the arguments commonly made to prove "Christ is not [univocally]{g:univoce} man with others." First, by this: nothing is placed in a species through something accidental to it: now the [humanity]{g:humanitas} of Christ is accidental to him: therefore he is not in a new species because of it: now before the incarnation he was not of the same species with us: therefore neither after, because of an adventitious [union]{g:unio}. This is confirmed by the topic from likeness. For if a man newly acquires whiteness, or any other form inhering accidentally, he is not by this of a different species: therefore, by the same token, neither is Christ, because of a humanity so adventitious.

## ¶3 · 123.13–123.21

> [p. 123] Quantum ad illud patet ex dictis quod falsum assumitur. Nam Verbum, quod prius erat super omnem speciem, per assumpcionem humanitatis factum est in nostra specie et ‘sub lege.’ Et quantum ad similitudinem⁵ patet ex dictis racionis diversitas. Si enim per impossibile albedo vel alia forma inherens esset illud, quo subiectum esset aliquid vel aliud quam prius fuerat, (sicut⁶ humanitate fit Verbum aliquid aliud quam prius fuerat),⁶ procederet racio per locum a simili: sed antecedens est impossibile.

As to that, it is clear from what has been said that something false is assumed.[^1] For [the Word]{g:verbum}, who before was above every species, by the [assumption]{g:assumpcio} of the humanity was made in our species and "under the law" (Gal 4:4). And as to the likeness, the difference of the reasoning is clear from what has been said. For if, per impossibile, whiteness or another inhering form were that by which the [subject]{g:subiectum} would be something, or other than it had been before (as by the humanity the Word becomes something other than he had been before), the argument would proceed by the topic from likeness: but the antecedent is impossible.

## ¶4 · 123.22–124.16

> [p. 123] Et si multiplicentur argumenta sophistica ad probandum quod, quicquid est Verbum, eternaliter fuit Verbum; patet ex dictis de formis solucio. Nam aliquid, quia aliqua [p. 124] forma substancialis vel natura,¹ nunc est Verbum, quod non in primo instanti temporis erat Verbum. Non enim antequam ‘Verbum caro factum est’ ‘in plenitudine temporis,’ erat homo; et certum² est quod esse hominem est esse aliquid: ideo quando fiebat homo, fiebat aliquid,³ quod prius non fuerat. Verumptamen omne suppositum, ypostasis, vel persona, que est, vel que⁴ fuit, Verbum, eternaliter erat⁵ Verbum. Sed absit ex isto concludere—ergo quicquid.⁶ Aliud enim, sed⁷ non alius, est Verbum post incarnacionem quam prefuit.⁸ Et sic querenti—Quis est⁹ nunc Filius Dei, qui non semper erat ?—Idem—dicitur¹⁰: quod nullus¹¹ est vel esse potest Filius Dei naturalis, nisi¹² qui absolute necessario semper erat. Sed querenti—Quid est Filius Dei, quod ante incarnacionem ipse non fuerat ?—dicitur quod— Natura humana sive humanitas; et illa est quiditas suppositi vel persone—.

And if sophistical arguments are multiplied to prove "whatever is the Word was eternally the Word", the solution is clear from what has been said about forms. For something, since it is some [124] [substantial form]{g:forma-substancialis} or [nature]{g:natura}, is now the Word which was not the Word in the first instant of time.[^2] For before "the Word was made flesh" (John 1:14) "in the fulness of time" (Gal 4:4), he was not man;[^3] and it is certain that to be a man is to be something: so when he was becoming man, he was becoming something which he had not been before. Nevertheless every [supposit]{g:suppositum}, [hypostasis]{g:ypostasis}, or [person]{g:persona} which is, or which was, the Word, was eternally the Word. But far be it to conclude from this, "therefore whatever".[^4] For the Word is something other, but not someone other, after the incarnation than he was before. And so to one asking, "Who is now the Son of God who was not always?", "The same", [the answer is]{g:dicitur}: [meaning] that no one is or can be the natural Son of God except one who absolutely necessarily always was.[^5] But to one asking, "What is the Son of God that he had not been before the incarnation?", the answer is: "Human nature, or humanity; and that is the [quiddity]{g:quiditas} of the supposit or person."

## ¶5 · 124.17–124.23

> [p. 124] Et patet solucio ad tales argucias—Omnis¹³ persona, que est Verbum, eternaliter erat Verbum: sed quicquid iam est¹⁴ Verbum est persona, que est Verbum: ergo, quicquid iam est Verbum,¹⁵ eternaliter erat Verbum. Omnes quidem deducciones tales sophistice sanantur¹⁶ per noticiam fallacie accidentis. Nam persone Verbi accidentalis est quiditas in genere, sicut substancie create accidentalis est qualitas.

And the solution is clear to such [quibbles]{g:argucie} as: "Every person which is the Word was eternally the Word: now whatever is now the Word is a person which is the Word: therefore whatever is now the Word was eternally the Word." Indeed all such deductions, [made] [sophistically]{g:sophistice}, are cured by knowledge of the [fallacy]{g:fallacia} of accident.[^6] For quiddity in a genus is accidental to the person of the Word, as quality is accidental to created substance.

## ¶6 · 124.24–124.29

> [p. 124] Et si 3°. sic arguatur—Hoc¹⁷ Verbum in principio erat Verbum, et hoc Verbum est hec humanitas; ergo hec humanitas in principio erat Verbum—patet quod idem est ac si sic argueretur—In principio fuit, quod hoc est Verbum; et hoc Verbum est ille homo; ergo tunc fuit, quod ipsum est homo; sic enim significat humanitas simpliciter abstractive.

And if, third, it is argued thus: "This Word in the beginning was the Word (John 1:1), and this Word is this humanity; therefore this humanity in the beginning was the Word", it is clear that it is the same as if it were argued thus: "In the beginning it was [so] that this is the Word; and this Word is that man; therefore it was then [so] that it is man"; for so "humanity" signifies, [without qualification]{g:simpliciter}, [abstractively]{g:abstractive}.[^7]

## ¶7 · 124.30–125.23

> [p. 124] Et si 4°. sophista replicet per conversionem ab exclusivis aut exceptivis—Tantum hoc nunc est Verbum—demonstrando personam Verbi—et hoc eternaliter erat Verbum; [p. 125] ergo nichil iam est Verbum, nisi quod eternaliter erat Verbum—constat quod antecedens est verum et consequens falsum. Licet enim tantum hoc sit Verbum, non tamen tantum ista humanitas est Verbum: cum deitas,¹ que est aliud quam illa humanitas, est Verbum et² eternaliter erat Verbum. Et patet quod, licet tantum homo et tantum Deus sit Verbum, non tantum humanitas, nec nichil preter deitatem est Verbum. Nam aliud quam deitas et aliud quam humanitas est Verbum; sed nec alius nec aliud quam Deus vel homo est Verbum. Si enim tantum deitas esset Iesus, tunc Pater in divinis non esset aliqualiter³ maior Iesu. Et constat quomodo exclusive ac exceptive sint suis universalibus adaptande; ut sic dicto—omnis Iesus est humanitas—⁴ licet correspondenter dare istam⁴ exclusivam— tantum persona, que est humanitas, est Iesus—et proporcionaliter exceptivam et non conformissimam⁵ sophisticacionem contingit habere de quolibet alio homine, qui per se est homo, sed adventicie natura corporea.⁶ Unde quilibet nostrum in finali resurreccione erit aliud quam erat⁷ in morte, quia alia natura; cum in resurreccione erit tam natura corporea quam natura incorporea; in morte autem dumtaxat natura incorporea. Sed non erit alius: ymo solummodo idem ipse, cum non potest esse nisi eadem persona.

And if, fourth, the [sophist]{g:sophista} [rejoins]{g:replicatur} by [conversion]{g:conversio} from [exclusives]{g:exclusiva} or exceptives, "Only this is now the Word" (pointing to the person of the Word) "and this was eternally the Word; [125] therefore nothing now is the Word except what was eternally the Word",[^8] it is evident that the [antecedent]{g:antecedens} is true and the [consequent]{g:consequens} false. For although only this is the Word, yet not only this humanity is the Word: since [the Godhead]{g:deitas}, which is something other than that humanity, is the Word and was eternally the Word. And it is clear that, although only man and only God is the Word, not only the humanity is the Word, nor is nothing besides the Godhead the Word.[^9] For something other than the Godhead and something other than the humanity is the Word; but neither someone other nor something other than God or man is the Word. For if only the Godhead were Jesus, then the Father, in divine things, would not in any way be greater than Jesus (John 14:28). And it is evident how exclusives and exceptives are to be fitted to their universals; so that, this being said, "every Jesus is a humanity", it is permitted correspondingly to give this exclusive, "only a person which is a humanity is Jesus", and proportionally the [exceptive]{g:exceptiva}; and a not wholly conformable [sophistry]{g:sophisticacio} may be had about any other man, who is man [of himself]{g:per-se}, but adventitiously by bodily nature.[^10] Hence each of us in the final resurrection will be something other than he was in death, because another nature; since in the resurrection he will be both bodily nature and incorporeal nature, but in death only incorporeal nature.[^11] But he will not be someone other: rather, solely the same self, since he cannot be other than the same person.

## ¶8 · 125.24–126.6

> [p. 125] Et illam methaphisicam intexuit⁸ ille magnus philosophus sanctus Iob 19 ca°. quando dixit; ‘in carne mea videbo Deum salvatorem meum, ego ipse, inquit, et non alius.’ Unde Gregorius 18 Moralium ca°. 32. ‘Quamvis Christus sit aliud ex Patre aliud ex matre,⁹ non tamen est alius ex Patre alius ex virgine: sed ipse eternus ex Patre, temporaliter ex matre⁹; ipse,¹⁰ qui fecit, factus¹⁰; ipse auctor operis, ipse opus auctoris; manens unus ex utraque et in¹¹ utraque natura¹²; nec naturarum¹¹ copulacione confusus, [p. 126] nec naturarum distinccione geminatus.’¹ Ecce aliud sancti doctoris testimonium de² sentencia supradicta.² Et sic intelligendus est Augustinus in Dialogo ad Felicianum circa medium et alibi crebrius, quando dicit: ‘quamvis enim aliud corpus, aliud animus, unus tamen atque idem homo et corpus dicitur et animus.’

And that great philosopher, holy Job, wove that metaphysics in, in chapter 19, when he said: "in my flesh I shall see God my saviour: I myself," he says, "and not another."[^12] Hence Gregory, *Morals* 18, chapter 32: "Although Christ is something from the Father, something other from the mother, yet he is not someone from the Father, someone other from the virgin: but he himself is eternal from the Father, temporally from the mother; he himself who made, [was] made; he himself the author of the work, he himself the work of the author; remaining one from both and in both natures; neither confused by the coupling of the natures, [126] nor doubled by the distinction of the natures."[^13] Behold another testimony of a holy doctor on the [teaching]{g:sentencia} so often stated.[^14] And so Augustine is to be understood in the *Dialogue to Felicianus*, about the middle, and more often elsewhere, when he says: "For although the body is one thing, the soul another, yet one and the same man is called both body and soul."[^15]

## ¶9 · 126.7–127.4

> [p. 126] 2°. principaliter arguitur contra ydemptitatem specificam Christi cum aliis ex hoc quod³ ipse solum per accidens et quilibet alius per se est homo. Sed patet ex dictis quod non sequitur. Cum illud accidens, quo Christus est homo formaliter, quod Apostolus vocat humanitacionem⁴ et habitum,⁴ sit eiusdem racionis cum formis, quibus confratres Christi sunt homines formaliter, et non est accidens simpliciter. Sed quia Christus est gigas gemine substancie, scilicet, deitas et humanitas, quarum inferior contingenter inest superiori; ideo dicitur⁵ humanitas ad sensum expositum accidens Verbo Dei. Et si queritur⁶ utrum Christus⁷ sit per se homo, videtur michi quod sic. Nam cum ‘Christus’ dicit duas formas, scilicet deitatem et humanitatem,⁸ ydemptificatas⁹ in eadem persona, et potest supponere simpliciter pro utraque: patet quod, intelligendo eum secundum humanitatem, ipse eque per se est homo sicut aliquod¹⁰ individuum sui generis; cum eque vere causatur¹¹ ex partibus qualitativis et quiditativis consimilis racionis, et eque dependet a corpore et anima a specie et genere cum suis differenciis, sicut Petrus. Nec est color, si Christus sit per accidens homo quia secundum deitatem, quod non sit per se homo: quia¹² extra deitatem est humanitas Christi, que est per se homo,¹² cum de sua essencia sit universalis¹³ humanitas. Et ita sicut duplici nativitate nascitur Verbum Dei, sic videtur michi quod duplici [p. 127] racionalitate¹ satis equivoce est ipsum² racionale. Nam² quoad creatam substanciam causatur³ ab humanitate et racionalitate¹ communi sicut alie creature: et hinc competit sibi primus modus predicandi per se ex 2°. posteriorum.

Second, [as a main head]{g:principaliter}, it is argued against the identity of species of Christ with others from this, that he is man only [by accident]{g:per-accidens}, and anyone else of himself. But it is clear from what has been said that it does not follow; since that accident by which Christ is formally man, which the Apostle calls "[humanation]{g:humanitacio}" and "habit", is [of the same kind]{g:eiusdem-racionis} as the forms by which Christ's brothers are formally men, and is not an accident [without qualification]{g:simpliciter}.[^16] But because Christ is a giant of twofold substance, namely Godhead and humanity, of which the lower is contingently in the higher,[^17] therefore the humanity, in the sense set out, is said to be an accident to the Word of God. And if it is asked whether Christ is of himself man, it seems to me that he is. For since "Christ" [signifies]{g:dicere} two forms, namely the Godhead and the humanity, identified in the same person, and can stand without qualification for either:[^18] it is clear that, understanding him [as to the humanity]{g:secundum-humanitatem}, he is as much of himself man as any [individual]{g:individuum} of his kind; since he is as truly caused from [qualitative]{g:pars-qualitativa} and [quidditative]{g:quiditativus} parts of like kind, and depends as much on body and soul, on species and genus with their [differences]{g:differencia}, as Peter. Nor is there [colour]{g:color}, if Christ is man by accident because [he is so] as to the Godhead, [for saying] that he is not man of himself:[^19] for apart from the Godhead there is the humanity of Christ, which is of itself man, since [universal]{g:universale} humanity is of its essence. And so, as the Word of God is born by a twofold birth, so it seems to me that by a twofold [127] rationality he is, quite [equivocally]{g:equivoce}, rational. For as to the created substance he is caused by humanity and common rationality, like other [creatures]{g:creatura}: and hence the first mode of predicating of itself, from the *Posterior Analytics* 2, belongs to him.[^20]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Falsum assumitur*: the logician's *assumere*, "take as a premiss" (the minor, *humanitas Christi est sibi accidentalis*, 123.5–6), not the assumption of the nature at 123.15.
[^2]: **C.** *Aliquid, quia aliqua forma substancialis vel natura*: *forma* and *natura* are nominative, "since it is some substantial form or nature", the humanity. Read loosely as a cause ("by some form"), the sentence would say the Word became the Word by a form.
[^3]: **S.** *In plenitudine temporis* is Wyclif's form; Gal 4:4 Vulgate *ubi venit plenitudo temporis*, DR "when the fulness of the time was come". The two phrases are joined as one quotation in the print's punctuation.
[^4]: **T.** *Ergo quicquid* is the print's, with O. A B read *quicquam* without *ergo* ("far be it to conclude anything from this"), C *quod quitquam*. The print is kept: the point is that the universal *quicquid* does not follow from *omne suppositum*.
[^5]: **T/C.** *Idem* (124.11) is the print's, with O; A B C read *eidem* ("to him the answer is"), which makes the sentence run parallel to *Sed querenti ... dicitur quod* below. The print is kept. "The same" answers the question: the Son of God now is none other than the one who always was.
[^6]: **C.** *Sophistice* is taken with *deducciones tales*, "made sophistically"; with *sanantur* it would make the cure sophistical. The fallacy of accident: what holds of the person does not hold of whatever is now the person by an accidental form.
[^7]: **C.** *Simpliciter abstractive*: the abstract *humanitas*, taken without qualification, signifies the fact "that it is man"; so "this Word is this humanity" amounts to "this Word is that man", and the conclusion only says that what is now man was the Word in the beginning (John 1:1).
[^8]: **C.** *Per conversionem ab exclusivis aut exceptivis*: the logicians' rule that an exclusive ("only A is B") converts to a universal with the terms exchanged ("every B is A"), and an exceptive ("nothing except A is B") likewise.
[^9]: **C.** *Nec nichil preter deitatem est Verbum*: a double negative, kept: it is false that nothing besides the Godhead is the Word, since the humanity is the Word too.
[^10]: **T.** *Non conformissimam* (125.16) is the print's; A B C omit *non* ("and a most conformable sophistry"); O's abbreviation (*ɔformissia˥*) leaves its reading uncertain. The print is kept: in other men the case is like Christ's but not the same, since their bodily nature is adventitious to a man who is man of himself.
[^11]: **C.** *Alia natura ... natura corporea ... natura incorporea* are taken as nominatives (he will be another nature); they could be ablatives ("by another nature", "by bodily nature"), as *adventicie natura corporea* is just above.
[^12]: **S.** Job 19:26–27, Vulgate *in carne mea videbo Deum meum: quem visurus sum ego ipse ... et non alius*; DR "in my flesh I shall see my God. Whom I myself shall see ... and not another". Wyclif's *Deum salvatorem meum* is not the Vulgate's.
[^13]: **S/T.** Gregory, *Moralia* XVIII (Wyclif's chapter 32) (LEDGER I08a-1). *Ipse, qui fecit, factus* (125.30) is the print's, with O; A B C read *ipse qui fecit ipse qui factus est*. If Gregory's text reads with A B C, the source is cause to follow them (same row).
[^14]: **T.** *Sepe dicta*, with A B C O, against the print's *supradicta*: A B C read *de s͡cia sepe dicta*, O *de sm͡a sepe dicta*. The noun is Harris's expansion, *sentencia*, kept.
[^15]: **S.** Vigilius of Thapsus, *Contra Felicianum Arianum*, among Augustine's works (LEDGER I08a-2).
[^16]: **T.** *Humanitacionem et habitum* (126.12) is the print's, with O; A B C read *humanitatem et hominem*. The Apostle's word is *habitus* (Phil 2:7, "in habit found as a man"), which supports the print.
[^17]: **S.** *Gigas gemine substancie*: Ambrose's hymn *Intende qui regis Israel* (*Veni redemptor gentium*), *geminae gigas substantiae*, on Ps 18:6, "hath rejoiced as a giant to run the way" (LEDGER I08a-3).
[^18]: **T/C.** *Ydemptificatas* is Harris's emendation; every manuscript reads *ydemptitas*, and A B C add *que sunt* before it ("which are identity in the same person"). Neither reading construes well, so the print is kept. *Christus* (126.18) is the print's, with O; A B C read *Iesus*: the argument turns on the name *Christus* (126.19). *Supponere simpliciter* may be the logicians' simple supposition; rendered by the glossary's "without qualification".
[^19]: **C.** *Color*: a plausible ground. The construal is: if Christ is man by accident when taken as to the Godhead, that gives no ground for denying that he is man of himself.
[^20]: **S.** The first mode of *per se* predication (the predicate in the definition of the subject), Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics* I.4, 73a34–37; Wyclif's "2°" is not explained (LEDGER I08a-4).
