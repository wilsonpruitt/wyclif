# sed/autem extract 04: chunks 10-a–13-e

### 10-a ¶1 · 204.28–206.10  (1 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 204] Istis premissis descendendum est specialiter ad privilegium Westmonasterii super quo versatur contencio. Supponatur itaque in primis (ut patet ex vera copia ministrata) quod dictum Westmonasterium habeat [p. 205] privilegium concessum ab Edgaro et sancto Edwardo confessore, concessum (inquam) sibi et ecclesie sub hac forma: Cognoscat, inquit Edgarus, magnitudo seu utilitas vestra quam discernimus et in perpetuum mansurum iubemus atque constituimus ut pro reverencia reliquiarum gloriosissimi apostoli Petri et quiete monachorum ibidem Deo famulancium, honor et laus eidem ecclesie habeatur, ut quisquis fugitivorum pro quolibet scelere ad prefatam basilicam sancti Petri fugiens precinctum eius intraverit sive pedes vel pedester, sive eques vel equester, sive de curia regali sive de civitate seu villa, seu cuiuscunque condicionis sit, quocunque delicto facinoris contra nos vel succesores reges Anglie vel contra alium quemlibet fidelem sancte ecclesie foris factus sit, relaxetur et liberetur et vitam atque membra absque ulla contradiccione optineat. Preterea interminamur divina auctoritate et nostra, ut neque nos neque successores nostri neque ullus episcopus nec quicunque alius de iudiciaria potestate in ipsam sanctam basilicam vel in manentes in ipsa vel in homines qui cum sua substancia vel rebus ad ipsam tradere vel devovere se voluerint, nisi per voluntatem abbatis et suorum monachorum, ullam unquam habeant potestatem, **sed** sit hec mater ecclesia libera et absoluta ab omni invasione vel inquietudine omnium hominum cuiuscunque ordinis vel potestatis. Obsecramus eciam omnes successores nostros reges et [p. 206] principes per sanctam et individuam trinitatem et per adventum iusti iudicis, ut quoniam ex munificencia antecessorum nostrorum ipse locus videtur esse dicatus, nullus episcoporum vel abbatum aut eorum ordinatores vel quelibet persona possit quoquo ordine de loco ipso aliquid auferre aut aliquam potestatem sibi in ipso monasterio usurpare vel aliquid quasi per commutacionis titulum absque voluntate ipsius congregacionis vel nostrum permissum minuere presumat.

ENGLISH: These things premised, we must come down specifically to the [privilege]{g:privilegium} of Westminster, over which the contention turns.[^1] Let it be supposed, then, in the first place (as is clear from a true copy supplied [to me]) that the said Westminster has a [205] privilege granted by Edgar and by Saint Edward the Confessor, granted (I say) to it and to the [church]{g:ecclesia} in this form:[^2] "Let your greatness or usefulness know," says Edgar, "what we decide, and command and establish to abide for ever: that, for reverence of the relics of the most glorious apostle Peter and for the quiet of the monks serving God there, honour and praise be paid to that church; so that whoever of the fugitives, for any crime whatever, fleeing to the aforesaid basilica of Saint Peter, has entered its precinct, whether a foot-soldier or on foot, whether a horseman or on horseback, whether from the royal court or from a city or township, or of whatever condition he be, by whatever offence of crime he has [committed forfeiture]{g:forisfacere} against us or the kings of England our successors, or against any other faithful man of holy church, let him be released and set free and keep life and limbs without any gainsaying.[^3] Moreover we forbid under threat, by divine authority and our own, that neither we nor our successors nor any bishop nor anyone else of judicial power should ever have any power over that holy basilica, or over those dwelling in it, or over the men who wish to deliver or devote themselves to it with their substance or goods, except by the will of the abbot and his monks; but let this mother church be free and quit of all invasion or disturbance by all men of whatever order or power. We also beseech all our successors, kings and [206] princes, by the holy and undivided Trinity and by the coming of the just judge, that, since that place is seen to have been dedicated by the munificence of our predecessors, none of the bishops or abbots or their ordainers, or any person whatever, may in any manner take anything away from that place, or usurp any power for himself in that monastery, or presume to diminish anything as if by [title]{g:titulus} of [exchange]{g:commutacio}, without the will of the congregation itself or our permission."[^4]

### 10-d ¶1 · 216.27–217.8  (1 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 216] Ideo restat iam quarto obicere contra illa. Videtur primo: Si tenor privilegiorum debet sic intelligi nude ad litteram, tunc posito quod exercitus inimicorum invadat Angliam, debet in illo loco habere refugium, sic quod post recreacionem posset animosius predari provinciam. Nam quelibet persona talis exercitus est [p. 217] aliquis homo de aliquo loco veniens, pedester vel equester, intrans illum locum ex causa aliqua, **sed** omnis talis iuxta tenorem verborum privilegii habebit in loco illo immunitatem omnimodam, ergo quelibet persona talis exercitus virtute privilegii, quam diu voluerit, quiescet ibi pacifice; si enim in aliquo casu capit instanciam, oportet nuda verba abicere et sapere sanam sentenciam.

ENGLISH: Therefore it remains now, fourthly, to object against those [arguments]. It seems, first: if the tenor of the [privileges]{g:privilegium} must be understood thus, barely, according to the letter, then, supposing that an army of enemies invades England, it must have refuge in that place, so that after refreshment it could plunder the province more boldly. For every person of such an army is [217] some man coming from some place, on foot or on horseback, entering that place for some cause; but every such man, according to the tenor of the words of the privilege, will have in that place immunity of every kind; therefore every person of such an army, by virtue of the privilege, will rest there in peace as long as he wishes. For if in any case it admits an [objection]{g:instancia}, the bare words must be cast aside and sound [teaching]{g:sentencia} understood.[^1]

### 10-d ¶2 · 217.9–218.13  (3 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 217] Hic dixit mihi quidam ingeniosus legista quod quelibet persona talis exercitus gaudebit hoc privilegio, cum non oportet propter tales casus inpossibiles limitare vel restringere privilegia regum. Illud **autem** capit calumpniam multiplicem aput quemlibet mentis compotem. Nam nec rex potuit concedere tale privilegium nec clerus debuit ipsum ut privilegium sibi admittere, cum non potest regno nostro vel parti eius esse privilegium fovere destructores regni et tocius ecclesie Anglicane, sicut non est privilegium alicui se ipsum deteriorando destruere. Ideo quicunque impetraverit vel defenderit hoc ut privilegium regno nostro, tamquam infidelis Deo et regno degenerat. Nec valet dicere quod talis comitiva non possit consurgere et regnum nostrum invadere, cum in tribus conquestibus scilicet Britonum, Saxonum et Normanorum hoc contigit, ymmo tales depopulatores tempore regis Edwardi secundi surrexerant et cardinales in comitatu Dunelmie spoliarunt, et in nobili regno Francie, in Lumbardia et in aliis regnis potentibus tales comitive depopulantes nostris temporibus surrexerunt: ymmo diebus istis regna nunquam vel raro sunt sine talibus vel corporaliter vel spiritualiter confederatis ad destruccionem eorum, cum talis corruptela sub pallio privilegii posset per se regnum perdere. [p. 218] Item, rex non potest quicquam rite concedere quod vergeret ad enervacionem regni sui vel iniuriam sui populi, **sed** utrumque contingeret datis verbis recitati privilegii, secundum nudum verbalem conceptum. Ergo non subiacet potestati regis privilegium tale concedere. Maior patet ex dictis de privilegio, cum nichil sit privilegium, nisi quod directe vergit ad utilitatem sancte matris ecclesie; destruccio **autem** regni nostri et iniuria facta suo populo non potest directe vergere ad honorem Dei et utilitatem sancte matris ecclesie, ergo non subiacet potestati regis alicuius privilegium tale concedere; Deus enim non potest in contradiccionem.

ENGLISH: Here a certain ingenious lawyer told me that every person of such an army will enjoy this privilege, since there is no need, on account of such impossible cases, to limit or restrict the privileges of kings.[^2] But that incurs manifold challenge with anyone in his right mind. For neither could the king grant such a privilege, nor ought the [clergy]{g:clerus} to admit it as a privilege for themselves, since it cannot be a privilege for our [kingdom]{g:regnum}, or for a part of it, to shelter the destroyers of the kingdom and of the whole English [church]{g:ecclesia}, just as it is no privilege for anyone to destroy himself by making himself worse. Therefore whoever has [obtained]{g:impetrare} or defended this as a privilege for our kingdom degenerates, as one [faithless]{g:infidelis} to God and to the kingdom. Nor does it avail to say that such a company could not rise up and invade our kingdom, since this happened in three conquests, namely of the Britons, the Saxons and the Normans; indeed, such depopulators had risen in the time of King Edward the Second and despoiled cardinals in the county of Durham; and in the noble kingdom of France, in Lombardy and in other powerful kingdoms such depopulating companies have risen in our times: indeed, in these days kingdoms are never or rarely without such men, confederated bodily or spiritually for their destruction, since such corruption under the cloak of privilege could of itself destroy a kingdom.[^3] [218] Again, the king cannot rightly grant anything that would tend to the enervation of his kingdom or to the injury of his people; but both would happen, given the words of the privilege recited, according to their bare verbal conception. Therefore it does not lie within the king's power to grant such a privilege. The major is clear from what has been said of privilege, since nothing is a privilege except what tends directly to the benefit of holy mother church; now the destruction of our kingdom and injury done to its people cannot tend directly to the honour of God and the benefit of holy mother church; therefore it does not lie within the power of any king to grant such a privilege; for God cannot [do] what is contradictory.[^4]

### 10-d ¶3 · 218.14–219.22  (5 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 218] Et confirmacio est quod officium regis terreni in quantum huiusmodi) est ecclesiam sanctam defendere, inimicos eius rigide cohercere et cunctis **sed** specialiter suis legiis iusticiam observare (ut patet libro III° cap°. Minor **autem** sic ostenditur: Nichil plus enervaret regnum quam tutela facinorosorum sine correpcione legitima. Unde Ecclesiastici X°, 8, scribitur: Regnum de gente in gentem transfertur propter iniusticias, iniurias, contumelias et diversos dolos; **sed** nichil plus cederet ad fomentum istorum quattuor quam concedere cuilibet libertatem quod, quidquid facinoris perpetraverit, fugiendo ad tam patentem locum non erit ab aliquo castigatus; ergo hoc evidenter minaretur ruinam regno. Si enim hoc privilegium esset per totam Angliam concessum, quomodo staret regnum? Rex enim non haberet potestatem ad cohercendum civiliter homines legios suos, et tunc non foret univoce rex eorum vel populi qui sue non subiacet potestati. Nec est racio quare rex posset concedere libertatem tanto loco, [p. 219] quin per idem et patrie, et sic alius rex alii patrie, et sic quousque tota Anglia fuerit libertata. Si enim totalis exempcio extingueret totam regiam potestatem, particularis exempcio extingueret partem eius, cum de tanto non haberet rex potestatem loci vel persone incolentis ad castigandum ipsam, quantumcunque in regem vel regnum deliquerit, et sic non foret rex tocius Anglie, cum locus exemptus foret in regno, **sed** non de regno. Non enim haberet rex tantam potestatem ad subiugandum incolas talis loci sicut habet potestatem ad puniendum gentem Francie, cum tantum eximuntur a cohercione regia sicut incole Almanie vel omnino aliene provincie. Et quantum ad iniuriam populi, patet quod vendicatur libertas, ut quantumcunque sceleste quis iniuriatus fuerit proximo, homicidio, furto, mechia, prevaricando in omnem speciem mandatorum decalogi, fugiat ad illum locum et precluditur iniuriato iusticie complementum, cum nec tenetur per se vel procuratorem ibi vel alibi stare iuri, et tamen rex Anglie in coronacione sua iuratus est religione laudabili quod non negabit **sed** concedet iusticiam cuicunque.

ENGLISH: And the confirmation is that the office of an earthly king, as such, is to defend holy church, rigidly to [coerce]{g:cohercere} its enemies, and to keep justice for all, but especially for his lieges (as is clear in book 3, chapter [ ]).[^5] Now the minor is shown thus: nothing would more enervate a kingdom than the protection of criminals without lawful correction. Hence it is written, Ecclesiasticus 10:8: "A kingdom is translated from one people to another, because of injustices, wrongs, injuries and divers deceits"; but nothing would contribute more to the fostering of these four than to grant anyone the liberty that, whatever crime he has perpetrated, by fleeing to so open a place he will not be chastised by anyone; therefore this would evidently threaten ruin to the kingdom.[^6] For if this privilege were granted throughout all England, how would the kingdom stand? For the king would not have power to coerce his liege men by civil [process], and then he would not be univocally king of them, or of a people that is not subject to his power. Nor is there a reason why the king could grant liberty to so great a place [219] but that by the same token [he could grant it] also to a [homeland]{g:patria}, and so another king to another homeland, and so on until the whole of England were set at liberty. For if total [exemption]{g:exempcio} would extinguish the whole royal power, partial exemption would extinguish a part of it, since to that extent the king would not have power over the place, or over the person dwelling in it, to chastise it, however much it had offended against king or kingdom; and so he would not be king of all England, since the exempt place would be in the kingdom but not of the kingdom.[^7] For the king would not have as much power to subjugate the inhabitants of such a place as he has power to punish the people of France, since they are exempted from royal coercion as much as the inhabitants of Germany or of a wholly foreign province. And as regards the injury of the people, it is clear that a liberty is claimed such that, however wickedly anyone has injured his neighbour, by homicide, theft, adultery, [transgressing]{g:prevaricari} against every kind of the commandments of the decalogue, he may flee to that place, and the fulfilment of justice is shut off from the injured man, since [the fugitive] is not bound, in person or by [proctor]{g:procurator}, there or elsewhere, to stand to [law]{g:ius}; and yet the king of England at his coronation is sworn, by a laudable religious bond, that he will not deny but will grant justice to anyone whatever.[^8]

### 10-d ¶5 · 219.29–220.25  (1 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 219] Unde quesivi ab adversariis duos casus: Primo, si predans, furans vel mutuans quantumlibet magnum thesaurum ab alia ecclesia et illuc fugiens cum thesauro habebit ad sensum expositum omnimodam libertatem. Et dictum est mihi quod sic; tum quia verba privilegii a quibus non licet recedere generaliter [p. 220] illud sonant, tum eciam quia aliter foret nimis suspectum, negando laycis complementum iusticie et faciendo clero iusticiam. **Sed** tunc sequitur, primo quod rex qui potuit concedere ut sceleratissimi homines in perpetuum iniuste detineant reliquias et quecunque bona immunis ecclesie posset concedere ut iusti auferant temporalia ab ecclesia delinquente. Nam per se notum est quod ille qui potest concedere magis elongatum a communi iure potest concedere consonancius iuri Dei. Et iterum notum est quod maius peccatum foret iniuste, pertinaciter et obstinate detinere rem alienam, quam foret iniusta ablacio, quia pro primo est homo excommunicandus tamquam hereticus, et pro secundo non, nisi affuerit contumax obstinacia, ut noverant legiste. Ideo non dubium, si rex potest concedere privilegium ut quilibet regni sui habeat libertatem ad perpetuo detinendum iniuste ablata de sancta matre ecclesia, multo magis potest concedere licenciam, ut iusti auferant elemosinas suas quibus fraudantes regnum et sanctam matrem ecclesiam abutuntur. Unde inconsonum factum est et sibi ipsi contrarium tam ferventer defendere illud tamquam privilegium catholicum et solicite laborare, quando raciones deficiunt, ad dampnandum aliud tamquam hereticum.

ENGLISH: Hence I put two cases to [my] adversaries. First, whether one who plunders, steals or borrows a treasure however great from another church, and flees thither with the treasure, will have, on the sense expounded, liberty of every kind. And I was told that he would: both because the words of the privilege, from which it is not lawful to depart, [220] say that generally, and also because otherwise it would be too suspect, denying the laity the fulfilment of justice and doing justice to the clergy.[^10] But then it [follows]{g:sequitur}, first, that the king, who could grant that the most wicked men should unjustly keep back in perpetuity the relics and any goods whatever of an immune church, could grant that the just should take away [temporal goods]{g:temporalia} from a delinquent church. For it is known of itself that he who can grant what is further removed from common law can grant what is more consonant with the law of God. And again it is known that it would be a greater sin unjustly, pertinaciously and obstinately to keep back another's property than an unjust taking away would be; because for the first a man is to be excommunicated as a heretic, and for the second not, unless contumacious obstinacy is present, as the lawyers knew.[^11] Therefore there is no doubt: if the king can grant a privilege that anyone in his kingdom may have liberty perpetually to keep back what has been unjustly taken from holy mother church, much more can he grant licence that the just may take away their [alms]{g:elemosina}, which those who defraud the kingdom and holy mother church abuse. Hence it has been an inconsistent thing, and contrary to itself, to defend that so fervently as a catholic privilege, and anxiously to labour, when reasons fail, to condemn the other as heretical.[^12]

### 10-e ¶1 · 220.26–221.16  (4 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 220] Ego **autem** dico consonancius pro privilegiis sancte matris ecclesie quod rex non potest cuiquam concedere libertatem ad violente auferendum et multo evidencius ad iniuste detinendum aliquod bonum de quocunque membro ecclesie, nisi prius peccaverit hoc merendo. Nec est hic sophisma: Rex concedit malefico libertatem, ut non puniatur et ut maneat in perpetuum inaccusatus de crimine, **sed** non concedit sibi licenciam ad peccandum vel cuiquam iniuriandum; nam licenciare [p. 221] ad formaliter atque notorie antecedens ad quamcunque iniuriam est licenciare ad ipsam iniuriam; ut si Deus dat mihi licenciam ad frangendum caput presbyteri, et hoc non potest fieri sine facinore, tunc Deus dat mihi licenciam ad facinus perpetrandum. Correspondenter, notato scelerato fugitivo quiescente in dicto monasterio cum rapina pro suo perpetuo, queratur ab eo vel defendente hoc privilegium, a quo habet licenciam sic pausandi. Si dicatur quod non habet, tunc licitum est collacionem tam illicitam removere. Si dicatur quod a rege habet illam licenciam, sequitur cum veris quod rex licenciat ad antecedens formaliter ad peccatum, et longe coloracius dici poterit quod rex legitime licenciat ad auferendum temporalia, **sed** nullo modo ad faciendum iniuriam cuicunque, nam ablacio fieri potest legitime **sed** non talis detencio.

ENGLISH: But I say, more consonantly with the [privileges]{g:privilegium} of holy mother [church]{g:ecclesia}, that the king cannot grant anyone liberty violently to take away, and much more evidently [none] unjustly to keep back, any good from any [member]{g:membrum} of the church whatever, unless [that member] has first sinned, deserving this.[^1] Nor does the [sophism]{g:sophisma} [hold] here: "The king grants the evildoer liberty not to be punished and to remain for ever unaccused of the crime, but he does not grant him licence to sin or to injure anyone"; for to license [221] to what is formally and notoriously antecedent to any injury whatever is to license to the injury itself: as, if God gives me licence to break the [head]{g:caput} of a [presbyter]{g:presbiter}, and this cannot be done without crime, then God gives me licence to perpetrate a crime.[^2] Correspondingly, when a wicked fugitive is noted resting in the said monastery with his plunder as his own for ever, let it be asked of him, or of the one defending this privilege, from whom he has licence so to rest. If it is said that he has none, then it is lawful to remove so unlawful a bestowal. If it is said that he has that licence from the king, it [follows]{g:sequitur}, together with truths, that the king licenses to what is formally antecedent to sin; and it could be said with far more [colour]{g:color} that the king lawfully licenses the taking away of [temporal goods]{g:temporalia}, but in no way the doing of injury to anyone; for a taking away can be done lawfully, but not such a keeping back.[^3]

### 10-e ¶2 · 221.17–224.13  (11 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 221] Secundo quesivi, si fugitivus huiusmodi possit excommunicari, dum fuerit in loco illo pro facinore exterius perpetrato, vel si excomunicatus extra locum et fugiens ad ipsum ne virtute brevis regii capiatur ad carcerem, habebit in dicto monasterio libertatem? Et dictum est mihi quod non, quia leges humane non derogant legibus et iuri ecclesie, et specialiter ut inobedientissimis excommunicandis apponatur anime medicina; aliter enim liceret excludere et privilegiare spiritualiter infirmum, ut sordescat in crimine nec accipiat correccionem ab homine; quod cum sit contra legem correpcionis fraterne, Matthei XVIII°, 15, Si peccaverit in te frater tuus etc, manifestum videtur quod talis defensio non habet privilegium **sed** pravilegium Antichristi. Ymmo cum talis sit plene et non minus excommunicatus a Deo, non obstante quod sentencia non sit lata ab homine, sequitur [p. 222] iuxta fidem scripture et leges ecclesie quod non est communicandum cum tali, nisi forte ut convertatur, ne inficiat gregem Christi. Ex quotlibet talibus evidenciis dicitur quod licet talem fugitivum excommunicare et post quadraginta dies excommunicacionis carceri mancipare. **Sed** dato isto sequitur, cum licet excommunicare pro quocunque facinore, quod fugitivus talis facinorosus sit statim excommunicandus et consequenter post quadraginta dies incarcerandus, et cum non habebit post excommunicacionem maiorem favorem quam ethnicus et publicanus, videtur quod sit de monasterio extrahendus et in carcere publico cum aliis excommunicatis tamquam alienus a filiis ecclesie detrudendus. Unde cum in dubiis via securior est tenenda, et vel est, dubium vel probabile quod fovens taliter criminosum erit particeps criminis ex consensu vel culpabilis, negando coreccionis suffragium, quod foret verum privilegium, oppositum hinc privilegio, videtur, cum cursus legis communis sit via securior, quod illa sit pocius sustinenda. Quomodo rogo tenderet ad profectum persone pro querenda beatitudine indifferenter omnes facinorosos, diabolicos et inimicos ecclesie in domo sua protegere et medicinam correpcionis et censuras ecclesie quibus sanarentur ab eis precludere? Revera nec hoc nec participacio rapine sonat religiosis ad commodum. Nam Hely non propter fomentum malicie filiorum **sed** propter remissionem correpcionis filiorum punitus fuit morte et privatus summo sacerdocio in sua posteritate, ut patet I° Regum, II° et IV° capitulis, et III° Regum II°. Hoc ergo posset sonare in privilegium dicti loci quod fugitivos inpotentes, de quibus foret spes conversionis ad frugem melioris vite, in vite necessariis et pecuniis debitis de regis elemosinis misericorditer relevarent et predas latronum [p. 223] illatas fideliter restituerent. Hoc enim esset elemosina et sic privilegium cedens undique ad utilitatem parcium, ad honorem Dei et profectum ecclesie (ut sepe superius inculcavi). Tercio principaliter arguitur ex hoc quod omne bonum utile est fundabile in scriptura iuxta conclusionem secundam capituli VIIIⁱ, **sed** non est fundabile in scriptura nec in lege ecclesie quod locum vel personam sic generaliter privilegiare liceat. Ergo non est bonum utile ecclesie et sic non privilegium alicui sic facere. Minor patet ex hoc quod in veteri testamento cautum est de civitatibus refugii, ut dictum est capitulo VII°; nullibi **autem** signatum est refugium pro sceleratis, **sed** limitatum est ut pro occisione casuali preter propositum habeat occisor refugium, ut patet Levitici XXV°, Deuteronomii XIX° et Josue XX°. In novo **autem** testamento non signatur tale refugium, **sed** precipitur Matthei XVIII°, 17, quod talis sceleratus sit sicut ethnicus et publicanus. Unde ergo nobis autoritas ad hostes Dei ac sancte ecclesie taliter protegendum, specialiter cum legalis correccio sive punicio foret plus misericors atque iusta, tempore **autem** legis gracie obligamur ad misericordiam ampliorem? Quantum **autem** ad iura canonica, videtur mihi quod specificantur quinque casus in quibus malefici a defensione ecclesie excluduntur; tres **autem** casus exprimuntur III° Decretalium, De Immunitate Ecclesiarum, capitulo Inter alia, sub hac forma: Hoc verum est, scilicet quod fugientes ad ecclesiam habebunt [p. 224] immunitatem nisi publicus latro fuerit vel nocturnus populator agrorum qui, dum itinera frequentata vel publicas stratas obsidet aggressionis insidiis, ab ecclesia extrahi potest, inpunitate non prestita secundum canonicas sancciones. Si vero servus fuerit qui confugerit ad ecclesiam, postquam de inpunitate sua dominus eius clericis iuramentum prestiterit, ad servicium domini sui redire compellatur eciam invitus, alioquin a domino poterit occupari. Ecce tres casus in quibus licet fugitivum invitum extrahere, et libro V°, De Homicidio, ponitur pro lege illud Exodi XXI°, 14, Si quis per industriam occiderit, ab altari meo evellas eum, ut moriatur.

ENGLISH: Secondly I asked whether such a fugitive can be excommunicated while he is in that place, for a crime perpetrated outside; or whether one excommunicated outside the place, and fleeing to it so as not to be taken to prison by virtue of the king's writ, will have liberty in the said monastery. And I was told no; because human laws do not derogate from the laws and right of the church, and especially [not from the law] that the medicine of the soul be applied to the most disobedient, who are to be excommunicated; for otherwise it would be lawful to shut off and privilege the spiritually sick man, so that he grows filthy in crime and receives no correction from man.[^4] And since that is against the law of fraternal correction, Matthew 18:15, "If thy brother shall offend against thee", etc., it seems manifest that such a defence has not a privilege but a "pravilege" of Antichrist.[^5] Indeed, since such a man is fully, and no less, excommunicated by God, notwithstanding that sentence has not been passed by man, it follows, [222] according to the faith of scripture and the laws of the church, that one is not to communicate with such a man, unless perhaps so that he may be converted, lest he infect the flock of Christ.[^6] From any number of such [evidences]{g:evidencia} the answer is that it is lawful to excommunicate such a fugitive and, after forty days of excommunication, to commit him to prison. But, granted this, it follows, since it is lawful to excommunicate for any crime whatever, that such a criminal fugitive is to be excommunicated at once and consequently, after forty days, imprisoned; and since after excommunication he will have no greater favour than "a heathen and a publican", it seems that he is to be dragged out of the monastery and thrust into the public prison with other excommunicates, as a stranger to the sons of the church. Hence, since in doubtful matters the safer way is to be held, and it is either doubtful or probable that one who shelters a criminal in this way will be a partaker in the crime by consent, or guilty by denying the [aid]{g:suffragium} of correction (which would be the true privilege, opposed on this side to the [pretended] privilege), it seems, since the course of the common law is the safer way, that it is rather to be upheld.[^7] How, I ask, would it tend to the profit of a person [vowed] to seeking blessedness to protect in his house, without distinction, all criminals, devilish men and enemies of the church, and to shut off from them the medicine of correction and the [censures]{g:censura} of the church by which they might be healed? Truly, neither this nor a share in plunder tends to the advantage of the [religious]{g:religiosi}. For Eli was punished with death, and deprived of the high priesthood in his posterity, not for fostering the malice of his sons but for remitting the correction of his sons, as is clear from 1 Samuel, chapters 2 and 4, and 1 Kings 2.[^8] This, then, could tend to the privilege of the said place: that out of the king's [alms]{g:elemosina} they should mercifully relieve impotent fugitives, of whom there was hope of conversion to the fruit of a better life, in the necessaries of life and in money owed, and faithfully restore the plunder of thieves [223] brought in. For this would be alms, and so a privilege turning on all sides to the benefit of the parties, to the honour of God and the profit of the church (as I have often inculcated above).[^9] Thirdly, as a principal [argument], it is argued from this: that every useful good is groundable in scripture, according to the second [conclusion]{g:conclusio} of chapter 8; but it is not groundable in scripture, nor in the law of the church, that it is lawful to privilege a place or a person so generally; therefore it is not a useful good of the church, and so it is no privilege for anyone to do so. The minor is clear from this: that in the old testament provision was made concerning cities of refuge, as was said in chapter 7; but nowhere was a refuge designated for the wicked; rather it was [assigned]{g:limitare} that for accidental killing, beyond intention, the killer should have refuge, as is clear from Leviticus 25, Deuteronomy 19 and Joshua 20.[^10] And in the new testament no such refuge is designated; but it is commanded, Matthew 18:17, that such a wicked man be "as the heathen and publican". Whence, then, have we authority to protect thus the enemies of God and of holy church, especially since legal correction or punishment would be more merciful and just, and in the time of the [law of grace]{g:lex gracie} we are bound to a larger mercy?[^11] And as regards the canon laws, it seems to me that five cases are specified in which evildoers are excluded from the defence of the church; and three cases are expressed in the third [book] of the Decretals, *De immunitate ecclesiarum*, chapter *Inter alia*, in this form: "This is true", namely that those fleeing to a church will have [224] immunity, "unless he be a public thief or a nocturnal waster of fields who, while he besets frequented routes or public highways with ambushes of assault, can be drawn out of the church, impunity not being afforded, according to the canonical sanctions. But if it be a slave who has fled to the church, after his [lord]{g:dominus} has given the clerics an oath concerning his impunity, let him be compelled to return to his lord's service even unwilling; otherwise he may be seized by his lord."[^12] Behold three cases in which it is lawful to drag out a fugitive against his will; and in book 5, *De homicidio*, that [text] of Exodus 21:14 is laid down for law: "If a man kill on set purpose, thou shalt take him away from my altar, that he may die."[^13]

Third, if not even so does the light of truth appear, put your hand to the catholic ecclesiastical histories gathered by the holy doctors. Fourth, if the difficulty that is enquired into does not come clear even by them, call keenly to mind the examples of the saints; for the examples of the saints' life ought to be to us a mirror of how to live. [229] Fifth, if, when all these have been examined, the nature of the question does not grow clear, let it be decided by decree of the elders of the province gathered for this, with the help of God, who cannot fail his own.[^4]
Third, I suppose this. Since the said privilege is not founded in canon or civil law, but specially in the laws of our kingdom and in the charters of that church recited above, note should be taken of the form, and of the end for the sake of which these privileges were granted (for the end, as the second [book] of the *Physics* teaches, measures all the means to it); now in the privileges of King Edgar a threefold end is expressed: namely, reverence for God in the relics of his apostle; the quiet of the monks living there; and the honour of the holiness of that peculiar basilica, in which there live special [bedesmen]{g:oratores} for the king and kingdom, who are maintained specially for this out of their alms.[^5] Hence in the bull of Nicholas III it is contained thus: "Let the monks there be subject to no person at all except the king."[^6] From which it seems that the inhabitants of that place are specially subject to their king, not only because [they are] the king's liege men, nor only because [they are] his special [almsmen]{g:elemosinarius}, but by the pope's command; for according to blessed Gregory the multiplicity of benefits increases the obligation of the one benefited to his giver.[^7] From which it seems (since the monks of that place are so singularly subject to the king, and their guests or fugitives ought not to be more exempt or privileged than the monks from whom the privilege flows), it seems, [I say,] that all refugees, in so far as they are in that place, are subject to the king of England with a certain pre-eminence, from the fact that they receive from him there a singular benefit. Nor does it accord with the faith of the religion of Christ that a monk, if (to suppose what is possible) he should kill a man in the said place, would afterwards be freer and receive more of the fruit of the privilege than when he was guiltless before God and [230] man and more capable [of it]. Nor does it seem reasonable [to deny] that, supposing capacity in a monk or in any servant of that place, he will have the fruit of the privilege after an offence even rather than an outsider; for otherwise everyone resting there obstinately in crime would, as such, make himself [unfit]{g:indispositus} to have the aforesaid privilege.[^8]
These things premised, it seems to me (as elsewhere) that, although throughout all England remission of every kind ought to be granted, in so far as it tends to mercy and no further, yet for the place of Westminster such a general remission ought to be kept, by virtue of the singular privilege piously granted to that place by the kings.[^9] The first part is clear from this, that privileges, since (as such) they tend of themselves to good, are reasonably to be embraced; for it would be better that the whole church should have privilege, that is, law for its own advantage, than that any part of the church should fall short of true privilege, since it sins or is punished by so much as it falls short of the full privileges of the blessed. And it is clear that there is no privilege except in so far as it tends to alms or a work of mercy, since there is no privilege except in so far as it is mercifully given by God, turning to a man's profit. And the second part is clear from this, that the king's [grant]{g:concessio} must have a special efficacy, to be observed there more diligently; for otherwise it would be in vain. And from these it is clear that no privilege is contrary to the common [law]{g:ius}; for if it were, then it would be against justice, and consequently an unjust law and not a privilege. Hence the saying of some, who say that it would not be a privilege unless it were contrary to the common law, seems to me wholly foolish. For a lawful variation of rite for place and time is not contrary to truth, and consequently not to any law.[^10] Therefore those who privilege must take every care [231] not to withdraw justice from anyone or inflict injury on anyone. Hence in C. 25 q. 2, *Quod vero*, Gregory says: "Far be this from me, that I should break the statutes of our forebears with my priests in any church; for I do myself injury if I disturb the rights of my brothers." And without doubt the judgment would be the same of any wayfarer: that if by privileging he shuts anyone off from justice, he harms both himself and the one to be privileged; which it is not lawful even for God to do.[^11]
From these, second, it [follows]{g:sequitur} that the fugitives privileged in Westminster are not thereby set free so as not to satisfy [their creditor], or so as to remain there in perpetuity without the penalty of wholesome correction; but they have there some singular defence, which tends to their advantage and not to the harm or injury of anyone.[^12] The first part is clear, both because this does not follow from the tenor of the words of the privilege, and also because this would not be liberty or privilege but the most depraved corruption. For the command of the apostle, and so of God, is Romans 13:8: "Owe no man any thing, but to love one another." How, then, is it lawful for a creature to license anyone to remain in debt to his brother, not making satisfaction, even as regards God? Such heresies, therefore, if they should sprout even through the lords of our kingdom, the [prelates]{g:prelatus} are bound to destroy.[^13] Hence in C. 25 q. 1, *Omne quod*, it is written thus: "An unjust judgment and an unjust definition, ordained by judges through a king's fear or command, shall not be valid; for in no way shall anything stand that has been done against the evangelical or prophetic or apostolic [doctrine]{g:doctrina}, or their constitution, or [that] of the holy fathers." Hence it is said that the Roman [232] pontiffs ought to confirm this doctrine even to [the giving of] life and blood (as in the chapter immediately preceding).[^14] How, then, do these stand together: that it is lawful for no one to owe anything to another beyond the love of [charity]{g:caritas}, and yet a fugitive is licensed in the said place to owe perpetually both a bodily and a spiritual debt without paying back satisfaction? Truly God cannot [consent] to this usurped blasphemy, since he does not license a man to anything for which he damns [him].[^15] And the second part of the conclusion is clear from this, that some privilege must be given to such fugitives from the king's grant, by the preceding conclusion.
And this teaching is notably taught in C. 25 q. 1, *Ideo permittente domino*, where it is said that whenever the Roman church grants a privilege, it expresses or understands [the clause] "unless it should decree that it be ordered otherwise". "Hence," it says, "in some chapters of the councils, when something is decreed to be observed, there is at once added: 'unless the authority of the Roman church should command it to be done otherwise', or 'saving always in all things the right of the Roman church', or 'saving in all things the apostolic authority'. When, therefore, other privileges are granted to some by the apostolic [pontiff], even if they seem to say something against the general law, yet they are not understood to grant anything against it." "Privileges," it says, "are so called as being private laws (*privata legia*)"; and it concludes finally: "The Roman church, therefore, has power to grant certain things by special favour outside the general decrees, yet with regard had to the equity of reason, so that the mother of justice may be found in nothing to dissent from it: that is, so that privileges, which are granted for the sake of religion, or of necessity, or of service rendered, may not, by relieving someone, make him so rich that, through failing to look to the losses of many, they cast some into the misery of poverty."[^16]

ENGLISH: From these a threefold corollary is clear. First, the declaration and confirmation of the [definition]{g:descripcio} of the aforesaid [privilege]{g:privilegium}.[^1] It is clear, second (since the act of privileging by the kings of the earth must be copied from the [privileging]{g:privilegiacio} of the holy Roman [church]{g:ecclesia}, which must tacitly understand a condition, as God too must, from whom all privileging is copied), that the kings of the earth must tacitly understand the reasonableness of the privilege, so that it may not tend to anyone's injury; and if [a king] has done so in ignorance, the error, once perceived, must at once be revoked, by Christ's command and by his pure licence.[^2] And it is clear, third, that it is foolish to [infer]{g:concludere} from the bare words of a privilege some [so-called]{g:vocatus} privileges which are manifest errors; because (as is clear from the fifth [book] of the *Ethics*, last chapter, on *epieikeia*) in such things one must tacitly understand a condition taken from the end. For otherwise, besides the threefold [absurdity]{g:inconveniens} (drawn out in the preceding chapter), a manifest blasphemy would follow: for example, the king of England grants to every such fugitive liberty of every kind; but freedom from sin is the greatest, without which there is no true liberty at all; therefore the king grants to every such man that he be free from sin.[^3] Since this tends to blasphemy, it is manifest that this was not the king's intention, but that they should have whatever earthly privilege lies within his power, both in goods of nature and in goods of fortune.
From these, third, it is gathered that by virtue of the said privilege many fugitives to the said place ought in [such a] case to be sharply punished, deprived of the goods of others that they occupy, and purged by other medicines, both bodily and spiritual, tending to the health of their souls. This is clear from the fact that there, by virtue of the privilege, they will have what [234] rather turns to their advantage; but [now] in such a case this would be medicine; therefore the conclusion.[^4] Hence three things move to this: the king's privileging; the end for the sake of which the privilege is granted to that place; and the benefit which the king, in charity, ought to intend for the fugitive. For if the king in error intended an end opposed to that, then, if he has not been purged of that error, his punishment is aggravated by whatever malice follows directly from it; and not only this, but [so is] the sin of the surviving kings, lords and prelates who out of sloth omit the correction they owe.[^5] For in distinction 83 it is written thus: "An error that is not resisted is approved, and truth, when it is not defended, is crushed. For to neglect to disturb the perverse, when you can, is nothing other than to foster them. Nor is he free from the scruple of a hidden fellowship who ceases to oppose a manifest crime." And would that our lords, our prelates and our schoolmen attended to that saying! For the whole labour of the schools ought to tend to this end, out of zeal for the Lord of truth, against whom offence is given.[^6]
And the quiet of the monks living there tends to the same. For since every hurtful disturbance draws its origin from sin, it is clear that the monks would not be quieted but disturbed by favour to and support of sinners. And if it is said that it is gain for them, since the fugitives share out the [temporal goods]{g:temporalia} they bring, an accusing aggravation is added: that men so holy, so estranged from the world, should be companions of thieves, communicating with the excommunicate, who hinder their contemplation and quiet in the Lord; for in Isaiah 9:5 it is written: "Every violent taking of spoils [is] with tumult." Indeed, since the holiness of a place [235] is drawn chiefly from those who dwell in it, it is clear that such an infection of the monks detracts from the holiness and reverence of the place which the privileging king intended.[^7]
Nor does it avail to say (as I have heard some feign) that within the place those injured would have the full measure of justice against the refugees, who would answer [at law] there.[^8] First, because the praiseworthy usage of the kingdom, of holding the contentious court in a profane place, ought not, for such wrongdoing, to be transferred into the house of prayer or a holy place. Second, because harm of soul would be inflicted on the monks, for whose quiet the king provided so tenderly, since [there would be] disquiet drawing them away from contemplation, the opposite of which the privileger intended. Third, because it is against divine [law]{g:ius} and the law of the church to violate the [bride]{g:sponsa} of Christ or her place. Hence in the Sext, *De immunitate ecclesiarum*, chapter *Decet*, after it is shown from scripture that holiness becomes the house of God in many ways, it is inferred thus: "Let no one, in the same places in which it is fitting that vows be celebrated with peace and quiet, stir up sedition, raise an outcry, or commit violence; let there cease in them all councils of corporations and societies, disputes and public parliaments; let vain talk cease, and much more foul and profane talk; let all chatter cease; but, finally, let whatever else could disturb the divine office or offend the eyes of the divine majesty be wholly foreign to them, lest where pardon for sins is to be asked, there occasion of sinning be given, or sins be found committed. Let there cease in churches and their cemeteries trading, and especially the tumult of fairs and of any market [236]. Let all the din of secular judgments be quiet in them. Let no cause, especially a criminal one, be pleaded there by laymen; let the same places be strangers to gatherings of laymen." Thus far there.[^9] How, I ask, could the church be a fomenter of crimes, or defend any who offend against it? Hence on the word *Sedicionem* the [gloss]{g:glosa} says that one who commits theft in a church shall not have immunity in it; and Hostiensis holds this, and rightly, since he invokes the [aid]{g:suffragium} of a place in vain who defiles it.[^10]
Since, then, whoever offends in one point is become guilty of all, it is manifest that everyone who sins mortally within a church steals [its] spiritual aid, by which God is offended more than by a theft of temporal goods. Indeed, since everyone who occupies temporal goods in mortal [sin] falls short of the right of using anything, it is then manifest that he steals the goods of the church in which he rests.[^11] If, then, he is a [transgressor]{g:prevaricator} of the tenth commandment who unjustly covets the goods of others, much more is he a transgressor of the decalogue who fraudulently and unjustly withholds the goods of others. If, then, it is not lawful for anyone to dispense with any of the Lord's commandments, it seems manifest that it is not within human power to privilege a place or a man so that it may be lawful for him to live against the Lord's command.[^12]
And here some wonder how, since it is a privilege for holy places that they be exempted from every tumult of secular business, the higher [superiors]{g:prepositi} of the church, namely bishops, priors and abbots, not only permit but claim as a privilege, and obstinately defend, that in their churches and their cemeteries there be fairs and secular business, which are never carried on without fraud and manifold crime. Now, since it is not possible that two contraries be at once a privilege to the same church, it is manifest that one must either deny the privilege of divine law and canon law concerning the [exemption]{g:exempcio} [237] of the church from the tumult of secular business, or grant that this is a privilege that hinders good morals.[^13] And I believe that our [clergy]{g:ecclesiastici} would assent to this [teaching]{g:sentencia} if they were not blinded by the gain of temporal goods; the proof of which would be [this]: if the prince took away the profit, the privilege would be renounced. But it seems most wicked and sacrilegious to sell a divine privilege for money.[^14] And by the same token [that reply] is [disposed of]{g:tollere}, so that the laws of England should not be changed, on the pretext of an error in this privilege, by bringing the King's Bench, its judge, or secular pleading into the house of the Lord; for then "the last error would be worse than the first", since an error of ignorance committed against God would be more fully increased by supine contempt; for one sin, ensnaring, draws [a man] more deeply to another.[^15]
As to the third person, namely those who flee, it is clear that the privileger intends the advantage of the fugitive; for otherwise it would not be alms or a work of mercy to him, but wholly the contrary, if he intended to harm his neighbour. Now, that in [such a] case it would be a privilege to a fugitive to be so punished is clear, supposing that some, taking occasion amiss from the security of such a refuge, injure their neighbours and so harm their own souls by sin.[^16] I suppose, second, that it would be a greater work of mercy to hinder one who thus kills his own soul than one who wishes to kill a bare body. And from these, third, it is argued thus: every greater good work of mercy is rather to be bestowed; now it is a greater good work of mercy to withdraw a good from such a man in the case specified, and to chastise the frenzied for their everlasting health, than in this way to relieve one who is needy only in bodily goods; therefore one who is able ought rather to bestow that alms. Supposing, then, the case of fugitives who have thus done harm elsewhere, the conclusion is clear.[^17]

### 11-c ¶2 · 238.28–239.31  (3 sed/autem)

### 11-d ¶2 · 243.11–245.25  (9 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 243] Unde cum dilatacio talis non foret ampliacio privilegii **sed** perversio privilegii, pars securior foret tenenda, innitendo iuri communi in cuius execucione exclusa est undequaque iniuria. Ulterius, si queratur de limitibus remissionis talium profugorum, videtur mihi quod pium esset, quoad debitum pecuniarum, docto quod fugitivi sine suo demerito sint casualiter impotentes, ut ex naufragio, incendio vel rapina, quod a suis creditoribus et specialiter a mundi divitibus et clericis dimittantur liberi, vel totaliter vel in parte; egenis **autem** creditoribus satisfaciant suo laboricio paulative. Nam XIª q. IIIª ex auctoritate beati Isidori sic habetur: Qui consentit peccantibus et defendit aliquem delinquentem maledictus erit aput Deum et homines et corripietur increpacione severissima. Hinc eciam quidam sanctissimus [p. 244] pater ait: Si quis peccantem defenderit, acrius quam ille qui peccavit coherceatur. Hinc eciam alius pater ait: Si quis alterius errori consenserit, sciat se cum illo simili modo culpabilem iudicandum. Sufficit ergo prepositis peccatum proprium etsi non cumulent alienum. Insuper aliud mirum est quomodo extortus est sensus nephandus ex dicto privilegio quod licet sceleratissimis cubare ibidem in suo crimine sine correpcione penali pro tota suo periodo, cum nec verba privilegii illud annuerint. Nam potissime extorqueretur de isto verbo quod habeant ibidem omnimodam libertatem. **Sed** revera ex istis verbis patenter infertur oppositum, cum libertas ad pausandum in peccatis non foret libertas, eo quod tunc spiritus infernales forent liberrimi. Anglia **autem** quia prius habuit consuetudinem misericordius libertantem ecclesiam, ideo dimissa martirizacione sui populi venit coronarius ad fugitivum, querens causam sui aufugii; quod si pro alio delicto quam felonia aufugerit, libertas ecclesie non ad eum. Si **autem** pro felonia, dum tamen non repugnat provido statui regni, licet sibi per quadraginta dies libere ibi quiescere et in fine unum ex tribus eligere, vel per stratam regiam tamquam exulans exire provinciam, vel publice exire, stando tamquam indigena iuri regni, vel tercio remanere infra basilicam sine hoc quod ultra de cibariis provincie sustentetur. Et (ut mihi videtur) privilegium illud foret misericordius et racionabilius quam privilegium notans casus in quibus salvandi in Anglia extracti sunt alibi et occisi. Ideo laudemus nostros principes, non vendicantes alieno iure, quod consuetudo Anglie non permittit; nam Angliam non obligant aliena privilegia alleganda, **sed** sufficit nobis fructus ulterioris privilegii, nec est aliqua lex fundabilis pro ambobus.

ENGLISH: Hence, since such an extension would be not an enlarging of the privilege but a perversion of the privilege, the safer part should be held, relying on the common law, in whose execution injury is excluded on every side. Further, if it is asked about the [limits]{g:limites} of the remission of such refugees, it seems to me that it would be pious, as regards a debt of money, once it is shown that the fugitives are by mischance unable [to pay] without demerit of their own, as from shipwreck, fire or robbery, that they be released free by their creditors, and especially by the rich of the world and by clerics, either wholly or in part; and to needy creditors they should make satisfaction by their labour, little by little.[^4] Hence many wonder how the sense of the privilege was wrested to a debt of money, since before the twenty-fifth year of Edward III there was no fleeing to a church for such a debt; and those who [privileged]{g:privilegiare}, for many years before that time, had not intended, as neither could they, that no debtor fleeing there should be bound to satisfy his creditor.[^5] For in C. 11 q. 3, on the authority of blessed Isidore, it is had thus: "He who consents to sinners and defends anyone offending will be cursed before God and men, and will be rebuked with the severest reproof." Hence also another father says: "If anyone consents to another's error, let him know that he is to be judged culpable in like manner with him." For it would be wrested above all from this word: that they have there liberty of every kind. But in truth the opposite is plainly inferred from these words, since a liberty to rest in sins would not be liberty, because then the infernal spirits would be the most free. And if it is asked how much liberty they will have there, the answer is that a great liberty has been granted by the kings to the English church, beyond what other kingdoms have or the canon laws express: namely, that every fugitive to any church in England for any felony has a period of forty days there, like the Ninevites and the penitents in Lent, to whom so long a time of penance has been granted.[^7] For that place, however, the privilege exceeds this in two things: first in this, that whereas in other places the site is restricted to the basilica or a few paces outside the church, there they can rest secure through the whole precinct of Westminster; [second, that] whereas in other churches also the cause of flight is [limited]{g:limitare} to felony, there it is extended to any misdeed or offence whatever with which the prosperity of the kingdom and the integrity of the [regality]{g:regalia} can stand. Therefore let us praise our princes, who do not lay claim by a foreign law, which the custom of England does not permit; for foreign privileges that may be [alleged]{g:allegare} do not bind England, but the fruit of the further privilege suffices us, nor is there any law that can be founded for both.[^10]

### 11-e ¶1 · 246.26–248.20  (9 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 246] **Sed** quia videtur per verba decreti quod membra coincidunt, licet racio non sit diversa, ideo notandum quod lex potest dici communis dupliciter, scilicet obiectiva obligacione vel formali predicacione; primo [p. 247] modo est lex communissima quod Deus est super omnia appetendus, cum nedum omnem creaturam racionalem **sed** omnem creatam substanciam semper obligat. Leges **autem** communes predicacione sunt species vel genera legum que in suis individuis sunt fundata, ut clericum curare de sua fama et genera similia, sive accepta pro legum privatarum multitudine, sive pro universalibus quiditative predicatis de sua specie. Et sic iste due maneries legum non sunt contrarie in suo analogo, cum canon describit legem publicam que a sanctis patribus scriptis est confirmata; quod notum est competere cuilibet vero privilegio, sicut et descripcio privilegii competit legi communi. Cum ergo iuxta decretum omnis lex privata a spiritu sancto inscribitur, manifestum est quod omnis lex privata concessa est a principe ad utilitatem ecclesie vel persone, eo quod opera trinitatis sunt indivisa ad extra. Deus **autem** nescit docere vel dare nisi utilia, et sic omnis lex creata individua est privilegium. Et patet quomodo loquendum est logice de privilegiis, detecta equivocacione termini, ut oportet. Ad secundam instanciam negatur assumptum, cum Christus dedit ecclesie duplex genus privilegii, quoddam [p. 248] extrinsecum, affirmativum, negativum vel privativum, ut Matthei X°, 9, dicitur quomodo privilegiavit discipulos, quando misit illos ad predicandum: Nolite, inquit, possidere aurum neque argentum neque pecuniam in zonis vestris. Privilegia **autem** corporalia positiva concedit eis, ut patet Luce X°, 7: In eadem, inquit, domo manete edentes et bibentes que aput illos sunt; dignus est enim operarius mercede sua. Talia **autem** privilegia instrumentaliter et ministratorie concedi possunt a domino temporali; **sed** est dare spiritualia privilegia pociora que Christus prius concessit suis discipulis, ut patet Matthei X, 8: Infirmos, inquit, curate, mortuos suscitate, leprosos mundate, demones eicite, Talia **autem** potestativa privilegia appropriate conceduntur a domino, que licet princeps terrenus non posset concedere, tamen concedendo temporalia privilegia propriatorie potest disponere ut Deus concedat privilegia pociora, sicut aperiens fenestram vel removens prohibens est occasio, unde lumen solis per domum infunditur.

ENGLISH: But because it seems from the words of the decree that the members [of the division] coincide, although the aspect is not different, it must therefore be noted that a law can be called common in two ways: namely, by objective obligation or by formal predication.[^1] In the first [247] way the most common law is that God is to be desired above all things, since it binds always not only every rational creature but every created substance. For God is the good which all things desire; and so all the other commandments of the decalogue are common laws objectively, since they necessarily bind all men. But laws common by predication are the species or genera of laws which are founded in their individuals, such as that a cleric should take care of his reputation, and similar genera, whether taken for the multitude of private laws or for universals predicated of their species as to their [quiddity]{g:quiditas}.[^2] And so these two kinds of law are not contrary in their analogue, since the canon defines public law as that which has been confirmed by the writings of the holy fathers; and this is known to fit every true privilege, just as the [definition]{g:descripcio} of privilege fits common law.[^3] Hence that law which is common to a private part of the [church]{g:ecclesia} is a [privilege]{g:privilegium} with respect to its whole: as a law which is common to the individual persons of a given monastery of monks is a privilege to the whole house; and an [endowment]{g:dotacio} in temporal things, if it is a law common to the whole [possessioner]{g:possessionati} clergy and to its individual persons, is then a privilege with respect to the endowed person of the clergy, because it does not belong to the person of the clergy that lives [without property]{g:exproprietarie}, but privately to the former person.[^4] Since, therefore, according to the decree every private law is written in by the Holy Spirit, it is manifest that every private law is granted by a prince for the benefit of the church or of a person, because the works of the Trinity outwards are undivided. And God knows not how to teach or give anything but what is beneficial; and so every created individual law is a privilege. And it is clear how one must speak logically of privileges, once the equivocation of the term has been exposed, as is fitting.[^5] To the second [objection]{g:instancia} the [assumption]{g:assumptum} is denied, since Christ gave the church a twofold kind of privilege: one [248] extrinsic, affirmative, negative or privative, as it is said in Matthew 10:9 how he privileged the disciples when he sent them to preach: "Do not," he says, "possess gold, nor silver, nor money in your purses." And positive bodily privileges he grants them, as is clear in Luke 10:7: "In the same house," he says, "remain, eating and drinking such things as they have: for the labourer is worthy of his hire." Now such privileges can be granted instrumentally and ministerially by a [temporal lord]{g:temporales domini}; but there are spiritual privileges, of greater worth, which Christ granted his disciples before, as is clear in Matthew 10:8: "Heal," he says, "the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out devils."[^6] And such privileges of power are granted appropriately by the Lord; and although an earthly prince could not grant them, yet by granting temporal privileges he can, preparatorily, dispose [things] so that God grants the privileges of greater worth; as one who opens a window, or removes what hinders, is the occasion whereby the light of the sun is poured through the house.[^7]

### 11-e ¶3 · 249.3–249.34  (4 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 249] Unde videtur mihi quod collegium predicte abbathie primo attenderet, si vendicatum privilegium sit privilegiis Christi contrarium. Secundo quod lucrum reportabit ex privilegio sic defenso, quia indubie si non meritum quoad Deum **sed** nude mundi gloriam ex privilegio reportat anime detrimentum. Tercio, supposito quod reportet ex operibus misericordie indultis profugis salutem anime, attenderet cum suis fautoribus quod pro veris privilegiis plus necessariis defendendis diligenciam tantam apponerent vel maiorem. Sepe nempe mundo decepti cece preponderant folia fructui, umbram luci, ymmo venenum pruriens temporale perpetue saluti. Ad tercium dicitur quod falsum assumitur, nam peccatum cum non sit privata lex nec concessum principe non est privilegium. Quamvis enim princeps huius mundi excitat ad peccandum, non tamen potest concedere, sicut nec Deus, ad illud licenciam, et si per impossibile concederet ad hoc vel pretense licenciam, hoc non faceret ad privilegiandi commodum **sed** ad dampnum. Conceditur tamen ex immensitate potestatis veri principis quod racione cuiuscunque peccati occasionatur ecclesie Christi verum privilegium, cum timentibus Deum omnia cooperantur in bonum Rom. Et hoc est verum non solum de alienis peccatis **sed** de peccatis propriis que predestinati perpetrant. ENGLISH: Hence it seems to me that the college of the aforesaid abbey should attend, first, to whether the privilege claimed is contrary to the privileges of Christ. Second, to what gain it will carry away from the privilege so defended; because undoubtedly, if it carries away from the privilege not merit with respect to God but barely the glory of the world, it carries away harm to the soul. Third, supposing that it carries away the salvation of the soul from the works of mercy granted to the refugees, it should attend, with its supporters, to [this]: that they should apply as much diligence, or greater, to defending the true privileges, which are more necessary.[^10] For often those deceived by the world blindly weigh the leaves above the fruit, the shadow above the light, indeed an itching temporal poison above everlasting salvation. To the third [objection] [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that a falsehood is assumed; for sin, since it is neither a private law nor granted by a prince, is not a privilege. For although the prince of this world stirs [men] up to sin, yet he cannot, any more than God can, grant licence for it; and if, per impossibile, he granted licence for it, even a pretended one, he would do this not for the advantage but for the harm of the one privileged.[^11] Yet it is granted, from the immensity of the power of the true prince, that by reason of any sin whatever a true privilege is occasioned to the church of Christ, since "to them that fear God all things work together unto good" (Romans 8:28): not that sin is a privilege to them, but that the profit arising [by occasion]{g:occasionaliter} from sin turns to their privilege. And this is true not only of others' sins but of the sins of their own which the [predestinate]{g:predestinatus} commit. Yet it is granted that punishment turns to privilege for the sons, since the sting of the flesh, given by God to the Apostle, turned for him to the exercise of virtue, as is clear in 2 Corinthians 12[:7].[^12]

### 11-e ¶4 · 250.1–250.16  (3 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 250] Unde sancti plus contentantur de tribulacione quam de mundana prosperitate propter securitatem et fructum meriti ampliorem, dampnati **autem** habent quoddam bonum ultra bonum nature ex merito Christi et sua punicione iusta; quod bonum, licet sit bonum utile beatis et honestum domino punienti, non tamen videtur mihi quod illud bonum sit eis privilegium utile vel honestum **sed** bonum mitigativum malicie vel quomodocunque aliter apcius nominetur. ENGLISH: Hence the saints are more content with tribulation than with worldly prosperity, because of the security and the ampler fruit of merit; and the damned have a certain good beyond the good of nature, from the merit of Christ and their own just punishment; and this good, although it is a useful good to the blessed and an honourable one to the Lord who punishes, yet it does not seem to me that it is a useful or honourable privilege to them, but a good that mitigates malice, or however else it may more aptly be named. Hence, although it is a private law granted by a prince, yet it is not for the benefit of the person in whom it formally inheres. Nor do I contend [against it], but I grant, in an equivocal sense, that that damnation is a privilege to the blessed, since by reason of the just punishment of their enemies, who serve them perpetually, their joy is in a manner increased.[^13]

It is a falsehood to profess oneself a bishop, priest or cleric and to work things contrary to this order."[^2] And such laws of the church ought to be preached both to the greater clerics and to the lesser, since they are their chief law, and also to the laity, since the father of lies with his sophists has infected a great part of the [so-called]{g:vocatus} church with falsehoods. To claim, then, secular [exercise of lordship]{g:dominacio}, even more than the laity, over goods so much another's, the goods of the poor, is a horrible falsehood.[^4] Therefore the faithful man ought not, because of such falsehoods, to fail to preach to the people the danger from turning aside [252] from the law of the Lord; because, as is said in C. 11 q. 3 [c. 55], *Inter verba*: "It is enough for a Christian to call a cleansed conscience and God to witness of his work. 'We have,' says Gregory, 'Paul saying: For our glory is this, the testimony of our conscience. Job also says: Behold my witness is in heaven. If,' he says, 'we have a witness in heaven, a witness in the heart, let us leave the foolish to say outside what they will. ### 12-a ¶3 · 253.8–254.23  (4 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 253] Secundo principaliter arguitur per hoc quod dicta de potestate privilegiandi nedum sonant in Dei blasphemiam **sed** derogant potestati pape et regalie regum seculi. Dictum est enim quod Deus non potest auferre quicquam ab homine stante iure suo, nisi satisfaciat aliunde; quod repugnat potencie adnichilandi, et multo magis dominus papa non potest licite auferre beneficium a digno nisi deliquerit. Nec rex habet super omnibus bonis sui regni plenum dominium; que videntur sonare blasphemiam et sacrilegium. Hic negatur conclusio, cum Deus absolute necessario habet summam potestatem privilegiandi, et illa est infinitum maior quam aliqua potestas creata. Quantum ad primam instanciam, concedi debet conclusio: nam si Deus aufert quidquam ab homine stante iure suo sine hoc quod recompenset aliunde, tunc iniuriatur illi homini, quod non potest. Nam iustum est quod haberet ablatum, cum stat ius primum, ut ponitur, **sed** nemo est causa quare ius illud tollitur nisi Deus, ut ponitur; ergo solus Deus est causa illius iniurie. Et per consequens, cum iusticia sit constans et perpetua voluntas divina, tribuens unicuique quod suum est, patet cum veris sequi quod Deus est contrarius sibi ipsi, quia simul vult quod habent datum bonum, cum sit iustum, et vult quod nunc non habeat, cum aufert. Loquimur enim de habere opposito ablacioni [p. 254] qua Deus aufert, et de iure ad sic habendum pro eadem mensura. Similiter vel est ablatarius contentatus de ablacione Dei vel remurmurat vel est neuter. **Sed** si contentatus, meretur a Deo meritum vel augmentum meriti, quod est valencius quam foret abicio oblati, et per consequens, Deus per commutacionem vel recompensacionem super remunerat. Si ablatarius remurmurat, perit propter peccatum suum ius habendi, et sumus extra casum conclusionis, sicut Egipcii forisfacientes in capitalem dominum exciderunt a iure bonorum a quibus ex mandato domini habentis ipsa in fisco fuerant spoliati. Cui, inquam, debuit iustus iudex dare bona sic confiscata nisi servis suis qui prius ipsa meruerant? Si **autem** ablatarius sit neuter quoad actum explicitum, equanimiter tollerat ipsum eventum, et quicquid Deus decreverit faciendum, et per consequens, accrescit in merito; talem enim actum vel habitum confusum oportet inesse omni iusto; et patet conclusio. Unde ista fides de absolute necessaria iusticia et misericordia Dei facit christianos gaudenter pati et gratulanter acceptare quicquid prosperitatis vel pene eis acciderit, quia sciunt demonstrative quod Deus non potest eis humiliter pacientibus deficere, quin loco ablati tribuat pociora.

ENGLISH: Second, [it is argued principally, by appeal to]{g:arguitur per} this: that what was said about the power of privileging not only sounds to the blasphemy of God but derogates from the power of the [pope]{g:papa} and the [regality]{g:regalia} of the kings of the world. For it has been said that God cannot take anything away from a man while his right stands, unless he makes satisfaction from elsewhere, which conflicts with the power of annihilating; and much more, that the lord pope cannot lawfully take a benefice away from a worthy man unless he has offended; nor does the king have full [lordship]{g:dominium} over all the goods of his kingdom: things which seem to sound of blasphemy and sacrilege.[^10] To this the [conclusion]{g:conclusio} is denied, since God, absolutely necessarily, has the highest power of privileging, and it is infinitely greater than any created power. As to the first [objection]{g:instancia}, the conclusion ought to be granted: for if God takes anything away from a man while his right stands, without recompensing from elsewhere, then he does injury to that man, which he cannot do. For it is just that he should have what was taken away, since the first right stands, as is supposed; but no one is the cause why that right is taken away except God, as is supposed; therefore God alone is the cause of that injury.[^11] And consequently, since justice is the constant and perpetual divine will that gives to each what is his, it is clear that it [follows]{g:sequitur} together with truths that God is contrary to himself, because at the same time he wills that they have the good given, since it is just, and wills that he now not have it, since he takes it away. For we are speaking of having as opposed to the taking away [254] by which God takes away, and of the right to have so, for the same measure [of time].[^12] Likewise: either the one deprived is content with God's taking away, or he murmurs against it, or he is neither. Now if content, he merits from God merit or an increase of merit, which is of more worth than the loss of what was taken away would be; and consequently God, by [exchange]{g:commutacio} or recompense, over-rewards. If the one deprived murmurs, his right of having perishes through his sin, and we are outside the case of the conclusion: as the Egyptians, [committing forfeiture]{g:forisfacere} against the [chief lord]{g:capitalis dominus}, fell from the right to the goods of which, by command of the Lord who had them in his treasury, they had been despoiled. To whom, I say, ought the just judge to have given the goods thus confiscated but to his servants, who had earlier earned them?[^13] But if the one deprived is neither, as regards an explicit act, he bears the event itself with equanimity, and whatever God has decreed is to be done; and consequently he grows in merit; for such an act or [indistinct]{g:confusus} habit must be present in every just man; and the conclusion is clear. Hence this faith concerning the absolutely necessary justice and mercy of God makes Christians suffer joyfully and accept thankfully whatever prosperity or pain befalls them, because they know demonstratively that God cannot fail them when they suffer humbly, without giving things of greater worth in place of what was taken away.[^14]

And therefore Christ teaches, Matthew 6:2–3, that in doing alms you should not sound a trumpet before you as the hypocrites [do], but let the left hand (that is, the intention that expects a temporal reward) not know what the right hand does: [the right hand being] the part of the intention by which a spiritual and perpetual reward for the other world is rightly and purely expected; and according as these are more matched with greater charity, the alms in the giver is greater.[^13] From this it is clear, with the truth of the fact, that so-called perpetual alms for the most part, or always, have less of the nature of alms than temporary alms to be given. For one who gives alms in that way commonly fails in the discretion of giving to those more needy; he fails, second, in humbling, believing that what is given is his own to order as he will, with no superior lord moving him as his bare servant and instrument; [287] and finally, commonly, in alms both bodily and spiritual, there creeps in an affection of vainglory or of a useful temporal good; and (if I am not mistaken) Caesar's eye was in some way infected in these three.[^14] Who, I ask, could teach that Caesar or another secular lord, in founding perpetual alms so publicly, does not aim at the vainglory of the solemnity of his name and of his line, to last for long ages? And if that outweighs the intention of the right hand, he sins mortally, making alms out of what is another's.[^15] And [the answer] to the form of the argument is clear, by granting the conclusion and [what follows]{g:sequela}: for the greatest alms on the [way]{g:via} is that one should leave civil possession in the world entirely and lay down his present life for the honour of God and the profit of the church, as Christ says, John 15:13: "Greater charity no man hath than that a man lay down his life for his friends"; such a one gives him all his goods of fortune and all his natural goods; and since all things are the just man's, it is clear that in such a giving the most generous alms is founded; and the conclusion is clear.[^16]

### 13-d ¶2 · 288.9–289.15  (4 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 288] Unde logici cognoscunt quomodo data elemosina est alia maior; que alia est priori quantumlibet maior elemosina. Unde contingit quod potus aque frigide sit maior elemosina danti ex simpliciori oculo et maiori fervore caritatis, quam fuit dotacio qua Constantinus dotavit ecclesiam, vel elemosina qua rex terrenus fundavit grandissimam abbathiam. Nam nisi spirituale opus misericordie prevaleat corporali, stante isto cursu ecclesie, domini seculares forent nimis exaltati in celo et viri apostolici nimis mediocriter premiati. Et patet ad questionem qua queritur, cui seculares darent elemosinas nisi clero; nam illis tenentur dare prudenter de quanto sustinerent eorum privilegia data a Christo, et continuarent eorum disposicionem ad rependendum elemosinas spirituales. Alias **autem** elemosinas darent corporaliter magis egentibus. Unde si ex ceca pietate et falsa misericordia nimis onerant clerum cum possessione civili, contingit eos ex hinc incidere in excommunicacionem et heresim racione ablacionis primevi privilegii concessi a Christo suis discipulis, ut meminit Canon XXIIª d. Omnes. Et ita concedo quod dotacio ecclesie fuit altrinsecus meritoria, **sed** dotacio ac status in quo Christus ipsam [p. 289] instituit fuit perfeccior. Unde verisimile est quod multi ex defectu serpentine prudencie in ista elemosina ecclesie altrinsecus sunt dampnati; diuturnior **autem** elemosina est breviori prestancior, **sed** nemo dicit quod diuturnitas elemosine penes formam diuturnitatis expresse in cartis regulariter attendatur, cum stat elemosinam horalem de correpcione fraterna manere perpetuo in fructu, ubi elemosina cartalis sub nomine perpetuitatis expressa extinguitur infra annum tam in substancia elemosine quam in fructu. Sic ergo dans prudenter, ferventer et paulative elemosinas continue plus crescit et superaddit elemosinas quam ceteris paribus faceret sub signo elemosine perpetue dando simul, quia fervor caritatis et vigil custodia rectificande elemosine forent meritorius conservata.

ENGLISH: Hence logicians know how, any alms being given, there is another greater; and that other is an alms greater than the former by any amount whatever. Hence it can be that a drink of cold water is a greater alms, for one who gives it with a simpler eye and with greater fervour of [charity]{g:caritas}, than was the [endowment]{g:dotacio} with which Constantine endowed the church, or the alms with which an earthly king founded a very great abbey.[^4] For unless the spiritual work of mercy outweighed the bodily, as this course of the church stands, secular lords would be too much exalted in heaven and apostolic men too moderately rewarded. And [the answer] is clear to the question that asks to whom seculars should give alms, if not to the clergy: for they are bound to give to them prudently as much as would sustain their [privileges]{g:privilegium} given by Christ and continue their disposition to repay spiritual alms. And other alms they should give in bodily form to those more needy.[^5] Hence if, out of blind [piety]{g:pietas} and false mercy, they overload the clergy with civil possession, it happens that they thereby fall into excommunication and heresy, by reason of the taking away of the primeval privilege granted by Christ to his disciples, as the canon *Omnes* in distinction 22 recalls.[^6] And so I grant that the endowment of the church was meritorious on either side, but the endowment and state in which Christ [289] instituted it was more perfect. Hence it is likely that many, from lack of the serpent's prudence, have been damned on either side in that alms of the church; and a longer-lasting alms is more excellent than a briefer one, but no one says that the long duration of an alms is to be reckoned, as a rule, by the form of duration expressed in charters, since it can be that an hour's alms of fraternal correction remains perpetually in its fruit, where a charter alms expressed under the name of perpetuity is extinguished within a year, both in the substance of the alms and in its fruit.[^7] So, then, one who gives alms prudently, fervently and little by little grows continually more, and adds on alms more, than, other things being equal, he would by giving all at once under the sign of perpetual alms; because the fervour of charity and the watchful keeping of the alms that is to be set right would be meritoriously preserved.[^8]

### 13-d ¶3 · 289.16–289.22  (1 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 289] Tercio principaliter arguitur per hoc quod elemosina debet sic dari, ut generi secularium elemosinanti accresceret maior utilitas et generi cleri elemosinandi accresceret plus excitativa securitas ad vigilanter perficiendum officium clericale; **sed** quis dubitat quin hoc contingeret sub forma dotacionis perpetue? Ergo illa est dotacione temporali prestancior.

ENGLISH: Third, principally, [it is argued by appeal to]{g:arguitur per} this, that alms ought to be given in such a way that a greater utility would accrue to the line of seculars giving the alms, and a security more stirring to the vigilant performance of the clerical office would accrue to the line of clergy receiving the alms; now who doubts that this would come about under the form of perpetual endowment? Therefore that is more excellent than temporary endowment.[^9]

### 13-d ¶4 · 289.23–290.10  (3 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 289] Hic dicitur quod verum assumitur, unde pro minori quilibet christianus debet credere quod in temporalibus dotacio temporalis excluso signo perpetuitatis humane elemosine foret undiquaque perfeccior: et patet defectus consequencie. Unde pro declaracione prime partis maioris assumpte patet eius veritas ex dictis, cum talis elemosinacio foret factis Christi et apostolorum conformior, et per consequens elemosinanti utilior. Unde Act. III° legitur quomodo possessores agrorum vendebant et ponebant ante pedes apostolorum, non ut hereditarie vel perpetue possiderent, **sed** ut divideretur singulis prout cuique opus [p. 290] erat. Nec solum servata est illa forma elemosinandi in Judea, ubi Deus scivit apostolos non perpetuo permansuros, **sed** in Asia, in India, in Ethiopia et ubicunque terrarum christiani conversi sunt et presbiteri constituti; **sed** indubie illi ex maiori fervore caritatis, ex maiori prudencia ac simplicitate quam nostri succedentes qui sub forma dotacionis perpetue dabant sua et magis quieta, ac in pace Christi plus firmata est ecclesia illa primitiva conversa quam fuit nostra ecclesia cesarea sic dotata.

ENGLISH: To this the answer is that what is assumed [in the major] is true; hence, as to the minor, every Christian ought to believe that in temporal goods a temporary endowment, with the sign of the perpetuity of human alms excluded, would be in every respect more perfect: and the defect of the [inference]{g:consequencia} is clear.[^10] Hence, for the declaration of the first part of the major [premiss] assumed, its truth is clear from what has been said, since such almsgiving would be more conformed to the deeds of Christ and of the apostles, and consequently more useful to the [almsgiver]{g:elemosinans}. Hence in Acts 4:34–35 we read how the owners of lands sold and laid [the price] before the feet of the apostles, not that they should possess hereditarily or perpetually, but that distribution should be made to every one according as he had [290] need.[^11] Nor was that form of giving alms kept only in Judaea, where God knew that the apostles would not remain forever, but in Asia, in India, in Ethiopia, and wherever in the world Christians were converted and [presbyters]{g:presbiter} appointed; but without doubt those [gave theirs] with greater fervour of charity, with greater prudence and simplicity, than our successors, who gave theirs under the form of perpetual endowment; and that primitive converted church was quieter and more firmly established in the peace of Christ than was our caesarean church so endowed.[^12]

### 13-e ¶1 · 290.20–292.1  (3 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 290] Immo ut probabiliter creditur tota dissensio inter regna et gentes diversarum sectarum exoritur propter omissionem culpabilem consiliorum ac privilegiorum que Christus instituit clero suo. Nam si omnes redditus quibus in christianismo dotatur ecclesia, forent inter seculares dominos distributi et clerus ostenderet in se concorditer ac efficaciter contemptum seculi ac tercio predicaret laicis vigilanter de fallaci affeccione temporalium pro momento quo peregrinamur a domino, et quomodo instantaneum seculare desiderium inducit dampnacionem perpetuam, brevis **autem** mundi contemptus preparat efficaciter ad beatitudinem consequendam, [p. 291] tunc cessarent bella in christianismo, vel simpliciter vel in parte, et disponeretur ecclesia Christi ad conquirendum armis virtutum populum infidelem. Sic enim fuit tempore apostolorum, quando exproprietarie pure secundum Christi consilia vixit clerus, **sed** commixtus sinistris tradicionibus humanis cum dexteris Christi consiliis, vinum in quo inebriaretur ecclesia est limphatum et genus nervorum caritatis ecclesie debilitatum et sine custode zizannium discordie undique seminatum. Cum enim corpus ecclesie sit limitatum a Deo, quibus cibis et qualiter secundum partes dispares pasceretur, Deus sicut tribuit commensurale alimentum spirituale per prelatos distribuendum fidelibus, sic dat escam corporalem parciendam per activos tam predicatoribus in agro quam contemplatoribus in lecto. Ideo usurpantibus illis violentis officium et consumentibus inordinate eorum pabulum necesse est totum corpus ecclesie esse inordinatum ex defectu observacionis ordinis suarum parcium. Si enim fit bolismus in homine et breviter quocunque membro secundum appetitum caninum contra regulas virtutis regitive trahente alterius nutrimentum vel occupante alterius membri officium, fit in toto corpore discrasia. Si enim viator haberet vermem in stomacho qui consumeret nutrimentum membrorum, quomodo servaretur sanitas et valitudo eorum? Sic si sacerdotes Baal cum uxoribus et filiis consumunt mendaciter per meatus nocturnos et subterraneos bona plebis, patet littera Danielis XIV°: sic **autem** est in corpore ecclesie cuius virtus regitiva est patris sapiencia; et leges eius, ut mandata atque consilia, sunt privilegia singulariter membris ecclesie officium operandi et nutrimentum limitancia, ut membra superiora debent pasci precipue spiritu animali et regere ac movere membra supposita; [p. 292] quod si dislocantur et innaturaliter pascuntur, necesse est ut in aliis membris fiat discordia.

ENGLISH: Indeed, as is probably believed, the whole dissension between [kingdoms]{g:regnum} and peoples of different [sects]{g:secta} arises from the culpable omission of the counsels and [privileges]{g:privilegium} which Christ instituted for his [clergy]{g:clerus}. For if all the [revenues]{g:reditus} with which the [church]{g:ecclesia} is endowed in Christendom were distributed among the secular lords, and the clergy showed in themselves, with one accord and to good effect, contempt of the world, and thirdly preached watchfully to the laity of the deceitful affection for [temporal goods]{g:temporalia} for the moment in which we are [pilgrims]{g:peregrinari} away from the Lord,[^1] and of how an instantaneous worldly desire brings on perpetual damnation, while a brief contempt of the world prepares effectively for attaining blessedness, [291] then wars would cease in Christendom, either [without qualification]{g:simpliciter} or in part, and the church of Christ would be disposed to conquer the [unbelieving]{g:infidelis} people with the arms of the virtues. For so it was in the time of the apostles, when the clergy lived purely [without property]{g:exproprietarie} according to the counsels of Christ; but, [the clergy] being mingled with left-hand human traditions together with the right-hand counsels of Christ,[^2] the wine with which the church was to be inebriated has been watered, and the kind of the sinews of the church's [charity]{g:caritas} weakened, and, there being no keeper, the [cockle]{g:zizania} of discord sown everywhere.[^3] For since the body of the church has been [assigned]{g:limitare} by God what foods it should be fed with, and how, according to its unlike parts, God, as he grants commensurate spiritual nourishment to be distributed to the faithful through the [prelates]{g:prelatus}, so gives bodily food to be shared out through the active, to the preachers in the field as well as to the contemplatives in bed.[^4] Therefore, when those violent ones usurp [their] office and inordinately consume their fodder, the whole body of the church must be disordered, from failure to observe the order of its parts.[^5] For if bulimia arises in a man, and, in short, if any [member]{g:membrum}, by a canine appetite against the rules of the governing power, draws away the nourishment of another or occupies the office of another member, a dyscrasia arises in the whole body.[^6] For if a [wayfarer]{g:viator} had a worm in his stomach which consumed the nourishment of the members, how would their health and strength be preserved?[^7] So it is if the [priests]{g:sacerdos} of Baal with their wives and children consume the goods of the people deceitfully, through nightly and underground passages, as the letter of Daniel 14 makes clear; and so it is in the body of the church, whose governing power is the wisdom of the Father.[^8] And its laws, as the commandments and counsels, are privileges assigning singly to the members of the church the office of working and their nourishment, as the higher members ought to be fed chiefly by the animal spirit, and to rule and move the members placed beneath; [292] and if these are dislocated and fed unnaturally, discord must arise in the other members.[^9]

### 13-e ¶2 · 292.2–292.17  (1 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 292] Unde credo quod nunquam in corpore ecclesie cessabit simpliciter bellum, dolus atque contencio, antequam cleri dislocacio, secularis officii usurpacio, et per consequens officii speculatoris omissio sit per virtutem regitivam ecclesie restituta; in tanta namque armonia constituta est pars mundi superior quod secundum Commentatorem una stella addita celo vel moveret cum fatigacione et pena, vel simpliciter non moveret; et multo magis in regno celorum quod est ecclesia contrariacio superiorum suo motori facit quod tota ecclesia fatigabiliter sit turbata. Tota **autem** contrariacio stat in hoc quod bona temporalia plus bonis perpetuis sunt amata. Ideo ve illis qui stulte faciunt in affeccione pastorum temporalia spiritualibus anteponi.

ENGLISH: Hence I believe that war, guile and contention will never cease without qualification in the body of the church before the dislocation of the clergy, the usurpation of secular office, and in consequence the omission of the office of [watchman]{g:speculator}, has been set right by the governing power of the church. For the upper part of the world is constituted in such harmony that, according to the Commentator, one star added to the heaven would either move with fatigue and pain or would not move, without qualification;[^10] and much more, in the kingdom of heaven, which is the church, the contrariety of superiors to their mover makes the whole church troubled to the point of fatigue. And the whole contrariety consists in this, that temporal goods are loved more than perpetual goods. Therefore woe to those who foolishly cause temporal things to be put before spiritual in the affection of pastors.

### 13-e ¶3 · 292.18–292.28  (2 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 292] Si **autem** prelati sint magis avari, honoris mundani plus cupidi, plus viventes secundum fastum seculi, per bella excitata de bonis pauperum ad vindicandum proprias iniurias amplius secularibus insistentes, et (quod periculosissimum est) sub pallio sanctitatis quicquid perpetraverint fuco adulterandi verbum Dei sophistice defendentes, quomodo cum isto circulo quinarii stabit regnum? Scio quod in ista rota currus diaboli seducerentur (si fieri potest) eciam electi, et non sic preparatur ad hic augendum populum domini **sed** satellitum Antichristi.

ENGLISH: But if prelates are more avaricious, more covetous of worldly honour, living more according to the pomp of the world, pressing harder upon secular things through wars stirred up out of the goods of the poor to avenge their own injuries, and (what is most dangerous) defending sophistically, under the cloak of holiness, whatever they have perpetrated, with the paint of adulterating the word of God, how will the kingdom stand with this circle of five?[^11] I know that on this wheel of the devil's chariot even the elect would be led astray (if it can be), and so no preparation is made here for increasing the people of the Lord, but [the people] of the henchmen of Antichrist.[^12]

### 13-e ¶4 · 292.29–294.10  (2 sed/autem)

LATIN: [p. 292] Et quantum ad secundam partem assumpti, patet quod infideliter vendicatur a clericis in talibus dotacionibus a mundo securitas; nam carta evangelii quam Christus concessit non potest deficere. Unde Matthei VI°, 25, precipit clero suo ne sit sollicitus [p. 293] quid manducabit vel bibet, aut quomodo induatur, sicut solliciti sunt viventes gentiliter, quia, ut arguit per locum a maiori: Si Deus providet pastum volucribus celi et tegumentum liliis agri, quanto magis domesticis fidei? Si enim tempore legis veteris quando minus suadebatur elemosina, Psalmista dicat, Psalmo XXXVI°, 26: Junior fui, etenim senui et non vidi iustum derelictum nec semen eius querens panem, quanto plus hodie? Tollat ergo clerus offensam domini non declinando a lege sua, sicut communiter facimus affectando inordinate voluptatem et curiositatem, et non est compossibile quod ad deterioriacionem desint nobis necessaria vite nostre, quia Matthei VI°, 33, dicit veritas: Querite ergo primum regnum Dei et iusticiam eius, et hec omnia adicientur vobis. Privilegium **autem** Christi concessum Luce X°, suis discipulis est securius quam carta vel concessio mundana, cum dominus mundi qui mentiri non poterit sic precipit: In quamcunque domum intraveritis ibi manete edentes et bibentes que apud illos sunt. **Sed** debemus cavere sollicite ne amittamus hoc privilegium per abusum, quod fit quando contra regulam et vitam apostoli sumus hospitibus onerosi. Quomodo ergo stat cum fide et religione christiana quod clerus confederatus et obligatus mundo per cartas plus confidit in dotacione cesarea quam divina, cum Luce XXI°, 34, precipiat ut caveamus a crapula et curis seculi. Et patet ex fide scripture quomodo observantes ordinem illum preposterum sunt insecurissimi quoad homines et quoad Deum. Oportet enim primo quod originetur in Deo securitas que non potest fundari nisi in observancia legis sue. Et [p. 294] hec racio quare Deus ad abstergendum cecitatem mundanam ab oculis mentis nostre ordinat nunc translacionem regnorum in qua extinguitur cleri mundana securitas, nunc permittit predones, fures, incendium vel alium eventum fortuitum evenire. Unde tollantur temporalia in quibus seculares clerici prepostere confidebant, et nunc tercio permittit dissensiones oriri inter partes, tollens seculi potentes de medio per quod coniectura providencie humane eciam in superioribus ecclesie sepius est cassata.

ENGLISH: And as to the second part of the [assumption]{g:assumptum}, it is clear that security from the world is claimed faithlessly by clerics in such [endowments]{g:dotacio}; for the charter of the gospel which Christ granted cannot fail.[^13] Hence in Matthew 6:25 he commands his clergy not to be solicitous [293] what they shall eat or drink, or how they shall be clothed, as those who live like the heathen are solicitous; because, as he argues by [the topic from the greater]{g:locus a maiori}: if God provides food for the birds of the air and covering for the lilies of the field, how much more for those who are of the household of the faith?[^14] For if in the time of the old law, when [alms]{g:elemosina} were less urged, the Psalmist says in Psalm 36:26, "I have been young and now am old; and I have not seen the just forsaken, nor his seed seeking bread," how much more today?[^15] Let the clergy therefore remove the offence of the Lord by not turning aside from his law, as we commonly do by inordinately [desiring]{g:affectare} pleasure and curiosity; and it is not [compossible]{g:compossibilis} that the necessaries of our life should fail us to [our] impairment, for in Matthew 6:33 the Truth says: "Seek ye therefore first the kingdom of God, and his justice, and all these things shall be added unto you." And the privilege of Christ granted to his disciples in Luke 10 is more secure than a worldly charter or [grant]{g:concessio}, since the Lord of the world, who cannot lie, commands thus: "Into whatsoever house you enter, there remain, eating and drinking such things as they have."[^16] But we must take careful heed not to lose this privilege through abuse, which happens when, against the rule and life of the apostle, we are burdensome to our hosts. How then does it stand with the faith and the christian religion that the clergy, confederated with and bound to the world by charters, trusts more in caesarean endowment than in divine, since in Luke 21:34 he commands that we beware of surfeiting and the cares of the world?[^17] And it is clear [from the faith of scripture]{g:ex fide} how those who observe that preposterous order are most insecure as regards men and as regards God. For security must first originate in God, and it cannot be founded except in the observance of his law. And [294] this is the reason why God, to wipe worldly blindness from the eyes of our mind, now ordains the transfer of kingdoms, in which the worldly security of the clergy is extinguished, now permits robbers, thieves, fire or some other chance event to come about, so that the temporal goods in which secular clerics preposterously trusted may be taken away; and now, thirdly, he permits dissensions to arise between parties, removing the mighty of the world from the midst, whereby the conjecture of human providence, even in the superiors of the church, is quite often brought to nothing.[^18]
