# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 4, part 2: the union neither causes the humanity nor follows it; the namings of the three days

## ¶1 · 53.28–54.16

> [p. 53] Item vel est dicta unio causa humanitatis vel e contra; cum distinguuntur ut absolutum et respectivum, et non sunt impertinencia. Si humanitas sit causa dicte unionis tanquam¹¹ passionis que consequitur ad subiectum: tunc [p. 54] unio non requiritur ad humanitatem, et e contra pro¹ dicto triduo sunt omnes cause posite, que requiruntur ad humanitatem Christi;¹ et per consequens tunc est homo. Si e contra unio sit causa dicte humanitatis, hoc² videtur tripliciter esse falsum. Primo per³ hoc quod continuacio per idem foret causa corporis; quod improbatum est alibi. 2°. per hoc quod unio illa, cum dependeat ab extremis, non est essenciale principium hominis, eo quod posset manere idem homo variato corpore vel ablata eius parte quantitativa. Non ergo est illa unio causa beatitudinis, sciencie et aliorum⁴ accidencium⁴ hominis que insunt racione anime;. et multo minus est causa⁵ humanitatis, que est prior illis accidentibus. 3°. per hoc quod illa unio nedum dependet ab illis extremis absolute, sed ut copulantur qualitative ad invicem; et per consequens presupponit perfectum hominem: et sic non est previe requisita sed accessoria.

Again, either the said [union]{g:unio} is the cause of [the humanity]{g:humanitas} or the reverse, since they are distinguished as absolute and [relational]{g:respectivus}, and are not [irrelevant]{g:impertinens} [to each other].[^1] If the humanity is the cause of the said union, as of an [attribute]{g:passio} that follows upon its [subject]{g:subiectum}, then the [54] union is not required for the humanity; and, on the other side, for [the said three days]{g:triduum} all the causes are posited that are required for the humanity of Christ;[^2] and in consequence he is then a man. If, conversely, the union is the cause of the said humanity, this seems false in three ways. First, by this: that [continuity]{g:continuacio} would by the same token be the cause of the body, which has been disproved elsewhere. Second, by this: that that union, since it depends on its extremes, is not an essential principle of man, because the same man could remain with his body varied or a [quantitative part]{g:pars-quantitativa} of it taken away. So that union is not the cause of blessedness, of knowledge, and of the other accidents of a man that are in him by reason of the soul; and much less is it the cause of the humanity, which is prior to those accidents. Third, by this: that that union [not only]{g:nedum} depends on those extremes absolutely, but as they are joined qualitatively to each other; and in consequence it presupposes a complete man: and so it is not [required beforehand]{g:previe-requisitum} but accessory.[^3]

## ¶2 · 54.17–54.24

> [p. 54] Tales multas evidencias feci alias cum testimoniis sanctorum, quod tota humanitas et personalitas servatur in anima; aliter enim nimis esset mundus peioratus per deperdicionem absolute humanitatis Christi succedente⁶ loco illius iocalis⁷ preciosissimi sola morte. Oportet ergo plenam perfeccionem humanitatis Christi servari⁸ in composito discretive ex corpore et anima, quod plus sic perficit mundum quam prius perficit qualitative compositum, sicut contingit de Sampsone.

I have elsewhere made many such [evidences]{g:evidencia}, with testimonies of [the saints]{g:sancti}, that the whole humanity and personality is preserved in the soul; for otherwise the world would have been [exceedingly]{g:nimis} worsened by the loss outright of the humanity of Christ, death alone succeeding in the place of that most precious jewel.[^4] Therefore the full perfection of the humanity of Christ must be preserved in the composite made discretely of body and soul, which in this way perfects the world more than the qualitatively composite perfected it before, as happens in the case of Samson.[^5]

## ¶3 · 54.25–55.6

> [p. 54] Ulterius quoad denominacionem Verbi pro illo triduo ex corpore et anima resultantem, videtur responsio multum literalis, quando dicit quod' non placuit doctoribus ipsum denominari ab illis.' Nam semper⁹ Spiritui sancto auctori¹⁰ scripture placuit quod denominetur pro illo triduo' descendens ad inferos'¹¹ secundum animam, et' iacens in sepulcro' secundum corpus: et sic de sepultura et aliis ministeriis¹² ad sepulturam pertinentibus, ut condimento mixture mirre et aloes, involucione in sindone, posicione in monumento. [p. 55] Et racione anime denominatus est' rex spolians tartara,'¹ regens spiritualiter suum exercitum pacificando rebelles tam intraneos quam extraneos:² et sic de omnibus actibus regiminis, que ad regem spiritualem pertinent, longe efficacius quam antea fecit vivus³: tunc enim habuit exercitum plus unitum.

Further, as to the [naming]{g:denominacio} of [the Word]{g:verbum} for those three days resulting from the body and the soul, the reply seems very literal-minded when it says that "it did not please the doctors that he be named from them."[^6] For it has always pleased the Holy Spirit, the author of scripture, that he be named for those three days "descending into hell" as to the soul, and "lying in the sepulchre" as to the body; and so of the burial and the other ministrations belonging to burial, such as the embalming with a mixture of myrrh and aloes, the wrapping in the linen cloth, the laying in the monument.[^7] [55] And by reason of the soul he has been named "king [despoiling]{g:spoliare} Tartarus", ruling his army spiritually by pacifying rebels both within and without; and so of all the acts of rule that belong to a spiritual king, far more effectively than he did before while alive: for then he had a more united army.[^8]

## ¶4 · 55.7–55.16

> [p. 55] Item cum tam corpus Christi quam anima fuerunt secundum Doctorem tunc partes Christi, et nichil dicitur pars, nisi in comparacione ad aliquod totum: videtur quod fuit unum totum continuum vel discretum, cuius erant partes, ut dictum est superius: et illud discretum, cum non deficit in aliquo substanciali quod fuit in humanitate, quando affuit qualitativa composicio: videtur quod tunc,⁴ sicut mediante⁵ illo toto et unione sufficiunt partes ille⁶ denominari partes Christi, sic sufficiunt denominare Christum denominacionibus naturaliter⁷ consequentibus ex eodem.

Again, since both the body of Christ and the soul were, according to the Doctor, then parts of Christ, and nothing is called a part except in comparison to some whole, it seems that there was one whole, continuous or discrete, of which they were the parts, as was said above; and since that discrete [whole] lacks nothing substantial that was in the humanity when the qualitative composition was present, it seems that then, just as by means of that whole and the union those parts suffice to be named parts of Christ, so they suffice to [name]{g:denominare} Christ with the namings that follow naturally from the same.

## ¶5 · 55.17–55.24

> [p. 55] Et quantum ad⁸ exemplum Doctoris de albedine dependente a subiecto ut causante et non substantivante⁹: patet quod ymaginacio¹⁰ est ficta ex falso principio, scilicet quod albedo potest primo¹¹ esse ad omnem¹² punctum subiecti sui informans;¹³ 2°. conservata¹³ ab eo sine hoc, quod informat; et 3°. nec informans nec conservata ab eodem. Albedo enim, cum sit subiectum esse album, non potest esse sine subiecto.

And as to the Doctor's example of whiteness depending on its subject as causing and not as substantiving [it]: it is clear that the imagined case is feigned from a false principle, namely that whiteness can, first, be informing its subject at every point; second, be conserved by it without this, that it informs; and third, be neither informing nor conserved by the same.[^9] For whiteness, since it is the subject's being white, cannot be without a subject.[^10]

## ¶6 · 55.25–56.12

> [p. 55] Item de hoc quod dicit Christum posse denominari ab illis partibus ut sic' Si essent nomina imposita;' miror de istis verbis tam realis philosophi et subtilis, cum constat¹⁴ quod denominacio rei extra est impertinens imposicioni¹⁵ nostrorum nominum, ut albedo eque denominaret¹⁶ simplum¹⁷ album, nullo signo artificiali hominum¹⁸ existente; sicut quotlibet talibus positis secundum varia ydiomata. Unde denominaciones, quas Augustinus, Hugo de Sancto Victore, [p. 56] et alii precedentes Magistrum Sentenciarum imposuerunt Verbo Dei secundum illas¹ partes pro illo triduo, scilicet quod est caro, quod est anima, et sic de ceteris, non sonuerunt in vicium. Nec valet dicere quod ille denominaciones sunt synecdochice²; quia, ut patet alibi, construccio synecdochica³ est, quando parti quantitative attribuitur primo denominacio accidentalis, et consequentur toti secundum illam partem ut ;—homo est⁴ albus faciem et sanus thoracem.⁵ Cum ergo nec corpus nec anima Christi sit pars eius quantitativa; sequitur quod hic non est construccio synecdochica—Christus⁶ est anima—sicut nec—Hic homo est tibia.

Again, as to his saying that Christ can be named from those parts in this way, "if names were imposed": I wonder at these words of so realist and subtle a philosopher, since it is evident that the naming of a thing outside [the mind] is irrelevant to the imposition of our names, as whiteness would equally name the simple [thing] white with no artificial sign of men existing, just as with any number of such [signs] posited according to various languages.[^11] Hence the namings which Augustine, Hugh of St Victor, [56] and others before [the Master]{g:magister} of the *Sentences* imposed on the Word of God as to those parts for those three days, namely that he is flesh, that he is soul, and so of the rest, did not sound amiss.[^12] Nor is it valid to say that those namings are synecdochic; for, as is clear elsewhere, a [synecdochic construction]{g:construccio-synecdochica} is when an accidental naming is attributed first to a quantitative part, and consequently to the whole as to that part, as "a man is white as to his face and healthy as to his chest."[^13] Since, therefore, neither the body nor the soul of Christ is a quantitative part of him, [it follows]{g:sequitur} that here, "Christ is soul", there is no synecdochic construction, as neither in "This man is a shin."

## ¶7 · 56.13–57.6

> [p. 56] Item si pro dicto triduo corpus et anima sunt partes Christi, tunc denominant⁷ ipsum habere partes illas tanquam partes suas: quia constat quod non sequitur —Hec⁸⁹ res est mea et est pars; ergo est pars mea—quia quelibet pars creature esset pars Trinitatis increate; et, ut sophiste¹⁰ arguunt, homo¹¹ monstruose haberet multa capita asinina. Ideo concedit doctor¹² quod Christus potest vere denominari pro illo triduo habens partes illas ut partes suas, sed non carneus vel carnalis: ut si solum¹³ assumpsisset¹⁴ quantitatem, adhuc¹⁵ non¹⁶ esset quantus propter defectum subiecti materialis: sic licet habeat carneam partem suam, adhuc¹⁷ non est carneus, eo quod deest humanitas. Sed miror de ista similitudine propter tria. Primo cum impossibile sit quantitatem per se esse. 2°. cum hoc posito per impossibile Verbum esset quantum per ypostaticam unionem. Non enim est unio vel assumpcio personalis sine communicacione ydiomatum nature specifice assumpte¹⁸ Verbo vel persone assumenti: assumptum enim ypostatice oportet terminari suppositacione¹⁹ aliena et non esse suppositum proprie speciei, [p. 57] ut eciam¹ dicit doctor. 3°. videtur similitudo² impertinens; cum natura illa non dependet ab humanitate, cum manet naturaliter sine illa, et posset fuisse assumpta sine assumpcione humanitatis. Si enim Verbum posset pure assumere quantitatem, multo evidencius carnem mortuam per se de natura possibilem.

Again, if for the said three days the body and soul are parts of Christ, then they name him as having those parts as his parts: for it is evident that it does not follow, "This thing is mine and is a part; therefore it is a part of mine", since [then] any part of a [creature]{g:creatura} would be a part of the uncreated Trinity; and, as [the sophists]{g:sophista} argue, a man would monstrously have many asses' heads.[^14] Therefore the Doctor grants that Christ can truly be named for those three days "having those parts as his parts", but not "[fleshy]{g:carneus}" or "[carnal]{g:carnalis}": as, if he had [assumed]{g:assumere} quantity alone, he would still not be [quantified]{g:quantus}, for lack of a material subject; so, although he has his fleshy part, he is still not fleshy, because the humanity is lacking.[^15] But I wonder at that comparison, for three reasons. First, since it is impossible for quantity to be by itself. Second, since, this being posited *per impossibile*, the Word would be quantified by the hypostatic union. For there is no personal union or assumption without the [communication of idioms]{g:communicacio-ydiomatum} of the assumed [nature of the species]{g:natura-specifica} to the Word, or to the [person]{g:persona} assuming: for what is assumed [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} must be terminated by an alien [suppositation]{g:suppositacio}, and not be a [supposit]{g:suppositum} of its own species, [57] as the Doctor also says.[^16] Third, the comparison seems irrelevant, since that [nature]{g:natura} does not depend on the humanity, since it remains naturally without it, and could have been assumed without the assumption of the humanity. For if the Word could purely assume quantity, much more evidently [could he assume] dead flesh, which is possible by itself by nature.[^17]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** 53.29–30 *cum distinguuntur ... et non sunt impertinencia*: the disjunction is exhaustive because the humanity (absolute) and the union (relational) are distinct and bear on each other. *Et e contra* (54.1) is taken as "and, on the other side", the second half of the first case; the converse case begins at 54.3 (*Si e contra*).
[^2]: **T.** 54.1–3 *pro dicto triduo ... humanitatem Christi*: omitted by A B C; the print follows O and is rendered. 54.10–11 *aliorum accidencium*: A B C *rerum accedencium*, "of the things that accrue"; the print is rendered.
[^3]: **C.** 54.13–16: *qualitative* "qualitatively", as body and soul are the qualitative parts that make up a man. *Accessoria* "accessory", supervening on the complete man, against *previe requisita*.
[^4]: **T.** 54.20 *iocalis* "jewel", the print (O): A B C *vitalis*, "that most precious vital [thing]"; O *sic sedente* for *succedente*. 54.22 *servari*: A B C *servare*. The print is rendered.
[^5]: **S.** Judges 16:30 (Harris's margin): "and he killed many more at his death, than he had killed before in his life." So the body and soul, joined discretely after death, perfect the world more than the living composite did.
[^6]: **S.** The reply and the words quoted here and at 55.26 are the Doctor's: Duns Scotus (the Subtle Doctor of 49.24; "so realist and subtle a philosopher", 55.27), *Ordinatio* III d. 22 q. un., ad 3 (Wadding VII/1, 454–455, the pages Harris's notes give): *non placuit Doctoribus vt denominaretur ab illis*, the examples of whiteness and of quantity, and *Dico quod sic, si essent nomina imposita … Ita Christus in triduo, vel Verbum, potuit dici habere animam et corpus* (*verified*, LEDGER I04b-1). *Multum literalis*: "very literal-minded", keeping to the letter of the doctors. 54.28 *semper* is omitted by A B C.
[^7]: **S.** The creed's "descended into hell"; the burial: John 19:39–40, Matt. 27:59–60. The two quoted titles are not scripture's wording; their source is untraced (not in the PL, nor in Harris's notes; perhaps liturgical; LEDGER I04b-2). 54.31 *ministeriis*: O *misteriis*, "mysteries".
[^8]: **T.** 55.5 *vivus*: A B C omit it; O has *vn⁹*, which Harris reads as *vivus*. The print is rendered. "King despoiling Tartarus" is a quoted title, its source untraced (LEDGER I04b-2). 55.3 A B C *externos*.
[^9]: **T.** 55.18 *substantivante*: O M *subiectante*, "as being its subject"; O adds *secundum* at 55.17, "the Doctor's second example". 55.19 O *ymago* for *ymaginacio*. The print is rendered.
[^10]: **C.** 55.23 *cum sit subiectum esse album*: "the subject's being white": the accident is the subject's being so, and so cannot stand apart from it; *esse* is the infinitive taken as a noun, not existence.
[^11]: **T/C.** 55.29 *simplum*, the print (O): A B C *ipsum*, "would equally name it white"; 55.27 A B C *cum stat*. *Rei extra*: a thing outside the mind; *ydiomata* (55.31) are here languages, not the idioms of the communication of idioms.
[^12]: **S.** Augustine and Hugh of St Victor (*De sacramentis* II.1.11), writers before the Master, on the Word called "flesh" and "soul" in the three days: Augustine, *In Ioh.* 78.3, PL 35, 1836, *ac per hoc Christus est Deus anima rationalis et caro*; Hugh, PL 176, 401A–B and 407D, *Christus persona in sepulcro jacuit … secundum solam carnem* and *Ergo, inquis, homo est anima? Quidni?* (*verified*, LEDGER I04b-3). The Lombard (*Sent.* III d. 22 c. 2, PL 192, 803) quotes only Augustine's tractate 78 and does not name Hugh.
[^13]: **T.** 56.8–9 *sanus thoracem*: the print's reading stands in no manuscript: A B C *serenus thoracem*, O *sanus secundum thoracem*. The English, "healthy as to his chest", follows O, which differs from the print only by the explicit *secundum*. 56.8 *est*: A by a second hand, omitted by B.
[^14]: **C.** 56.15–16: what is mine and is a part is not thereby a part of me; so the parts must be Christ's by naming, not by that inference. The asses' heads are the sophism of the man who owns an ass. 56.17 A B C *sophistice*, "as they argue sophistically".
[^15]: **C.** 56.21–24 *carneus vel carnalis*: "fleshy" names from the matter, "carnal" savours of vice (as 50.28–31). *Quantus* "quantified", having quantity. 56.22 O omits *non*, against the sense.
[^16]: **C.** 56.28–31: the idioms of the assumed nature are communicated to the Word or the assuming person. *Assumptum* is here the assumed nature, not the logician's "assumption"; *suppositacione aliena* "by an alien suppositation": it stands in another's supposit (A B C *supposicione*, "supposition").
[^17]: **C.** 57.4–6 *multo evidencius carnem mortuam*: elliptical; "[could he assume]" is supplied from *posset ... assumere*. *Per se de natura possibilem*: dead flesh can by nature be by itself, as quantity cannot.
