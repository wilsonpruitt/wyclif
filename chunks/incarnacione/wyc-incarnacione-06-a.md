# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 6, part 1: that Christ is a man univocally with other men

## ¶1 · 85.0–85.0

<!-- the editor's bracketed argument at the chapter head: a Latin summary, then his own English
     summary in the same bracket (see the volume CARD for what is translated) -->

> [p. 85] [Ostendit quod Christus sit univoce homo cum aliis hominibus; et respondendo obiectibus declarat quod Christus est, secundum multorum doctorum testimonia, creatura. This chapter (A) shows that Christ is a man literally and in the same sense as other men are men; and by the discussion of objections to this statement (B) illustrates further the thesis of Chap. I., that Christ is a creature, in accordance with the testimony of many weighty authorities.]

[It shows that Christ is [univocally]{g:univoce} a man with other men; and, by answering objections, it [makes clear]{g:declarare} that Christ is, according to the testimonies of many doctors, a [creature]{g:creatura}.]

## ¶2 · 85.2–85.13

> [p. 85] Ex istis videtur consequi quod Christus sit univoce homo cum quolibet fratre suo. Probatur tripliciter. Primo sic. Christus est anima eiusdem racionis cum nostra, sicut et corpus eiusdem specifice racionis, que eciam univoce copulatur; ergo est univoce completa humanitas, et sic homo. Non enim est possibile aliquid complete principiari ex principiis omnino similibus cum adequatis principiis, ex quibus principiatur aliquid, nisi conveniat in natura. Cum ergo humanitas Christi et cuiuscunque alterius hominis sic se respiciunt, sequitur quod sunt idem in specie. Aliter enim Christus non esset pocius homo quam ydolum vel ymago; homo enim solum equivoce non est homo.

From these things it seems to follow that Christ is univocally a man with each of his brothers. This is proved in three ways. First thus. Christ is a soul of the same kind as ours, as [he is] also a body of the same specific kind, which also is univocally joined;[^1] therefore he is univocally a complete [humanity]{g:humanitas}, and so a man. For it is not possible that anything be completely constituted from principles wholly like the adequate principles from which something [else] is constituted, unless it agrees [with it] in [nature]{g:natura}. Since, therefore, the humanity of Christ and that of any other man stand so to one another, [it follows]{g:sequitur} that they are the same in species. For otherwise Christ would no more be a man than an idol or an image; for what is a man only [equivocally]{g:equivoce} is not a man.

## ¶3 · 85.14–86.15

> [p. 85] Confirmatur primo ex hoc, quod nec corpus nec anima, cum sit pars qualitativa suppositi, est¹ per se in specie, sed ipsum suppositum, ad cuius² speciem partes³ huiusmodi reducuntur. Cum ergo Christus secundum deitatem non sit in specie, nec humanitas eius est per se in specie, ut dicunt negantes ipsam esse suppositum; relinquitur quod Christus sit suppositum per se in specie. Sed in qua, rogo, si non fuerit⁴ in humana.⁴ Nec audebit catholicus dicere quod corpus et anima Christi erant solum fantastica non consimilis speciei cum partibus aliorum hominum. Quando autem [p. 86] communicatur cum hominibus ponentibus—‘omnia universalia esse signa,’—oportet aliter loqui: ut posito, quod¹ omnis natura¹ humana sit assumpta² a Verbo, et ipsum proferat voces et³ scripta talia—‘homo est animal racionale’—et⁴ correspondenter eliciat conceptus ut nos facimus; tunc sicut non exinde deficeret species humana ex parte rei, sic⁵ forent talia signa univoca vera ut prius, posito quod isti conceptus maneant post assumpciones significantes⁶ naturaliter et univoce sicut ante. Aliter enim, solo Christo existente de specie humana, vere sciret ad sensum⁷ univocum priorem nedum quod nemo est Deus, sed quod nemo est animal; cum species non esset, deficiente quocunque eius supposito.⁸ Quod videtur mihi insanum dicere, cum Christus maxime perficeret genus suum; et per consequens si omnis homo sit Deus, non exinde tollitur species hominis.

This is confirmed, first, from the fact that neither the body nor the soul, since [each] is a [qualitative part]{g:pars-qualitativa} of the [supposit]{g:suppositum}, is [of itself]{g:per-se} in a species, but the supposit itself, to whose species parts of this kind are reduced. Since, therefore, Christ [as to the Godhead]{g:secundum-deitatem} is not in a species, and his humanity is not of itself in a species, as those say who deny "it is a supposit", it remains that Christ is a supposit of itself in a species. But in which, I ask, if not in the human? Nor will a catholic dare to say that the body and soul of Christ were only [imaginary]{g:fantasticus}, not of a like species with the parts of other men. But when one converses [86] with men who posit "all universals are signs", one must speak otherwise: for instance, supposing that every human nature were [assumed]{g:assumere} by [the Word]{g:verbum}, and he uttered such spoken and written words as "man is a rational animal", and correspondingly elicited concepts as we do; then, just as the human species would not on that account fail on the side of the thing, so such univocal signs would be true as before, supposing that those concepts remain after the assumptions, signifying naturally and univocally as before. For otherwise, with Christ alone existing of the human species, he would truly know, in the former univocal sense, [not only]{g:nedum} "no man is God" but "no man is an animal";[^2] since the species would not be, when any supposit of it whatever was lacking. Which seems to me mad to say, since Christ would most of all perfect his genus; and consequently, if every man be God, the species of man is not on that account taken away.

## ¶4 · 86.16–86.29

> [p. 86] 2°. confirmatur ex hoc quod Christus est formaliter homo humanitate consimilis racionis cum humanitate alterius: ergo est univoce homo cum alio. Patet consequencia eo quod, quantumcunque⁹ subiecta variantur, dummodo¹⁰ forme, a quibus recipiunt predicaciones huiusmodi, sunt consimilis racionis, predicaciones sunt univoce; ut, quantumcunque subiecta¹¹ corpora¹¹ variantur, dummodo habent per totum albedines consimilis racionis, sunt alba univoce.¹² Quod autem humanitates¹³ Christi et aliorum essencialiter conveniunt exinde evidet, quod dimissa humanitate foret illa consimilis racionis cum alia; ergo cum unio sit sibi accidentalis, eo quod potest¹⁴ manere eadem essencia denudata;¹⁵ sequitur quod stante unione erit eiusdem racionis substancialis¹⁶ cum alia.

Second, this is confirmed from the fact that Christ is formally a man by a humanity of like kind with the humanity of another; therefore he is univocally a man with another. The [inference]{g:consequencia} is clear, because, however much the [subjects]{g:subiectum} vary, provided the forms from which they receive predications of this kind are of like kind, the [predications]{g:predicacio} are univocal: as, however much the subject bodies vary, provided they have whitenesses of like kind throughout, they are white univocally. Now that the humanities of Christ and of others agree essentially is evident from this, that, the humanity [being laid aside]{g:dimittere}, it would be of like kind with another; therefore, since the [union]{g:unio} is accidental to it, because it can remain the same [essence]{g:essencia} stripped [of it], it follows that while the union stands it will be of the same substantial kind with another.[^3]

## ¶5 · 86.30–87.13

> [p. 86] 3°. confirmatur ex hoc quod Christus est homo, et non Pater neque¹⁷ Donum; quod non esset, nisi Christus plus conveniret¹⁸ cum homine quam Pater; ergo est maior conveniencia [p. 87] Christi ad hominem quam Patris ad hominem, vel quam ante assumpcionem fuerat. Et cum non¹ sit pertinens conveniencia nisi vel in genere vel in specie, sequitur¹ quod Christus factus homo convenit in genere cum aliis hominibus, et per idem in specie;² quia aliter non foret humanitate nobis similior quam perante, cum conveniencia presupponit unionem.³ Noto ergo gradu⁴ similitudinis⁵ vel conveniencie, tunc, cum omnis conveniencia accidentalis presupponit convenienciam substancie generalem, oportet quod secundum humanitatem, qua factus est nobis similis, conveniat nobiscum in⁶ genere vel in⁶ specie, cum conveniencia in humanitate sit alterius racionis⁷ quam conveniencia in aliquo accidente⁷ novem⁸ generum.

Third, this is confirmed from the fact that Christ is man, and not the Father or [the Gift]{g:donum}; which would not be so unless Christ agreed more with man than the Father [does]; therefore there is a greater agreement [87] of Christ with man than of the Father with man, or than there had been before the assumption. And since there is no pertinent agreement except either in genus or in species, it follows that Christ, made man, agrees in genus with other men, and by the same token in species; for otherwise he would not be more like us by the humanity than before, since agreement presupposes union.[^4] The degree of likeness or agreement being noted, therefore, then, since every accidental agreement presupposes a general agreement of substance, he must, as to the humanity, by which he was made like us, agree with us in genus or in species; since agreement in humanity is of another kind than agreement in some accident of the nine genera.

## ¶6 · 87.14–88.2

> [p. 87] Item omne suppositum speciei humane⁹ est homo univoce; Christus est suppositum speciei humane,⁹ cum sit filius hominis; ergo est homo univoce. Maior patet ex hoc, quod omne tale suppositum est illa species, et per consequens quiditas vel racio speciei. Et sic eadem est communis racio illi individuo et cuilibet¹⁰ eidem sibi¹⁰ in specie. Cum ergo illa descriptive dicuntur univoce predicata; quibus competit eadem racio predicati, patet quod non potest esse individuum speciei humane nisi sit homo univoce; aliter enim individuum esset homo, et non diffinicio vel quiditas hominis; et hoc esset ponere hominem non esse hominem. Minor¹¹ autem argumenti patet ex fide. Nam secundum Apostolum ed Hebre. 2. ‘nusquam angelos apprehendit, sed semen Abrahe ut fieret per omnia fratribus consimilis¹²;’ quia tam secundum naturam corpoream quam¹³ incorpoream,¹³ et illa sunt omnia. Et consequenter¹⁴ similatus est fratribus suis secundum omnia accidencia positiva¹⁵ naturaliter consequencia¹⁶ has naturas. Aliter enim non esset ‘mediator [p. 88] Dei et hominum homo Christus,’¹ ut dicit Apostolus prima ad² Thimo. 2.

Again, every supposit of the human species is univocally man; Christ is a supposit of the human species, since he is the son of man; therefore he is univocally man. The major is clear from this, that every such supposit is that species, and consequently the [quiddity]{g:quiditas} or account of the species. And so the account common to that individual and to each that is the same as it in species is the same. Since, therefore, those things are by definition said to be univocally predicated to which the same account of the predicate belongs,[^5] it is clear that there cannot be an [individual]{g:individuum} of the human species unless it is univocally man; for otherwise the individual would be man, and not the [definition]{g:diffinicio} or quiddity of man; and this would be to posit "man is not man". Now the minor of the argument is clear [from faith]{g:ex-fide}. For according to the Apostle, Hebrews 2, "nowhere doth he take hold of the angels, but of the seed of Abraham, that he might be made in all things like unto his brethren";[^6] for [he was made like them] both as to the bodily nature and as to the incorporeal, and those are all. And consequently he was made like his brothers as to all the positive accidents that naturally follow on these natures. For otherwise he would not be "mediator [88] of God and men, the man Christ",[^7] as the Apostle says, 1 Timothy 2.

## ¶7 · 88.3–88.28

> [p. 88] Confirmatur primo ex testimonio Augustini. Nam in De Vera Religione 27; ‘Ipsa, inquit, natura suscipienda erat que liberanda.’ Et 2°. De Visitacione Infirmorum; ‘Homo Deus participatus est nostre³ humanitatis ut nos participemur sue divinitatis;’ et sequitur; ‘quod tu es, fieri dignatus est.’ Et ad idem vadit processus Anselmi in libro suo Cur Deus Homo; et De Incarnacione Verbi; ‘quod aliter non fuisset competens satisfaccio pro peccato primi hominis, nisi individuum eiusdem nature, et sic idem in specie, solveret debitum, quod natura debuit creatori⁴ suo.’ Nec memini me legisse in scripturis alicuius doctoris famosi oppositum. Unde Lincolniensis⁵ in suo Exameron dicit quod Christus est homo eiusdem racionis cum⁶ aliis. Et idem dicit sanctus Thomas et omnes ponentes universalia ex parte rei. Sed et multi non ponentes universalia preter signa; quia multi philosophi, sive fideles sive infideles, conversantes cum Christo et ipsum sencientes, ut apostoli,⁷ haberent conceptum specificum in anima, qui signaret illis⁸ univoce illum cum aliis; quia aliter fuisset Christus illusor maximus, qui ad hoc ostendit eius humanitatem humanis sensibus, et verbo docuit ipsum esse verum hominem. Philosophi ergo habentes terminos vel conceptus specificos, quos vere predicarent de pronominibus demonstrantibus⁹ recto Christum¹⁰ cum aliis, haberent¹⁰ conceptus univocos Christo et aliis: et hoc sufficit ad univocacionem ac ydemptitatem specificam iuxta sic loquentes. Ergo conclusio.

This is confirmed, first, from the testimony of Augustine. For in *On True Religion* 27: "That very nature," he says, "was to be [taken up]{g:suscipere} which was to be set free."[^8] And *On the Visitation of the Sick* 2: "The man-God partook of our humanity, that we might partake of his [divinity]{g:divinitas}"; and it follows: "what thou art, he deigned to become."[^9] And to the same end goes the [argument]{g:processus} of Anselm in his book *Cur Deus homo*, and *On the Incarnation of the Word*: "that otherwise there would not have been fitting [satisfaction]{g:satisfaccio} for the sin of the first man, unless an individual of the same nature, and so the same in species, paid the debt which the nature owed to its creator."[^10] Nor do I remember having read the opposite in the writings of any famous doctor. Hence [Lincoln]{g:lincolniensis}, in his *Hexaemeron*, says that Christ is a man of the same kind with others.[^11] And the same is said by Saint Thomas and by all who posit universals on the side of the thing.[^12] But many also who posit no universals besides signs [say it]; for many philosophers, whether faithful or [unbelievers]{g:infidelis}, conversing with Christ and perceiving him, as the apostles [did], would have a specific concept in the soul which would signify to them him univocally with others; for otherwise Christ would have been the greatest [deceiver]{g:illusor}, who to this end showed his humanity to human senses, and taught by word that he was a true man. Philosophers, therefore, having specific terms or concepts which they would truly predicate of pronouns pointing out, [in the direct case]{g:in-recto}, Christ with others,[^13] would have concepts univocal to Christ and others; and this suffices for [univocation]{g:univocacio} and [identity of species]{g:ydemptitas-specifica} according to those who speak so. Therefore the [conclusion]{g:conclusio}.

## ¶8 · 88.29–89.10

> [p. 88] Confirmatur tripliciter. Primo ex hoc quod peccatum est illudere communicantes per verba vel signa sophistica; sed sic fecisset Christus potissime, si non habuisset aliquam denominacionem univocam cum aliqua creatura; ergo peccasset maxime. Pro assumpto dicitur Ecclesiastici 37. ‘Qui [p. 89] sophistice loquitur, odibilis est.’ Et Enchiridion 14. ‘Verbis uti ad fallaciam, non ad quod instituta sunt, peccatum est.’ Christus autem sepe¹ dixit se esse filium hominis et naturaliter ostendit se esse accidentatum² multipliciter; necnon et² passionem et mortem et resurreccionem predixit Apostolis ut patet Matth. 16, unde Petro dicenti sibi, ‘Absit hoc a te, Domine;’ respondit Veritas; ‘Vade post me, Sathana: non sapis ea, que Dei sunt.’ Et tamen eandem sentenciam Petri dicunt modo scolastici. Sequitur quod vel Christus fuit falsissimus³ vel illi sunt racionabiliter arguendi.³

This is confirmed in three ways. First from this, that it is a sin to deceive those with whom one communicates by sophistical words or signs; now Christ would have done so most of all, if he had not had some univocal [naming]{g:denominacio} with some creature; therefore he would have sinned most greatly. For [the assumption]{g:assumptum} it is said, Ecclesiasticus 37: "He that [89] speaketh [sophistically]{g:sophistice}, is hateful."[^14] And *Enchiridion* 14: "To use words for [fallacy]{g:fallacia}, not for what they were instituted for, is a sin."[^15] Now Christ often said that he was the son of man, and naturally showed himself to be [subject to accidents]{g:accidentatus} in many ways; and he also foretold his passion and death and resurrection to the apostles, as is clear in Matthew 16; hence, to Peter saying to him, "Be this far from thee, Lord," the Truth answered: "Go behind me, Satan: thou savourest not the things that are of God."[^16] And yet the schoolmen now say the same [teaching]{g:sentencia} as Peter. It follows that either Christ was most false or those men are with reason to be reproved.[^17]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Christus est anima … sicut et corpus*: bare predication, kept: Christ is a soul, and a body, of one kind with ours. *Eiusdem racionis* "of the same kind": *racio* is the "account" of Aristotle's univocals, so rendered where it stands alone (p. 87).
[^2]: **C.** *sciret* has no expressed subject: taken as the Word of the case just put ("he"); it may be impersonal, "one would know". *Nemo* "no man", as the argument needs.
[^3]: **C.** *dimissa humanitate … foret illa*: the ablative absolute and *illa* name the same humanity, laid aside by the Word. O omits *denudata* (with a gap) and *substancialis*.
[^4]: **T.** *unionem*, the print with O; A B C read *univocacionem* "univocation", on the word the step turns on. The print is kept.
[^5]: **S.** *descriptive* "by definition" (cf. *descripcio*): the definition of univocals, things that share both the name and the same account (Aristotle, *Categories* 1). To be checked (LEDGER I06a-1).
[^6]: **S.** Heb. 2:16–17, joined: Vulgate *sed semen Abrahae apprehendit. Unde debuit per omnia fratribus similari*; Wyclif's *ut fieret … consimilis* (A B C *similis*) is translated, the Douay–Rheims wording kept where his Latin agrees.
[^7]: **S.** 1 Tim. 2:5 (Douay–Rheims), without *Iesus* (O adds it).
[^8]: **S.** Augustine, *De vera religione*, Wyclif's "27". To be checked (LEDGER I06a-2).
[^9]: **S.** The pseudo-Augustinian *De visitatione infirmorum* II. To be checked (LEDGER I06a-3).
[^10]: **S.** A summary of Anselm's argument (*Cur Deus homo* II.6–8; *Epistola de incarnatione Verbi*), not a quotation, though the print sets it in quotation marks. To be checked (LEDGER I06a-4). A B C read *Deo* for *creatori*.
[^11]: **S.** Robert Grosseteste, bishop of Lincoln, *Hexaemeron*; the place to be checked (LEDGER I06a-5).
[^12]: **S.** Thomas Aquinas; no place given (LEDGER I06a-6).
[^13]: **T.** *in recto*, with A B O, against the print's *recto*: pronouns that point to Christ in the direct (nominative) case, as against *in obliquo*.
[^14]: **S.** Ecclus. 37:23 (Douay–Rheims).
[^15]: **S.** Augustine, *Enchiridion* 22 (c. 7) in the modern division, Wyclif's "14"; *fallacia* here is deception. To be checked (LEDGER I06a-7).
[^16]: **S.** Matt. 16:22–23 (Douay–Rheims), shortened: Wyclif adds *hoc* (Vulgate *Absit a te, Domine*) and omits *scandalum es mihi: quia*.
[^17]: **C.** *arguendi*: gerundive, "to be reproved" (*arguere* in the biblical sense), or "to be argued against". O reads only *falsum vel arguendi*.
