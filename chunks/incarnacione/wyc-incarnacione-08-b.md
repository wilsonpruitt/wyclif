# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 8, part 2: that the definitions of man fit Christ univocally

## ¶1 · 127.5–128.8

> [p. 127] Et quoad diffiniciones hominum dico quod nunquam inventa est a philosophis aliqua probabilis diffinicio sive descripcio hominis, quin illa univoce conveniat Iesu nostro. Est enim animal racionale mortale ante passionem, sicut veteres describebant hominem integrum⁴ secundum noticiam imperfectam, quam de ipso habuerant,⁵ quos⁴ sequitur Augustinus in De Qualitate⁶ Anime ca°.⁷: est iterum racionalis substancia ex corpore et anima composita tam post resurreccionem quam ante; sicut Augustinus describit hominem perfectum 15°. De Trinitate ca°. 7°.: est tercio anima racionalis habens corpus ab instanti sue concepcionis et ulterius in eternum. Et sic loquitur Augustinus de homine ut⁸ ad personam attinet, Ome. 19,⁹ exponens illud Ioh. 5. ‘Potestatem dedit ei iudicium facere, quia filius hominis est.’ ‘Ex virgine, inquit, Maria homo factus est filius hominis; sed hanc potestatem temporalem faciendi iudicium non haberet, nisi acciperet¹⁰ et esset homo prius sine hac potestate. Sed ipse filius hominis, qui et Filius Dei: herendo¹¹ enim ad unitatem persone filius hominis¹² Filius¹² Dei facta est una persona eadem Filius Dei et filius hominis. Quid autem propter¹³ quid habeat,¹³ dinoscendum est. Filius hominis habet animam, habet corpus: Filius Dei, quod est Verbum Dei, habet animam,¹⁴ tanquam anima habens corpus; et sicut anima habens corpus non facit duas personas sed unum hominem, sic¹⁵ Verbum habens hominem¹⁵ non facit duas personas sed unum Christum.’ Et sequitur. ‘Quid est homo? anima racionalis habens [p. 128] corpus. Quid est Christus? Verbum Dei habens hominem.’ Ex istis¹ patet quod supradicta sentencia est¹ beati Augustini et Hugonis de Sancto Victore cum aliis; scilicet, quod omnis homo est anima vel spiritus habens corpus. Et credo quod non est possibile hominem completum esse, nisi habeat tempore suo corpus: quia nisi assit corpus organicum, anima non creatur. Unde et Christus habuit corpus in triduo.

And as to the definitions of man, I say that the philosophers never found any reputable [definition]{g:diffinicio} or [definition]{g:descripcio}[^1] of man that does not belong [univocally]{g:univoce} to our Jesus. For he is a rational mortal animal before the passion, as the ancients defined the whole man according to the imperfect knowledge which they had had of him, and Augustine follows them in *De quantitate animae*, chapter —.[^2] Again, he is a rational substance composed of body and soul, after the resurrection as much as before; as Augustine defines the perfect man in *De Trinitate* 15, chapter 7.[^3] Third, he is a rational soul having a body from the instant of its conception and onward for ever. And so Augustine speaks of man as it pertains to the [person]{g:persona}, Homily 19, expounding that of John 5:27: "He hath given him power to do judgment, because he is the Son of man." "Of the virgin Mary," he says, "the Son of man was made man; but he would not have this temporal power of doing judgment unless he received it and were first a man without this power. But he himself is the Son of man who is also the Son of God: for by the Son of man's cleaving to the Son of God in unity of person,[^4] one and the same person was made Son of God and Son of man. But what he has on account of what must be discerned. The Son of man has a soul, has a body: the Son of God, who is the [Word]{g:verbum} of God, has a soul,[^5] as the soul has a body; and as the soul having a body does not make two persons but one man, so the Word having man does not make two persons but one Christ." And it goes on: "What is man? A rational soul having a [128] body. What is Christ? The Word of God having man." From these it is clear that the [teaching]{g:sentencia} given above is that of blessed Augustine and of Hugh of St Victor with others;[^6] namely, that every man is a soul or spirit having a body. And I believe that it is not possible for a man to be complete unless he has a body in his time: for unless an organic body is present, the soul is not created. Hence Christ too had a body in [the three days]{g:triduum}.

## ¶2 · 128.9–129.12

> [p. 128] Quod si 4°. volumus² describere hominem in sua communitate maxima, patet quod Christus fuit univoce homo cum aliis: cum hec descripcio hominis communissima sit superior ad descripcionem triplicem supradictam. Pro quo notandus est Lincolniensis in suo Exameron de opere sexte diei, qui post exposicionem huius—‘Faciamus³ hominem ad ymaginem et similitudinem nostram’—sic³ scribit: ‘Quid autem sit homo, ex hic cognitis et auditis diffiniamus: non⁴ enim egemus mutuari⁴ diffiniciones alienas. ‘Homo, inquit, est creatura racionalis, facta ad ymaginem conditoris.’⁵ Unde exponens illud Gen. 2°. ‘spiravit in faciem eius spiraculum vite,’ ita scribit: ‘Deus verbo⁶ beneplaciti⁷ spiraculum vite id est, animam racionalem formavit, et⁷ in unitatem persone corpori formando infudit.’ Et sequitur: ‘per hoc, quod “homo factus est in animam viventem,” notatur quod homo interior est homo, et corrupto homine exteriori nichilo minus manet veritas et personalitas uniuscuiusque hominis in subsistencia hominis interioris; quod ex verbis Domini satis insinuatur, cum dicit Matt. 22°. “Ego sum Deus Abraham, Deus Ysaac, et Deus Iacob: non est Deus mortuorum sed vivencium.”’ Hec Lincolniensis. Quamvis enim Christus sit Deus mortuorum prima morte, non tamen est Deus obiective beatificans mortuos secunda morte. Et hoc sonat theos in uno sensu. Cum autem Deus tempore Moysi particulariter dixit se esse Deum illorum patriarcharum [p. 129] non solum pro lapso tempore cum hodie non daret Moysi evidenciam ad credendum Deum tunc velle iuvare semen eorum; non mirum si isto argumento Salvator imposuit silencium Saduceis de negacione resurreccionis mortuorum. Cum enim ipsi perpetuo vixerant in spiritu, nec desiderium naturale ad naturam corpoream extingui poterat¹; sequitur quod sancti patriarche non ab hoc perpetuo finaliter sint frustrandi, licet secundum quid mortui sint ad tempus. Alii autem presciti mortui sunt simpliciter morte perpetua; et ista est exposicio beati Augustini Ome. 43. super illud Ioh. 8: ‘Si quis sermonem meum servaverit, mortem non gustabit in eternum.’

But if, fourth, we wish to define man in his widest commonness, it is clear that Christ was univocally man with the others: since this most common definition of man is superior to the threefold definition given above. For this, [Lincoln]{g:lincolniensis} is to be noted in his *Hexaemeron*, on the work of the sixth day, who after the exposition of this — "Let us make man to our image and likeness" (Gen 1:26) — writes thus: "But what man is, let us define from what has been learned and heard here: for we have no need to borrow alien definitions. 'Man,' he says, 'is a rational [creature]{g:creatura}, made to the image of the creator.'" Hence, expounding that of Gen 2:7, "breathed into his face the breath of life", he writes thus: "God by the word of his good pleasure formed the breath of life, that is, the rational soul, and poured it into the body he was forming, into unity of person." And it goes on: "By this, that 'man became a living soul', it is signified that the inner man is man, and when the outer man is corrupted the truth and personality of each man nonetheless remains in the subsistence of the inner man; which is sufficiently implied by the words of the Lord when he says, Matt 22:32, 'I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob: he is not the God of the dead but of the living.'" Thus Lincoln.[^7] For although Christ is God of the dead by the first death, he is not God objectively beatifying the dead by the second death. And this is what *theos* means in one sense. And since God in the time of Moses said specifically that he was the God of those patriarchs [129] not only for time past — since [so] he would not that day give Moses [evidence]{g:evidencia} for believing that God then willed to help their seed —, no wonder if by this argument the Saviour imposed silence on the Sadducees about their denial of the resurrection of the dead.[^8] For since they lived on perpetually in spirit, and the natural desire for bodily nature could not be extinguished, it follows that the holy patriarchs are not to be frustrated of this finally for ever, although they are dead [in a certain respect]{g:secundum-quid} for a time. But the others, [the foreknown]{g:prescitus},[^9] are dead [without qualification]{g:simpliciter} by perpetual death; and this is the exposition of blessed Augustine, Homily 43, on that of John 8: "If any man keep my word, he shall not taste death for ever."[^10]

## ¶3 · 129.13–129.26

> [p. 129] Nec est negandum a sic dicentibus quin angeli sint homines, iuxta testimonium scripturarum. Quod² si obicitur scripturam dicere² angelos esse viros, et per consequens pari auctoritate concedendum esset fore distinccionem sexuum angelorum. Sed sic arguens non recolit quod ‘vir’ notat etatem, formam, sexum,³ probitatem. Concedendum est ergo sine distinccione sexuum omnem creaturam racionalem virentem virtutibus virum esse. Unde Ierem.⁴ 31. dicitur significanter de beata virgine involvendo⁵ Christum duplici matricula, ‘femina circumdabit virum’; non quod tunc fuit vir forma corporis vel etate, sed quia omni genere carismatum tunc pollebat⁶; vel quia⁷ boni angeli sic virtutibus semper virent,⁸ non mirum⁹ si⁹ scriptura sine implicacione¹⁰ distinccionis sexuum verissime tales angelos vocet ‘viros.’

Nor is it to be denied by those who speak thus that angels are men, according to the testimony of the scriptures. And if [it is objected]{g:obicitur} that scripture says angels are "men" (*viri*), and consequently by equal authority it would have to be granted that there is a distinction of sexes among angels: but one who argues so does not remember that "man" (*vir*) signifies age, form, sex, probity.[^11] It is to be granted, therefore, that without distinction of sexes every rational creature flourishing in virtues is a "man". Hence in Jeremiah 31:22 it is said significantly of the blessed virgin, enfolding Christ in a twofold womb, "A woman shall compass a man"; not that he was then a man in bodily form or in age, but because he then abounded in every kind of [charisms]{g:carismata};[^12] or, since the good angels thus always live in virtues,[^13] no wonder if scripture, without implying a distinction of sexes, most truly calls such angels "men".

## ¶4 · 129.27–130.7

> [p. 129] Sed ulterius non est negandum quin Christus habet racionem equivocam individuantem, secundum quam est Iesus, que non potest alteri individuo convenire. Aliter enim non esset essenciale principium generis¹¹ Christiani, nisi in eo quod esset proprietatum collacio addita nature specifice per [p. 130] quam individualiter a singulis aliis distinguitur: ut dicit Anselmus in De Incarnacione 7°., ‘Quis alius, queso, est Deus et homo, et¹ ex illis principiis individuantibus temporaliter procreatus,² ex quibus efficitur noster Iesus?’ Sed³ procul hoc ad inferendum³ diversitatem specificam, quia per idem omnia que individualiter distinguuntur differrent⁴ specifice.

But further, it is not to be denied that Christ has an equivocal individuating account, according to which he is Jesus, which cannot belong to another [individual]{g:individuum}. For otherwise there would be no essential principle of the Christian kind, except in this, that there would be a [collection of properties]{g:collacio} added to [the nature of the species]{g:natura-specifica}, through [130] which he is individually distinguished from each of the others;[^14] as Anselm says in *De incarnatione* 7: "Who else, I ask, is God and man, and procreated in time from those individuating principles from which our Jesus is made?"[^15] But this is far from inferring diversity of species, since by the same token all things that are individually distinguished would differ in species.

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Diffinicio sive descripcio*: both are "definition" (GLOSSARY); the pair is the strict definition and the descriptive one. *Probabilis*: worthy of credence, "reputable". Below, *describere* is "define".

[^2]: **T.** *Quantitate*, with A B C and the title of Augustine's work, against the print's *Qualitate* (A B C are one recension, but the source agrees with them). The chapter number is lacking in all the manuscripts (127.11 n. 7). The definition "rational mortal animal" is discussed in *De quantitate animae* 25.47 (LEDGER I08b-1, to be checked).

[^3]: **S.** Augustine, *De Trinitate* XV.7.11, "man is a rational substance consisting of soul and body" (LEDGER I08b-2, to be checked).

[^4]: **T.** *Filio Dei*, with A B C (*filio … filio*), against the print's *Filius Dei* (127.24), which leaves *herendo* "cleaving" with nothing to cleave to; Augustine's text, as remembered, reads *filius hominis Filio Dei* (to be checked). A B C's first *filio* is not followed. The homily is Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 19.15 (A B C "9"; LEDGER I08b-3, to be checked).

[^5]: **T.** The print and the manuscripts read *animam* "a soul" (127.27); Harris asks whether *hominem* "man" should be read, as the comparison and the closing "the Word of God having man" require. Translated as printed; Augustine's text to be checked (LEDGER I08b-3).

[^6]: **S/C.** Hugh of St Victor, *De sacramentis* II.1.11 is the likely place ("man is a rational soul having a body"; LEDGER I08b-4, to be checked). *Sentencia*: the position taught (CONTEXTUAL row, first sense).

[^7]: **S.** Robert Grosseteste, bishop of Lincoln, *Hexaemeron*, on the sixth day (LEDGER I08b-5, to be checked). Gen 2:7: Wyclif's *spiravit* for the Vulgate's *inspiravit* (DR "breathed into"); "man became a living soul" is the same verse. Matt 22:32: DR "I am the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob? He is not the God of the dead but of the living"; the English follows Wyclif's Latin, which lacks the Vulgate's *et* before *Deus Isaac*.

[^8]: **C.** *Obiective beatificans*: God as the object whose possession makes the blessed blessed; that is the "one sense" of *theos* meant. *Cum hodie non daret* gives the reason the words cannot be about time past only: so understood, they would give Moses that day no ground to expect help for the patriarchs' seed (Exod 3:6). The silencing is Matt 22:34, "he had silenced the Sadducees".

[^9]: **G.** *Presciti*: Wyclif's term for those whom God foreknows will be damned; not "the reprobate".

[^10]: **S.** Wyclif's *mortem non gustabit* is the wording of John 8:52 (DR "he shall not taste death for ever"), where the Jews repeat the saying of 8:51 (*mortem non videbit*, "he shall not see death"). Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 43 (LEDGER I08b-6, to be checked).

[^11]: **C.** Latin has two words that English renders "man": *homo* (the species) and *vir* (the male). Quotation marks mark *vir*; Wyclif plays on *vir*, *virens* "flourishing" and *virtus* "virtue".

[^12]: **C.** *Duplici matricula*: *matricula*, diminutive of *matrix* "womb". *Carismata*: gifts (CONTEXTUAL row, first sense). The prophet is Jeremiah (Jer 31:22), as the print has it; A B C read *Ezech.*

[^13]: **T.** *Vivunt* "live", with A B C O (A B C *vivunt super*, O *semper vivunt*), against the print's *virent* "flourish" (129.25), which Harris restored from *virentem* above (129.20) against every manuscript. The manuscripts' reading construes, so it is followed, at the cost of the play on *vir*.

[^14]: **C.** *Racio*: "account", the species-sense (*racio speciei*), not the row's "aspect" (against *substancia*). *Collacio proprietatum*: the "collection of properties" that individuates, not the row's "conferral". Possibly the subject of *esset* is Christ: "he would not be the essential principle of the Christian kind". A B C read *ergo* for *generis* (129.30).

[^15]: **S.** Anselm, *Epistola de incarnatione Verbi* (Wyclif's "c. 7"; LEDGER I08b-7, to be checked). A B C omit *procreatus*.
