# *De ecclesia*, chapter 16, part 6: church treasure for the kingdom's need; the seventh argument: lordship held by a community

## ¶1 · 376.29–377.20

> [p. 376] et calices et preciosa tumbarum et ecclesie ornamenta, ut puta, si aliunde vel oportunius regnum non sufficit redimere captivos qui in defensione regni [p. 377] legitime militarunt; et pro iterata defensione regni necessarii sunt. Secundo, si regnum pro sua defensione necessaria non habeat aliunde vel congruencius unde militantibus pro defensione regni ministraret stipendia. Et tercio, si vulgus per taxas gubernacioni regni necessarias ad nimiam pauperiem sit reductus. Racio beati Ambrosii stat in isto: Melius est quod regnum religiose defendatur per propria quam quod in penam peccati sui depredetur de hiis que inutiliter et avare retinuit a gente sacrilega aliena. Sic enim Nabugodonosor ex peccato sacerdotum fecit templum in Jerusalem spoliari (ut patet Regum XX. et Daniel V°), sed quid propter superbiam qua plus timemus hostes cognoscere nostram egenciam, quam Deum offendere per nostram arroganciam, et quid per evidencias sophisticas et partem quam facit clerus potentibus lamentabiliter pauperes sunt predati? Et ista creditur una causa quare thesaurus ecclesie et cleri Francie fuit per nostros in Angliam transportatus, sed Felix quem faciunt aliena pericula cautum.

also the chalices and the precious things of tombs and the ornaments of the [church]{g:ecclesia}: as for instance if the [kingdom]{g:regnum} is not able, from elsewhere or more opportunely, to redeem captives who have [377] lawfully served as soldiers in the defence of the kingdom, and are necessary for its renewed defence.[^1] Second, if the kingdom has not, from elsewhere or more fittingly, the means for its necessary defence with which to minister wages to those serving as soldiers for the defence of the kingdom. And third, if the common people have been reduced to excessive poverty by taxes necessary for the government of the kingdom. The reason of blessed Ambrose stands in this: it is better that the kingdom be defended religiously with its own [goods] than that, in punishment of its sin, it be plundered by a sacrilegious foreign people of the things which it kept back uselessly and avariciously.[^2] For so Nabugodonosor, by reason of the sin of the priests, had the temple in Jerusalem despoiled (as is clear from Kings 20 and Daniel 5); but why, through pride, by which we fear more that enemies should know our need than to offend God by our arrogance, and why, through sophistical [evidences]{g:evidencia} and the side which the [clergy]{g:clerus} takes with the powerful, have the poor been lamentably plundered?[^3] And this is believed to be one reason why the treasure of the church and of the clergy of France was carried by our men into England; but "Happy is he whom others' dangers make wary."[^4]

## ¶2 · 377.21–378.4

> [p. 377] Et patet quod dictum collegium debet dare dicto pauperi militi elemosinam de bonis pauperum ad hoc datis, non autem ad hereditarie conquirendum dominium seculare, quia sic posset clerus conquirere omnia regna mundi, cum (ut inquiunt) possunt infinite adquirere, sed nichil alienare vel perdere, et sic videretur quod talis adquisicio omnium regnorum mundi foret monstrum abhominabile Antichristi, de quo Daniel IX°, 27: Erit in templo abhominacio desolacionis; cui dicto Christus alludit Matthei XXIV, 15: Cum, inquit, videritis abhominacionem desolacionis que [p. 378] dicta est a Daniele propheta stantem in loco sancto, velut dicitur Marci XIII, 14, stantem ubi non debet: qui legit, inquit, intelligat. Sed credo quod non veniet desolacio ad hunc gradum.

And it is clear that the said college ought to give the said poor knight [alms]{g:elemosina} from the goods of the poor given for this, but not in order to acquire secular [lordship]{g:dominium} hereditarily; because so the clergy could acquire all the kingdoms of the world, since (as they say) they can acquire without limit, but alienate or lose nothing; and so it would seem that such an acquisition of all the kingdoms of the world would be an abominable monster of Antichrist, of which Daniel 9:27: "There shall be in the temple the abomination of desolation"; to which saying Christ alludes, Matthew 24:15: "When," he says, "you shall see the abomination of desolation, which [378] was spoken of by Daniel the prophet, standing in the holy place", as it is said in Mark 13:14, "standing where it ought not": "he that readeth," he says, "let him understand." But I believe that the desolation will not come to this degree.[^5]

## ¶3 · 378.5–378.15

> [p. 378] Septimo principaliter sic arguitur: Domini temporales libere transferunt ius quod habent ad sua dominabilia nunc in certam communitatem et nunc in unam personam, quia translacio iuris temporalis est libertatis creature, nolentis uti vel esse particeps alterius talis iuris, sed in certam personam transferunt absque peccato sua dominia, ergo per idem possunt absque peccato in communitate transferre iura huiusmodi. Sed translato iure in communitatem pro suo perpetuo consequitur tam perseverans dominacio, ergo stat communitatem habere perpetuo tale dominium.

Seventh, principally, it is argued thus: [temporal lords]{g:temporales-domini} freely transfer the [right]{g:ius} which they have to the things they hold in lordship, now to a certain community and now to one person, because the transfer of a temporal right belongs to the freedom of a creature unwilling to use, or to be partaker of, another such right; now they transfer their lordships to a certain person without sin; therefore by the same token they can without sin transfer such rights to a community. Now when the right has been transferred to a community for its perpetuity, so lasting an [exercise of lordship]{g:dominacio} follows; therefore it is consistent that a community should have such lordship in perpetuity.[^6]

## ¶4 · 378.16–380.5

> [p. 378] Hic dico, primo quod conclusio deducta est concedenda de communitate civili que est capax civilis dominii, ut patet de multis communitatibus civium que licite civiliter dominantur. Sed procul hoc ad inferendum quod clerico (in quantum huiusmodi) licet dominari civiliter, et multo periculosius foret communitatem cleri dominari civiliter, cum error ille vix foret extinguibilis, sed tamquam cancer serperet ampliandoque procederet, quousque magnam partem regnorum inficeret. Satis ergo est nobis habere usum moderatum temporalium pro nostris propriis necessitatibus et aliorum pauperum relevandis vel extincta eorum dominacione civili vel in manus capitalis domini derelicta, ut notat Hugo libro secundo De Sacramentis, parte secunda, cap. III° et VII°. Layci, inquit, quia terrena et terrene vite necessaria tractant, pars corporis Christi sinistra sunt. Clerici vero, [p. 379] quoniam ea que ad spiritualem vitam pertinent dispensant quasi dextera pars sunt corporis Christi. Et post interpretando utrumque terminum sic infert: Clericus nisi Deum et ea que ad Deum spectant habere non debet, cui statutum est decimis et oblacionibus que Deo offeruntur sustentari. Laicis vero christianis fidelibus terrena possidere conceditur, sed clericis spiritualia committuntur, sicut fuit in veteri testamento. Et cap. VII° declarat quomodo quedam ecclesie Christi devocione fidelium conceduntur salvo iure terrene potestatis, ne fiat confusio, cum nihil inordinatum approbare potest Deus. Unde quandoque concedunt principes terreni nudum usum ecclesie, et quandoque usum et potestatem exercende iusticie, quod clericus per ecclesiasticas personas aut individua clericalia exercere non potest. Potest tamen habere personas laycas ministros ad illud officium, sic tamen, inquit, ut id ipsum quod potestatem habet a principe terreno se habere cognoscat et ipsas possessiones nunquam ita a regia potestate elongari posse intelligat, quin si racio postulaverit aut necessitas, illi ipse possessiones debeant in necessitate obsequium, quia, sicut regia potestas patrocinium quod debet alteri alienare non debet, sic ipsa possessio eciam ab ecclesiasticis personis obtenta obsequium quod regie potestati pro patrocinio debetur iure negare non potest. Iste profundus doctor non admitteret quod clericus haberet in se civilitatem, nec emeret vel caperet in se sive papa sive alius summum dominium seculare, sed regens laycos et dominaciones suas spiritualiter recipere posset titulo elemosine fructum possessionis vel potestatis terrene, sic tamen quod seculare dominium [p. 380] remaneat aput dominum secularem. Ulterius sicut doctor concedit quod est de libertate dominorum secularium quod possunt transferre ius et possessiones in alios, sic concederet evidencius hoc de clero, cum laico sit magis cognata secularis possessio.

To this I say, first, that the [conclusion]{g:conclusio} deduced is to be granted of a civil community which is capable of civil lordship, as is clear of many communities of citizens which lawfully [exercise civil lordship]{g:dominari}. But this is far from inferring that it is lawful for a [cleric]{g:clericus} (as such) to exercise civil lordship; and it would be much more dangerous for a community of clergy to exercise civil lordship, since that error would scarcely be extinguishable, but would creep like a cancer and advance by spreading, until it infected a great part of the kingdoms. It is enough for us, therefore, to have a moderate use of [temporal goods]{g:temporalia} for our own necessities and for relieving those of other poor people, their civil exercise of lordship being either extinguished or left in the hands of the [chief lord]{g:capitalis-dominus}, as Hugh notes in the second book *On the Sacraments*, part two, chapters 3 and 7.[^7] "Laymen," he says, "because they handle earthly things and the necessaries of earthly life, are the left part of the body of Christ. But clerics, [379] since they dispense the things which pertain to the spiritual life, are as it were the right part of the body of Christ." And afterwards, interpreting each term, he infers thus: "A cleric ought to have nothing but God and the things that belong to God, he for whom it is appointed to be sustained by the [tithes]{g:decime} and offerings which are offered to God. But to lay christian faithful it is granted to possess earthly things, whereas to clerics spiritual things are committed, as it was in the old testament." And in chapter 7 he declares how certain things are granted to the churches of Christ by the devotion of the faithful, saving the right of the earthly power, lest there be confusion, since God can approve nothing disordered. Hence earthly princes sometimes grant the church the bare use, and sometimes the use and the power of exercising justice, which a cleric cannot exercise through ecclesiastical persons or clerical individuals. He can, however, have lay persons as ministers for that office; yet so, he says, "that he acknowledge that he has that very power which he has from the earthly prince, and understand that those possessions can never be so removed from the royal power that, if reason or necessity should demand, those possessions do not owe service in necessity; because, as the royal power ought not to alienate the patronage which it owes to another, so that possession, even when obtained by ecclesiastical persons, cannot by right deny the service which is owed to the royal power for its patronage."[^8] This profound doctor would not admit that a cleric should have [civil standing]{g:civilitas} in himself, nor that either pope or anyone else should buy or take into himself supreme secular lordship; but [one] ruling laymen and their exercises of lordship spiritually could receive, by [title]{g:titulus} of alms, the fruit of a possession or of earthly power, yet so that secular lordship [380] remains with the secular lord.[^9] Further, as the doctor grants that it belongs to the freedom of secular lords that they can transfer right and possessions to others, so he would grant this more evidently of the clergy, since secular possession is more akin to a layman.

## ¶5 · 380.6–380.16

> [p. 380] Item, non dubium quin clero potest nocere terrenorum habicio, si ergo non liceret sibi tunc ipsa relinquere, ipse foret contra legem Dei ipsis diabolice copulatus; Christus enim precepit suis apostolis mundana relinquere. Paulus cum potuit habuisse vite necessaria de Corinthiis, non usus fuit illa potestate, sed et Augustinus rogavit cives suos recipere possessiones quas sibi donaverant. Quare ergo non potuit papa et quilibet alius cleri dotatus capitaneus sic fecisse; illi enim utilius procurarunt pro genere cleri postero quam Silvester.

Again, there is no doubt that the having of earthly things can harm the clergy; if, then, it were not lawful for it to relinquish them, it would be diabolically coupled to them against the law of God; for Christ commanded his apostles to relinquish worldly things. Paul, although he could have had the necessaries of life from the Corinthians, did not use that power; and Augustine also asked his citizens to take back the possessions which they had given him. Why then could not the [pope]{g:papa}, and every other endowed [captain]{g:capitaneus} of the clergy, have done the same? For they provided more usefully for the later generations of the clergy than Silvester.[^10]

## ¶6 · 380.17–380.30

> [p. 380] Item, non ex dotacione cesarea constituitur novus ordo cleri qui sine dotacione huiusmodi non fuisset; quilibet ergo ordo cleri potuit fuisse sine dotacione huiusmodi cum tanta religiositate ordinis sicut modo. Ex quo sequitur quod dotacio talis quecunque est ordini cleri impertinens; aliter enim in spoliacionibus domorum religiosarum et in conquestibus terrarum destruerentur proporcionaliter possessionatorum ordines, et sic variarentur ordines illi proporcionaliter, ut dotacio variatur, quod dementis est dicere. Sicut ergo in prima dotacione sua potuit religiosus possessiones oblatas relinquere, sic modo post possessionem, quod si generaliter voluntarie fieret, maneret idem ordo perfeccior quam est modo.

Again, it is not by the caesarean [endowment]{g:dotacio} that a new order of clergy is constituted, which without such endowment would not have existed; every order of clergy, therefore, could have existed without such endowment, with as great religiousness of the order as now. From which [it follows]{g:sequitur} that any such endowment whatever is not pertinent to the order of the clergy; for otherwise, in the despoiling of religious houses and in conquests of lands, the orders of [the possessioners]{g:possessionati} would be destroyed proportionally, and so those orders would vary proportionally as the endowment varies, which it is madness to say. As, therefore, at his first endowment [a religious]{g:religiosi} could relinquish the possessions offered, so [he can] now after possession; and if this were done generally and voluntarily, the same order would remain more perfect than it is now.[^11]

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** At 377.1 B reads *illata defensione*. **C.** The subject of *necessarii sunt* is the captives: the kingdom needs them for its defence again.

[^2]: **T.** At 377.5–6 B reads *regni necessitas*. **C.** *Racio beati Ambrosii*: Wyclif's statement of the reasoning in the passage just quoted, not a quotation. *Per propria*: "with its own [goods]", the church treasure within the kingdom, against plunder by a foreign enemy.

[^3]: **S.** "Kings 20" is Wyclif's reference: 4 Kings 20:17 (DR "all that is in thy house ... shall be carried into Babylon") is the prophecy, 4 Kings 24:13 the spoiling; Daniel 5:2 (DR "the vessels of gold and silver, which Nabuchodonosor, his father, had brought away out of the temple"). Which verses he meant is our inference. **C.** *Quid ... quid*: "why", introducing a rhetorical question. *Partem quam facit clerus potentibus*: the side the clergy takes in favour of the powerful.

[^4]: **R.** The French church treasure taken by English armies in the French war; Wyclif names no occasion. **S.** A proverbial verse (Loserth: *vulgo iactatus versus*), comparing Plautus, *Feliciter sapit qui alieno periculo sapit* (LEDGER E16f-1).

[^5]: **C.** *Ut inquiunt*: the claim of the endowed themselves, that what passes into mortmain can grow without limit but never be alienated. **S.** Daniel 9:27 (DR "there shall be in the temple the abomination of desolation"); Matthew 24:15, where the Vulgate has *Cum ergo* and Wyclif omits *ergo* and adds *inquit*; Mark 13:14 (DR "standing where it ought not (he that readeth let him understand)").

[^6]: **R.** A in the margin at 378.5: *Septima racio*. **T.** At 378.13 B reads *translacio*. **C.** *Dominabilia*: the things over which they have lordship. *Ius* is the subjective right. *Tam perseverans* has no correlative; the sense is presumably "as lasting [as the community]", which does not die.

[^7]: **R.** A in the margin at 378.15: *Responsio*; at 378.28: *Hugo*. **C.** *In quantum huiusmodi*: "as such", i.e. as cleric. *Vel extincta ... vel ... derelicta*: the clergy's civil exercise of lordship is either extinguished or left to the chief lord, the use remaining.

[^8]: **S.** Hugh of St Victor, *De sacramentis* 2.2.3 and 2.2.7 (Loserth: Migne ed., pp. 417 and 420, "with some words changed"; Hugh reads *Laici ergo Christiani qui*, and *partem Dei* for *ea que ad Deum*, *agnoscat* for *cognoscat*) (LEDGER E16f-2). **C.** Wyclif quotes chapter 3, paraphrases chapter 7 (*declarat quomodo*), and returns to quotation at *sic tamen, inquit*; the bounds given to the quotation are our reading. *Individua clericalia*: "clerical individuals", clerics acting singly; the sense is uncertain. Hugh reads *judicia saecularia* here (PL 176, 420), so the print's words may be a corruption in Wyclif's copy or text. **T.** At 379.15 B reads *personas ac individua*. **R.** A in the margin at 379.8: *Contra dominacionem civilem cleri*; B in the margin at 379.19: *Nota, tantum elongantur temporalia data clericis a brachio seculari*.

[^9]: **C.** *Profundus doctor*: Hugh. The subject of *regens* and *posset* is the cleric, unexpressed ("[one]"); *dominaciones suas*: the laymen's exercises of lordship. **R.** A in the margin at 380.1: *Nota*.

[^10]: **T.** At 380.6–7 B reads *terrenorum non abicio*; the print's *habicio* is translated. **S.** Christ's command: cf. Luke 14:33 (DR "every one of you that doth not renounce all that he possesseth cannot be my disciple"). Paul: 1 Corinthians 9:12 (DR "Nevertheless, we have not used this power"). Augustine's offer to give the church's possessions over to the people, who never would take them, is told by Possidius, *Vita Augustini* 23 (PL 32:53); c. 24 adds a deed of gift he sent back (LEDGER E16f-3). **C.** *Illi enim*: Paul and Augustine.

[^11]: **T.** At 380.25 A reads *proporcionabiter*. **C.** The argument is that endowment is accidental to an order: what came without changing the order can go without changing it. *Sic modo*: "[he can]" supplied.
