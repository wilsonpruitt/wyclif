# *De ecclesia*, chapter 21, part 2: Bonaventure against suffrages for the damned

## ¶1 · 494.13–496.12

> [p. 494] Rogo notetis quomodo allegatur doctor Bonaventura super IV° Sentenciarum procurasse effectualiter pro hac parte; scribit enim super distinccionem XLVIᵃᵐ libri quarti sub hac forma: Videtur, inquit, quod per suffragia aliqua mitigacio fiat dampnatis et faciendo quinque raciones pro parte illa recitat tres responsiones quas reprobat. Prima dicit quod suffragia ecclesie prosunt dampnatis, non tamen omnibus, quia non valde malis, et istam dicit fuisse magistri Prepositivi. Sed ista dimissio pene est solum ad tempus (ut quidam dicunt quod omni die Pasche habent omnes dampnati remissionem pene). Secundi dicunt quod fit remissio simpliciter non secundum proporcionem arismetricam sed geometricam (ut dicitur de indulgenciis), ita quod non quelibet operacio eque meritoria eque diminuit de pena eterna, sed quelibet operacio ecclesie eque meritoria diminuit proporcionaliter de pena quam invenit, ut si meritum ecclesie, ut quatuor diminuit centesimam partem [p. 495] qualitativam pene, tunc quodcunque meritum domini diminuit proporcionaliter centesimam partem pene quam invenit, et ita superest semper divitibus occasio impetrandi indulgencias, tam pro vivis quam mortuis, tam existentibus in purgatorio quam dampnatis. Tercia opinio dicit quod suffragia ecclesie non prosunt dampnatis vel ad liberacionem totalis pene nec ad ablacionem partis pene diminuendo vel mitigando, puniens ne tantum puniatur, sed fortificando punitum ut melius et tollerabilius sustineat. Sed reprobatis hiis tribus opinionibus sequitur doctoris sentencia in hec verba: Ideo, inquit, est aliorum opinio communior et verior quod suffragia dampnatis non prosunt, nec ecclesia intendit orare pro eis. Et si sophista instet quod quelibet istarum opinionum est tunc vera, patet quod doctor intelligit quod quelibet aliarum est vera secundum partem, vel negative non est vera. Ista vero eis contradictoria est vera totaliter. Et consequenter respondet ad quinque argumenta facta in oppositum: Primum est ex dicto beati Augustini, Enchiridion CX°, dicentis quod quibus mortuis prosunt suffragia ecclesie vel prosunt ut plena fiat remissio vel ut tolleracior fiat eis dampnacio. Ex quo videtur quod pena dampnati poterit mitigari. Dicendum, inquit, quod non intelligitur de dampnacione facta in inferno, ubi nulla est redempcio, sed in purgatorio, ubi dupliciter illis prosunt: vel statim liberando, ut eos qui parum habent de cremabili vel mitigando quoad eos qui gravius peccaverunt. Et eandem responsionem tenet sanctus Thomas super [p. 496] IV°, distinccione XLVIª, questione prima. Dampnacio, inquit, quandoque large accipitur pro deputacione ad penam quancunque, ut Luce XXIII°, 40 dicit beatus Latro, tu in eadem dampnacione es; quandoque vero accipitur stricte pro deputacione ad penam perpetuam, ut Matthei XXV°, 46: Ibunt hii in supplicium eternum; et ista significacio est famosior. Et istam responsionem tenet Ostiensis in Summa sua, libro quinto, De Penitenciis et Remissionibus, secundum quod anime defunctorum, et exponitur dictum Augustini, XIIIª, q. IIª Tempus. Glossa autem que dicit quod papa dispensat contra Deum male exponit contrarie Hostiensi.

I ask you to note how the doctor Bonaventure on the fourth [book] of the *Sentences* is [alleged]{g:allegare} to have acted effectually for this side; for he writes on the forty-sixth distinction of the fourth book in this form: "It seems," he says, "that some mitigation is made for the damned through [suffrages]{g:suffragia}"; and making five arguments for that side, he recites three answers, which he rejects.[^1] The first says that the suffrages of the [church]{g:ecclesia} profit the damned, yet not all, since not the very evil; and this, he says, was Master Prepositivus's. But this remission of punishment is only for a time (as some say that on every Easter day all the damned have remission of punishment).[^2] The second say that remission is made [without qualification]{g:simpliciter}, not according to arithmetical but according to geometrical proportion (as is said of [indulgences]{g:indulgencie}), so that not every equally meritorious work diminishes the eternal punishment equally, but every equally meritorious work of the church diminishes proportionally the punishment it finds; so that if the merit of the church, as four, diminishes a hundredth [495] qualitative part of the punishment, then any merit of the lord diminishes proportionally a hundredth part of the punishment it finds; and so there always remains to the rich occasion to [obtain]{g:impetrare} indulgences, for the living as much as for the dead, for those in purgatory as much as for the damned.[^3] The third [opinion]{g:opinio} says that the suffrages of the church do not profit the damned either for deliverance from the whole punishment or for the [taking-away]{g:ablacio} of part of the punishment, by diminishing or mitigating, the one punishing [acting] so that [the sufferer] is not punished so much, but by strengthening the one punished, so that he may bear it better and more tolerably.[^4] But, these three opinions having been rejected, there follows the doctor's [teaching]{g:sentencia} in these words: "Therefore," he says, "the opinion of others is more common and truer, that suffrages do not profit the damned, nor does the church intend to pray for them." And if a sophist should object that each of these opinions is then true, it is clear that the doctor understands that each of the others is true in part, or, negatively, is not true; but this one, contradictory to them, is true wholly.[^5] And consequently he answers the five arguments made for the opposite. The first is from the saying of blessed Augustine, *Enchiridion* 110, who says that to those dead whom the suffrages of the church profit, they profit either so that remission may be full or so that damnation may become more tolerable for them. From which it seems that the punishment of a damned man can be mitigated. "It must be said," he says, "that this is not understood of damnation made in hell, where there is no redemption, but in purgatory, where they profit them in two ways: either by freeing them at once, as those who have little of what is burnable, or by mitigating, as regards those who have sinned more gravely."[^6] And Saint Thomas holds the same answer on the [496] fourth [book], distinction 46, question 1. "Damnation," he says, "is sometimes taken broadly for [deputation]{g:deputacio} to any punishment whatever, as in Luke 23:40 the blessed Thief says, 'thou art under the same condemnation'; but sometimes it is taken strictly for deputation to perpetual punishment, as in Matthew 25:46: 'These shall go into everlasting punishment'; and this signification is the [best-known]{g:famosior}."[^7] And Hostiensis holds this answer in his *Summa*, book five, *On Penances and Remissions*, under "That the souls of the departed", and the saying of Augustine is expounded at [Causa] 13, q. 2, [c.] *Tempus*. But the Gloss that says that the [pope]{g:papa} dispenses against God expounds badly, contrary to Hostiensis.[^8]

## ¶2 · 496.13–498.7

> [p. 496] Secundo arguit doctor ex alio dicto beati Augustini dicentis de pena Arrii quod non est determinata, quia augetur secundum quod peccatores suo errore multiplicantur, sed Deus pronior est ad miserendum quam ad puniendum. Ergo si in statu dampnacionis potest eorum pena augeri, potest et minui. Dicendum, inquit, quod non est simile, quia remissio debet fundari in fundamento caritatis, sed Arrius continue habet malam voluntatem sed non caritatem. Tercio arguit sic: Impossibile est multorum preces non exaudiri, sed ecclesia orat pro omnibus fidelibus qui moriuntur; de quibus constat multos dampnari. Ergo si ecclesia non frustra orat, oportet omnes fideles salvari. Dicendum, inquit, quod si constaret ecclesie aliquem esse dampnatum, non oraret pro eo. Et quantum ad formam ac materiam ulteriorem istius argumenti patet inferius. Quarto arguit doctor sic: Plus placet Deo pena quam quis sustinet voluntarius quam alia [p. 497] quam quis sustinet invitus, sed dampnatus penam sustinet invitus. Sed vir iustus in casu penam sustinet voluntarius pro dampnato, ergo vir iustus superstes plus solvit Deo pro dampnato voluntarie paciendo, quam dampnatus sufficeret solvere pro se ipso. Ergo si Dei iusticie satisfactum est pro eo quod minus placet, Dei misericordia acquiretur pro eo quod magis placet. Dicendum, inquit, quod verum est, cum Deus remunerat virum iustum qui pie et condicionaliter orat pro defuncto dampnato. Sed (ut dicam posterius) talis orat realiter pro se ipso et solum pro capacibus. Ideo dicit doctor, eo quod pena voluntaria non potest assumi nec acceptari pro eo qui non habet caritatem. Quinto arguit doctor sic: Talis iustus punit se meritorie pro dampnato, ergo Deus acceptat hanc penam pro illo. Sed Deus non bis punit in id ipsum, ergo oportet quod dampnatus habeat mitigacionem pene. Patet ex significacione proximi argumenti quod iustus punit se pro merito suo atque ecclesie et non pro salvacione talis dampnati. Sed ulterius querit doctor utrum fiat mitigacio pene dampnatorum ex pietate misericordie divine. Et arguit primo quod sic per illud Psalmi LXXVI, 10: Numquid obliviscetur misereri Deus aut continebit in ira sua misericordias suas? Secundo per hoc quod peccans mortaliter offendit Deum a quo est omne bonum, ergo meretur amittere omne bonum; cum ergo Deus non adnichilat eum, videtur quod Deus misericorditer punit eum. Tercio per hoc quod, eo ipso quo quis peccat offendit in ordinem, et per consequens meretur statim ordinari ad penam, cum ergo Deus expectat eius conversionem et non statim [p. 498] dampnat eum, videtur quod pietas Dei se extendit ad dampnabilem. Quarto per hoc quod peccans offendit in infinitum bonum, ergo meretur infinitam penam, quam cum Deus non infligit, sequitur quod Deus misericorditer punit quemlibet peccatorem. Sed doctor respondet concedendo consequens: Dicendum, inquit, quod mitigacio pene dampnatorum dupliciter

Secondly the doctor argues from another saying of blessed Augustine, who says of the punishment of Arius that it is not determined, since it is increased according as sinners are multiplied by his error; now God is more inclined to have mercy than to punish. Therefore if in the state of damnation their punishment can be increased, it can also be lessened. "It must be said," he says, "that it is not alike, since remission must be founded on the foundation of [charity]{g:caritas}, now Arius continually has an evil will, but not charity."[^9] Thirdly he argues thus: It is impossible that the prayers of many should not be heard; now the church prays for all the faithful who die, of whom it is evident that many are damned. Therefore if the church does not pray in vain, all the faithful must be saved. "It must be said," he says, "that if it were evident to the church that someone was damned, it would not pray for him." And as to the form and the further matter of this argument, it is clear below.[^10] Fourthly the doctor argues thus: The punishment one bears willingly pleases God more than another [497] which one bears unwillingly; now the damned man bears his punishment unwillingly. Now a just man, in a given case, bears punishment willingly for the damned man; therefore the surviving just man pays more to God for the damned man by suffering willingly than the damned man would be able to pay for himself. Therefore if satisfaction has been made to God's justice by what pleases less, God's mercy will be gained by what pleases more. "It must be said," he says, "that it is true," since God rewards the just man who [piously]{g:pie} and conditionally prays for a dead man who is damned. But (as I shall say later) such a man really prays for himself and only for those capable [of it]. So the doctor says [it], because voluntary punishment cannot be taken on or accepted for one who does not have charity.[^11] Fifthly the doctor argues thus: Such a just man punishes himself meritoriously for the damned man; therefore God accepts this punishment for him. Now God does not punish twice for the same thing; therefore the damned man must have mitigation of punishment. It is clear from the signification of the previous argument that the just man punishes himself for his own merit and the church's, and not for the [salvation]{g:salvacio} of such a damned man.[^12] But further the doctor asks whether mitigation of the punishment of the damned comes about from the [piety]{g:pietas} of the divine mercy. And he argues first that it does, by that [verse] of Psalm 76:10: "Will God forget to shew mercy? or will he in his anger shut up his mercies?"[^13] Secondly, by this: that one who sins mortally offends God, from whom is every good; therefore he deserves to lose every good; since, then, God does not annihilate him, it seems that God punishes him mercifully. Thirdly, by this: that by the very fact that someone sins he offends against order, and in consequence deserves to be ordered at once to punishment; since, then, God awaits his conversion and does not at once [498] damn him, it seems that the piety of God extends to the damnable. Fourthly, by this: that one who sins offends against an infinite good; therefore he deserves infinite punishment; and since God does not inflict it, it follows that God punishes every sinner mercifully. ## ¶3 · 498.8–498.29

> [p. 498] potest intelligi, aut quantum ad taxacionem et infliccionem pene; et sic indubie est pene mitigacio in inferno, quia illa mitigacio non est nisi mitis et misericors perpetue pene deputacio. Sed secundo modo potest intelligi mitigacio post pene taxacionem et infliccionem, et hoc modo nulla cadit mitigacio a divina misericordia. Et rogo scolam hic vigilanter attendere si non sit fictum mendacium adducendo testimonium huius doctoris in aliquo, cum tam manifeste contrariatur dicenti quod ecclesia debet orare pro dampnatis in inferno, ut pena eorum fiat remissior. Unde sanctus Thomas (ubi supra) recitando tres opiniones erroneas imponit terciam Altissidorensi dicenti quod dampnati non suffragantur per diminucionem vel interrupcionem pene, sed per confortacionem pacientis, sicut si homo portaret grave onus et facies sua perfunderetur aqua, confortaretur, licet onus in nullo sit levius. Est, inquit sanctus, predicta opinio presumptuosa, ut puta, sanctorum dictis contraria et vana, nulla auctoritate fulcita et nihilominus irracionabilis, quia dampnati in inferno sunt extra vinculum caritatis, secundum quam opera vivorum continuantur defunctis.

But in the second way mitigation can be understood after the assessment and infliction of the punishment, and in this way no mitigation falls from the divine mercy."[^14] And I ask the school here to attend vigilantly whether it is not a feigned lie to bring forward the testimony of this doctor in anything, since he so manifestly contradicts one who says that the church ought to pray for the damned in hell so that their punishment may become more remiss. "The aforesaid opinion," says the saint, "is presumptuous, that is, contrary to the sayings of the saints, and empty, supported by no authority, and nonetheless irrational, since the damned in hell are outside the bond of charity, according to which the works of the living are joined to the dead."[^15]

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 494.13: He alleges the authority of Bonaventura, who is against him.
- 495.25: Damnation is used in two senses and may be only to pain in purgatory,
- 496.13: Argument from a saying of Augustine's;
- 496.23: from the Church's praying for all the departed.
- 497.1: 4. Voluntary atonement is better than involuntary; so the suffrages of the pious atone more than the pains of hell.
- 497.14: 5. God accepts the atonement.
- 497.17: Answer: The profit is to the Church, not to the damned.
- 497.21: The doctor argues that God's mercy extends to the damned.
- 498.6: God's mercy works in fixing their punishment, not in lessening it afterwards.
- 498.19: St. Thomas also declares the damned to be beyond our aid.

## Notes

[^1]: **S.** Bonaventure, *In IV Sent.* d. 46, a. 1, q. 1 (Loserth, 494.16; the page number is lost from the foot; LEDGER E21b-1). **C.** *Procurasse effectualiter pro hac parte*: ironic; Wyclif goes on to show that Bonaventure argues the other way. *Tres responsiones*: the three rejected answers (*opiniones* below); Bonaventure's own makes a fourth (A in the margin at 494.16: *Quadruplex opinio*). **T.** B *Beneventure* (494.13). **R.** Side-note 494.13.

[^2]: **C.** *Non valde malis*: the "not very bad", Augustine's class (*Enchiridion* 110). *Omni die Pasche*: "on every Easter day". Prepositivus: as at 493.31 (LEDGER E21a-2). **T.** The codices read *istum* for *istam* (494.21).

[^3]: **C.** *Secundi*: the holders of the second opinion. Geometrical proportion: each equal merit removes the same fraction (a hundredth) of whatever punishment remains, so that the punishment never reaches nothing and the rich always have reason to obtain more indulgences. *Ut quatuor*: the merit valued at four. *Meritum domini*: printed so; the sense wants "any [equal] merit", and *domini* may be corrupt. *Partem qualitativam*: printed so (the plate confirms the token); the argument wants *quantitativam*, a quantitative part.

[^4]: **C.** *Puniens ne tantum puniatur* does not construe as printed; it is taken as describing the kind of mitigation the third opinion denies (the punisher lessening what he inflicts). The opinion is that suffrages strengthen the sufferer and do not lessen the pain; Thomas attributes it to William of Auxerre (498.19–21). **T.** AA₁ *diminuende vel mitigande* (495.8), agreeing with *pene*.

[^5]: **C.** *Si sophista instet*: the comparatives *communior et verior* imply that the rejected opinions are also true; Wyclif answers that each is true in part, or not true at all, and only Bonaventure's contradictory is wholly true. *Sentencia*: "teaching", the position he holds.

[^6]: **S.** Augustine, *Enchiridion* 110 (the codices *CXII*; Loserth, 495.21; LEDGER E21b-2); Bonaventure, ibid., Opp. IV, p. 1019 (Loserth, 495.21; LEDGER E21b-3). **C.** *Ubi nulla est redempcio*: the liturgical *quia in inferno nulla est redemptio* (LEDGER E21b-4); *redempcio* here is release from the penalty, not the buying off of a sentence. *De cremabili*: of what can be burnt (cf. 1 Corinthians 3:12–15). **R.** Side-note 495.25; A in the margin at 495.25: *Responsio*.

[^7]: **S.** Thomas, *In IV Sent.* d. 46, q. 2 (Loserth, 496.1; Wyclif's *questione prima* differs; LEDGER E21b-5). Luke 23:40 and Matthew 25:46 (DR). **C.** *Dampnacio*: "damnation" throughout; DR's "condemnation" in Luke 23:40 renders the same word, and the broad sense is the point of the citation. *Deputacio*: "deputation", the assigning of someone to a punishment. **T.** A *ut eis* (495.28); B *ista signacio* (496.7). **R.** A in the margin at 495.28: *Dampnacio capitur dupliciter*.

[^8]: **S.** Hostiensis (printed *Ostiensis* here, *Hostiensis* at 494.1), *Summa aurea* bk 5, *De penitentiis et remissionibus*, § *Quibus mortuis suffragia*, which opens *Hoc sciendum est quod anime defunctorum quatuor beneficiis adiuvantur* (LEDGER E21b-6). Decretum C.13 q.2 c.23 *Tempus* (Loserth, 496.10, "Decr. sec. pars, causa XIII, q. II, cap. XXIII"; the modern form is ours; LEDGER E21b-7). **C.** *Secundum quod anime defunctorum*: taken as the rubric of a section of the *Summa*, *secundum* perhaps standing for a printed §; it does not construe as "according as". *Glossa*: the ordinary gloss, presumably on that canon (LEDGER E21b-8).

[^9]: **S.** Augustine on the punishment of Arius growing with his followers' error: Loserth refers only to Bonaventure (496.18; LEDGER E21b-9). **R.** Side-note 496.13; A in the margin at 496.18: *Responsio. Argumenta quod omnes salvabuntur et soluciones*.

[^10]: **S.** *Impossibile est multorum preces non exaudiri*: Ambrosiaster on Romans 15:30, *multorum preces impossibile est ut non impetrent* (PL 17:177D), carried by the Gloss (PL 114:517C) and Lombard (PL 191:1526D) (LEDGER E21b-10). **C.** *Patet inferius*: Wyclif will answer the argument later in the chapter. **T.** A *quis tinet* (496.30, 497.1) for *quis sustinet*. **R.** Side-note 496.23; A in the margin at 496.26: *Responsio*.

[^11]: **C.** *Solvit*: "pays", the satisfaction rendered to God's justice. *Dicendum … verum est*: Bonaventure grants the premiss; *cum Deus remunerat …* and the parenthesis *ut dicam posterius* are taken as Wyclif's gloss, and *Ideo dicit doctor* resumes Bonaventure's reason (voluntary punishment cannot be accepted for one without charity). Where Bonaventure's words end is not certain. *Pro capacibus*: for those capable [of being helped]. **S.** Bonaventure, ibid., p. 1021 (Loserth, 497.8; LEDGER E21b-11). **R.** Side-note 497.1; A in the margin at 497.8: *Responsio*.

[^12]: **S.** *Deus non bis punit in id ipsum*: the maxim drawn from Nahum 1:9 (LEDGER E21b-12). **T.** B *signacione* (497.18). **R.** Side-notes 497.14, 497.17; A in the margin at 497.19: *Responsio*.

[^13]: **S.** Psalm 76:10 (Wyclif *Numquid … aut* for the Vulgate's *aut … aut*). Bonaventure's second question, ibid., pp. 1021–1022, "with some words changed" (Loserth, 497.21–22; LEDGER E21b-13). **C.** *Pietas*: "piety", kept apart from *misericordia* "mercy". **R.** Side-note 497.21; A in the margin at 497.21: *Questio bona*.

[^14]: **C.** *Concedendo consequens*: Bonaventure grants that God punishes the damned mercifully, in the first of his two senses. *Taxacio*: the assessment, the fixing of the penalty's measure. The sentence runs across the paragraph division at 498.7–8. **T.** B *sequitur Deus* (498.4); A and B in the margin at 498.5: *Mitigacio pene dampnatorum dupliciter accipitur*. **S.** Bonaventure, ibid., pp. 1021–1022 (Loserth, 498.6; LEDGER E21b-13). **R.** Side-note 498.6.

[^15]: **S.** Thomas, *In IV Sent.* d. 45 q. 2 a. 2 qc. 1 co. (= *Suppl.* q. 71 a. 5), quoted closely, though Wyclif's . **C.** *Suffragantur*: passive, the damned "are aided by intercession". *Continuantur*: are joined; the works of the living reach the dead through the bond of charity. *Ut puta*: "that is", introducing the counts of the charge. **R.** Side-note 498.19.
