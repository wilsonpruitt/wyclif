# *De eucharistia*, chapter 5, part 8: the signification of the remaining bread, bodily food, and new place without change

## ¶1 · 143.27–144.7

> [p. 143] Quantum ad secundum obiectum quo dicitur quod tolleretur significacio sacramenti eo quod species que inducerent in noscenciam veri corporis Christi significarent magis substanciam; oportet, inquam, fidelem loycum cognoscere defectus talis argucie, nam sicut philosophice dicit sanctus Thomas illa accidencia [p. 144] remanencia naturaliter significant substanciam panis, sicut patet de homine et bestia qui per naturalem eorum significanciam estimant verum panem, et indubie quante totus panis significaret se ipsum, tante illa vocata accidencia que remanent significant naturam panis, cum panis non habet nisi per illa significare quidquam sensibile.

As to the second [objection]{g:instancia},[^1] by which it is said that the [signification]{g:signacio} of the [sacrament]{g:sacramentum} would be taken away, because the [species]{g:species} which would lead into knowledge[^2] of the true [body of Christ]{g:corpus-christi} would rather signify the [substance]{g:substancia}: a faithful logician, I say, must recognise the defects of such a [quibble]{g:argucia}. For, as Saint Thomas says philosophically, "those [accidents]{g:accidens} [144] remaining naturally signify the substance of the [bread]{g:panis}", as is clear of man and beast, who by their natural signifying judge [it] true bread;[^3] and without doubt, as much as the whole bread would signify itself, so much do those [so-called]{g:vocatus} accidents which remain signify the [nature]{g:natura} of the bread, since the bread has [no means] except through them to signify anything [perceptible]{g:sensibilis}.

## ¶2 · 144.8–144.26

> [p. 144] Et patet quam nuda et impossibilis est ista argucia: Si panis remaneret, significaret magis substanciam panis quam corpus Christi, ut patet in exemplo palpabili: In corpore quo scriptores liniunt litteras, dicciones et proposiciones remanent corpus substratum accidentibus que ipsum significant. Et tamen per artem et alios habitus naturales longe plus principalius et attencius significat imposita ad significandum apud intelligentes litteras quam significant ipsamet corpora in tantum quod naturalis significacio quam habet laycus in clerico est sopita; multo magis habitus fidei induceret fidelem ad intelligendum per panem consecratum verum corpus Christi. Sic enim crediderunt patres in lege veteri per manna, per agnum paschalem et per petram fluentem aqua totam humanitatem Christi (ut tactum est superius ex testimonio Augustini); ergo multo magis panis sanctificatus per verba Christi figurati significaret corpus suum expressius et principalius quam se ipsum.

And it is clear how bare and impossible this quibble is: "If the bread remained, it would signify the substance of the bread more than the body of Christ." This is clear in a palpable example. In the body with which scribes trace letters, [words]{g:diccio} and [propositions]{g:proposicio}, the body remains, laid under the accidents that signify it. And yet, by art and other natural habits, it signifies the things imposed for signifying, among those who understand letters, far more principally and attentively than the bodies themselves signify, so much that the natural signification which a layman has is, in a cleric, put to sleep.[^4] Much more would the [habit of faith]{g:habitus-fidei} lead the faithful man to understand by the consecrated bread the true body of Christ. For so the fathers in the old law believed, through the manna, through the paschal lamb and through the rock flowing with water, the whole [humanity]{g:humanitas} of Christ (as was touched on above from the testimony of Augustine);[^5] therefore much more would the bread sanctified through the words of the Christ who was figured[^6] signify his body more expressly and more principally than itself.

## ¶3 · 144.27–144.29

> [p. 144] Tercia evidencia est ex contrarietate cibi corporalis et huius sacramenti. Cibus enim, inquit, sacramenti esset pure spiritualis et non corporalis quod non competit pani.

The third [evidence]{g:evidencia} is from the contrariety of bodily food and this sacrament. For the food of the sacrament, he says, would be purely spiritual and not bodily, which does not befit bread.[^7]

## ¶4 · 144.30–146.3

> [p. 144] Hic miror, cum illud sacramentum secundum istam sectam sit corpus de genere quantitatis et habet dimensiones et acciones naturales in cibando et [p. 145] nutriendo, quod non foret cibus corporalis, cum non sit aliqua res nisi corporalis et ita grossa sicut panis alius consecrandus. Numquid credimus quod populus posset tantum seduci quod credat omnia bona adiacencia sacerdoti esse transaccidencia destructa tota substancia, et sic sacerdotes Baal viverent pro suo perpetuo sine cibo corporali? Revera deliramentum consimile foret dicere quod totum sacramentum quod Apostolus dicit ebriare et experiencia docet ipsum corporaliter nutrire sit cibus pure spiritualis et non corporalis. Pagani nimirum derident istas stulticias quibus sensus populi captivantur; tantum itaque sacerdos est dispositus ad iterum celebrandum cum paribus, si eque moderate et eque virtuose assumpserit tantumdem de accidentibus vel de pane vel vino non consecratis, sicut assumendo tantum de pane et vino consecratis, communiter tamen sacerdos non assumit hoc sacramentum intencione gule vel gracia nutricionis corporee, licet illa per accidens consequatur, ideo est ad celebrandum disposicior post assumpcionem sacramenti quam communiter est dispositus ad celebrandum post assumpcionem tanti cibarii non sacrati. Et patet quod ista via propter has tres argucias non est competens, sed competencia eius patet ex sanctis doctoribus qui declarant hoc fuisse competens propter sensum misticum quem panis et vini substancia representant. Hereticum itaque foret extinguere illum sensum, sicut stultum est credere quod propter ordinacionem ecclesie ut sumatur eukaristia per ieiunium, quamquam religiosius sumebatur a Christo et discipulis suis post prandium, per hoc, inquam, introducatur lex inpossibilis de accidentibus per se [p. 146] et dissolvatur de accidentibus necessaria lex nature; sufficit enim pro lege hominum quod ieiunium ecclesie sit servatum.

Here I wonder, since that sacrament according to this [sect]{g:secta} is a body of the genus of [quantity]{g:quantitas} and has dimensions and natural actions in feeding and [145] nourishing, that it should not be bodily food, since it is not any thing but bodily, and as gross as other bread that is to be consecrated. Do we believe that the people could be so far seduced as to believe that all the goods lying beside the priest are [transaccidents]{g:transaccidencia},[^8] the whole substance destroyed, and that so the priests of Baal would live in perpetuity without bodily food?[^9] [In truth]{g:revera} it would be a like [raving]{g:deliramenta} to say that the whole sacrament, which the Apostle says makes drunk and experience teaches nourishes [bodily]{g:corporaliter},[^10] is purely spiritual food and not bodily. No wonder the pagans deride these follies by which the senses of the people are taken captive. So a priest is as much disposed to celebrate again, other things being equal, if he has taken, equally moderately and equally virtuously, just as much of accidents, or of bread or wine not consecrated, as by taking as much of consecrated bread and wine. Yet commonly a priest does not take this sacrament with the intention of gluttony or for the sake of bodily nourishment, though that follows [by accident]{g:per-accidens}; therefore he is more disposed to celebrate after the taking of the sacrament than he is commonly disposed to celebrate after the taking of so much food not [hallowed]{g:sacrare}.[^11] And it is clear that this way is not fitting by reason of these three quibbles, but its fittingness is clear from the holy doctors, who [make clear]{g:declarare} that this was fitting on account of the mystical sense which the substance of bread and wine represent.[^12] So it would be heretical to extinguish that sense, just as it is foolish to believe that, because of the [ordinance]{g:ordinacio} of the [church]{g:ecclesia} that the Eucharist be [received]{g:sumpcio} fasting (although it was received more religiously by Christ and his disciples after the meal),[^13] by this, I say, an impossible law about accidents [by themselves]{g:per-se}[^14] [146] should be brought in, and the necessary law of nature about accidents be dissolved. For it suffices for the law of men that the church's fast be kept.

## ¶5 · 146.4–146.24

> [p. 146] Quantum ad illud de inpossibilitate et hereticacione istius sentencie patet quod stolide sine evidencia est proruptum. Et quantum ad porisma quod induceret inpossibilitatem scilicet quod inpossibile est aliquid esse ubi prius non fuit nisi alio in ipsum converso vel ipso mutato, patet quod habet multiplicem calumpniam, primo de Deo qui in principio mundi fuit ubi nunquam prius fuit; et tamen ipse non fuit exinde mutatus nec aliquid in ipsum conversum, et idem contingeret Deo creante corpus extra hunc mundum, quod adversarius dicit absolute necessario posse esse. Et quantum ad secundum corpus patet quod hoc principium habet multas calumpnias; nam supposito (ut loquar ad hominem) quod mille mundi creentur extra hunc mundum totaliter quiescentem, patet quod ista terra erit noviter in mille locis sine hoc quod ipsa pertinenter moveatur vel aliud in ipsam convertatur, ut loyci arguunt de columpna quod continue erit in novo loco vel novo ubi secundum quod ex nova generacione loci, aque vel aeris habebit novum ubicans circumscribens.

As to that point about the impossibility of this [teaching]{g:sentencia}[^15] and its being pronounced heretical, it is clear that it has been blurted out stupidly, without evidence. And as to the [porism]{g:porisma} which would bring in an impossibility, namely that it is impossible for anything to be where it was not before unless another has been converted into it or it has itself been changed, it is clear that it admits manifold [challenge]{g:calumpnia}: first, from God, who at the beginning of the world was where he never was before; and yet he was not changed by it, nor was anything converted into him; and the same would happen with God creating a body outside this world, which the adversary says can be, with absolute necessity.[^16] And as to the second, of a body, it is clear that this principle admits many challenges. For supposing (to speak to the man) that a thousand worlds were created outside this world, which is wholly at rest, it is clear that this earth will newly be in a thousand places without its being [fittingly]{g:pertinenter} moved or anything else being converted into it; as the logicians argue of a column that it will continually be in a new place or a new where, according as, from the new generation of the place, of water or of air, it will have a new thing that circumscribes it and gives it its where.[^17]

## ¶6 · 146.25–147.2

> [p. 146] Iterum, si Deus potest servare accidens sine subiecto, multo magis vel a pari potest servare quodcunque corpus, quocunque eius accidente corrupto, et per consequens Deus potest servare corpus inmotum anichilando vel destruendo eius ubi vel locum et continue creando novum, et per consequens omne corpus potest esse noviter alicubi postquam ipsum non fuit ibi sine conversione in ipsum vel participancia motus sui. Sic enim Deus potest habere [p. 147] quotlibet lumina creata ex amiccione nova sine hoc quod moveatur.

Again, if God can preserve an [accident without a subject]{g:accidens-sine-subiecto}, much more, or from the equal, can he preserve any body whatever when any accident of it whatever has been corrupted; and consequently God can preserve a body unmoved by [annihilating]{g:anichilare} or destroying its where or place and continually creating a new one; and consequently every body can be newly somewhere after it was not there, without [conversion]{g:conversio} into it or a share in its motion.[^18] For so God can have [147] any number of created lights from a new loss, without his being moved.[^19]

## ¶7 · 147.3–147.8

> [p. 147] Et si dicatur (ut dicit ista secta) quod Deus continue movetur extense loquendo de motu iuxta illud Sap. VIIᵐᵒ, 24: Omnibus mobilibus mobilior est sapiencia, revera evidencius diceretur quod corpus Christi movetur, quando acquirit nova loca per transsubstanciacionem.

And if it is said (as this sect says) that God is continually moved, [speaking in an extended sense]{g:extense-loquendo} of motion, according to that of Wisdom 7:24, "Wisdom is more active than all active things",[^20] in truth it would be said more evidently that the body of Christ is moved when it acquires new places through [transubstantiation]{g:transsubstanciacio}.

## Notes

[^1]: **S.** *Obiectum*: the second of the three arguments against the remaining of the bread, set out in Thomas's text quoted at 137.10–18 (*Esset eciam contra significacionem sacramenti*; Loserth: cf. p. 137.17; LEDGER K05h-1). The third follows at 144.27.

[^2]: **T.** "Knowledge" renders Loserth's *noscenciam*; the manuscripts read *innocenciam* (apparatus 143.29), which does not construe. The print is translated.

[^3]: **S.** Thomas on the accidents that remain naturally signifying bread, with the case of the animal that takes the host for bread: Thomas, *In IV Sent.* d. 11 q. 1 a. 1 qc. 1 ad 2 (*species sic remanentes repraesentant aliquo modo substantiam quam prius afficiebant*), cf. *ST* III q. 75 a. 2 ad 3 (*per accidentia cognoscitur ratio substantiae*); the clause on man and beast is in neither, cf. *ST* III q. 80 a. 3 ad 3 (*mus aut canis*) (LEDGER K05h-2). The quotation marks follow Loserth's italics, which set off "illa accidencia" and the clause on man and beast as Thomas's words. D (144.5) reads *tanto illa accidencia remanencia signant*. The quotation is closed after *substanciam panis*: Loserth's italics run on through the man-and-beast clause, which is in neither Thomas text (LEDGER K05h-2).
[^4]: **C.** The sentence is compressed. *Significat* (singular) has the writing body as subject: it signifies the things imposed to signify more than *ipsamet corpora* "the bodies themselves" signify; the alternative construal takes *corpora* as object ("than it signifies the bodies themselves"). *Laycus in clerico* is rendered as the layman's natural reading of the marks being asleep in the lettered reader; the Latin can also be read "which a layman has in [the presence of] a cleric". ABCD write *intelligentes* twice (144.16); D has *signacio* (144.17).

[^5]: **S.** 1 Cor. 10:1–4 underlies the manna and the rock; the earlier citation of Augustine is 122.3–8, *Sermo* 352.3 (PL 39, 1552), which names the manna, the rock and the paschal lamb together (*quicumque in manna Christum intellexerunt … Petra enim Christus … alter agnus occisus ut manducaretur Pascha*); cf. ch. 4, 88.9–21 (LEDGER K05h-3). AB read *fluente* (144.22).

[^6]: **C.** *Verba Christi figurati*: the participle agrees with *Christi*, "of the Christ who was figured" by the manna, the lamb and the rock. Taking *figurati* with *verba* would need *figurata*.

[^7]: **C.** *Inquit*: the adversary whose three arguments Wyclif is answering. E reads *convenit* for *competit* (144.29).

[^8]: **G.** *Transaccidencia*, "transaccidents": a noun of the series *transaccidentacio* "transaccidentation" (no row of its own), here of things that have passed into accidents with their substance destroyed. Loserth's side-note reads "transaccidentated".

[^9]: **S.** The priests of Bel who lived on the food set before the idol, Dan. 14:2–21; Wyclif writes *Baal*.

[^10]: **S.** 1 Cor. 11:21, "and another is drunk" (Loserth's reference). D reads *id est re vera* (145.7).

[^11]: **C.** The argument concerns the rule of fasting before celebrating: by nature the consecrated and unconsecrated elements dispose a priest alike; the difference lies in his intention. *Assumere / assumpcio* here is plain "take / taking", not the assumption of a nature. *Non sacrati*: *sacrare* "hallow", kept apart from *consecrare*. D omits *sacramenti … assumpcionem* (145.21–22, homoeoteleuton).

[^12]: **C.** *Ista via propter has tres argucias non est competens* is rendered literally. It most likely means that the fittingness of the remaining of the bread does not rest on answering the three quibbles but on the saints; the alternative, that the quibbles do not show the way unfitting, comes to the same point. ABC read *propter istas* (145.23).

[^13]: **S.** The supper of Matt. 26:26, Luke 22:20 (*postquam cenavit*); Wyclif writes *post prandium*. ABCD read *religionis* for *religiosius* (145.30).

[^14]: **G.** *Per se*, of accidents existing apart from any subject: the spatial sense of the row, "by themselves".

[^15]: **C.** *Sentencia* "teaching": Wyclif's position that the bread remains, which the adversary pronounced impossible and heretical.

[^16]: **C.** *Quod adversarius dicit absolute necessario posse esse*: that a body outside this world is possible with absolute necessity. The adverbs may also go with *dicit* ("says absolutely and necessarily"). AB omit *fuit* at 146.11; C supplies it in the margin; E reads *fuit qui*.

[^17]: **G.** *Ubi*: the category of "where". *Ubicans circumscribens*: the surrounding body that gives the column its where, rendered by a phrase. ABD read *novum vertans* (146.24). AB read *pertinente* (146.20).

[^18]: **T.** *Participancia*, "share": D reads *participaria*; Loserth's apparatus adds *Recte: participativa* (146.33). The print is translated. One MS reads *et destruendo* (146.29).

[^19]: **T.** *Ex amiccione nova* (*amissio*, "loss") is translated as printed; its sense is uncertain. D reads *ex annunciacione*; A in the margin, in another hand, *ex annichilacione*, "from a new annihilation", which fits the argument of the paragraph (147.1).

[^20]: **S.** Wis. 7:24, without the Vulgate's *enim*. Douay–Rheims "active" renders *mobilibus … mobilior*, which the argument takes in the sense "movable … more movable".
