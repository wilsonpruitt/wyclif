# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 8, part 4: Christ a person from the Godhead; the matter of his conception

## ¶1 · 134.4–135.15

> [p. 134] Hic dicitur quod antecedens primi argumenti est verum et consequens impossibile. Nec est verum quod aliqua creatura preter Christum est primo per se suppositum vel persona; quia in omni persona creata proprietates accidentales individuant ipsam; et sic idem est sibi personalitas et singularitas que ad ipsam consequitur, sicut passio ad subiectum: Christus autem solum personatur per se primo ex¹ divinitate,¹ et non ex proprietatibus accidentalibus ut alia singularia. Ideo non mirum, si sua personalitas recipit singularitates personarum,² ut sibi accidencia, et non ut personalitates. Due ergo nature create eciam in sancto sabato sunt simul utraque eadem persona Verbi. Si ergo persona esset per se superius ad istam naturam, sicut aliquid est ad hominem, tunc sequeretur—si³ Christus est hoc singulare creatum, tunc⁴ est ista persona creata—; sicut sequitur, quod Christus⁵ sit aliquid creatum,⁴ ex hoc quod est natura creata; et tunc foret duplex persona creata, sicut est duplex natura creata, quarum utraque est persona. Modo⁶ autem ille ambe nature, et utraque illarum, sunt eciam pro triduo separate idem individuum, suppositum, et persona: licet enim sint due essencie singulares, tamen sunt unita individua substancia nature humane. Et sophista satis colorate concederet⁷ quod illa persona, demonstrando humanitatem Christi, est persona et natura eterna. Ideo in talibus oportet ultra pronomina significancia pure essencias explicare res de quibus loquitur per terminos specificantes formas.* Quod⁸ si arguitur eo ipso quo ponitur aliqua⁹ per se [p. 135] et completa causa causabilis, sequitur illud causabile: sed natura singularis in Christo cum communi creata substancia sufficit per se et complete causare creatum suppositum: ergo illis positis et unitis, sicut est in Christo, sequitur quod oportet ponere creatam personam ex illis principiis resultantem: nam eiusdem speciei specialissime est creata natura atque substancia in Christo, cuius est in quolibet fratre suo. Sicut ergo Christus est univoce cum Petro racionalis nature individua substancia, sic cum eo univoce est persona: cui enim univocatur diffinicio eciam competit univoce diffinitum. Confirmatur ex hoc quod tractantes pollitice¹ cum Christo acceptarent univoce cum aliis personam suam in testimonium et iudicium et quodlibet aliud opus sensibile vel quodlibet personale. Nec potest fingi quod deitas impedit ista principia ad causandum personam.

Here [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that the [antecedent]{g:antecedens} of the first argument is true and the [consequent]{g:consequens} impossible.[^1] Nor is it true that any creature besides Christ is first and [of itself]{g:per-se} a [supposit]{g:suppositum} or [person]{g:persona}; since in every created person accidental properties individuate it; and so personality and the singularity which follows on it are the same for it, as an [attribute]{g:passio} [follows] on its [subject]{g:subiectum}: but Christ is made a person, first and of itself, only from the [divinity]{g:divinitas}, and not from accidental properties like other singulars. So no wonder if his personality receives the singularities of persons as accidents to it, and not as personalities.[^2] Therefore the two created natures, even on the holy Sabbath, are, each of them together, the same person of the [Word]{g:verbum}.[^3] If, therefore, person were of itself superior to this [nature]{g:natura}, as something is to man, then it would follow — "if Christ is this created singular, then he is this created person" —; as it follows that Christ is something created from the fact that he is a created nature; and then there would be a twofold created person, as there is a twofold created nature, each of which is a person. But as it is, both those natures, and each of them, are, even when separated for [the three days]{g:triduum}, the same [individual]{g:individuum}, supposit, and person: for although they are two singular [essences]{g:essencia}, yet they are a united individual substance of human nature. And a [sophist]{g:sophista} would grant, [with colour enough]{g:colorate}, that that person — pointing to Christ's [humanity]{g:humanitas} — is a person and an eternal nature. So in such matters one must, beyond pronouns signifying pure essences, make explicit the things one speaks of by terms specifying the forms. But if it is argued:[^4] by the very fact that some cause, of itself and complete, of a causable thing is posited, [135] that causable thing follows: now the singular nature in Christ, with the common created substance, suffices of itself and completely to cause a created supposit: therefore, those being posited and united, as is the case in Christ, it follows that one must posit a created person resulting from those principles: for the created nature and substance in Christ is of the same [most special species]{g:species-specialissima} as that in each of his brothers. As, therefore, Christ is univocally with Peter an individual substance of rational nature, so he is univocally a person with him: for to whatever the [definition]{g:diffinicio} belongs univocally, the thing defined also belongs [univocally]{g:univoce}. This is confirmed from this, that those dealing civilly with Christ would accept his person, univocally with others, in testimony and judgment and in any other [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} work or any personal one.[^5] Nor can it be feigned that the [Godhead]{g:deitas} impedes these principles from causing a person.

## ¶2 · 135.16–136.7

> [p. 135] Hic dicitur quod minor est falsa cum nulla creatura causat naturaliter nisi de quanto a posteriori subicitur voluntati divine prius causanti. Sed voluntas divina non potest ex istis causare creatam personam: nam assumpcio impedit, vel verius iuvat ut natura creata sit suppositum increatum. Cum enim omne recipiens denudatur a natura rei recepte, oporteret Verbum vel esse duas personas, ut dixit Nestorius; vel recipiendo personalitatem creatam personalitatem increatam dimittere; quorum utrumque cum sit summe impossibile, sequitur quod natura rei exaltantis naturam assumptam, ut quasi insorbendo, non permittat² eam habere personalitatem creatam. Et hoc intendunt loquentes de suppositacione aliena. Et dicitur quod ista natura est persona gloriosissima, sed illi non competit diffinicio persone create. Pro quo notandum quod substancia est commune in Greco ad ypostasim, usiosim, et usion: quando autem diffinitur persona per nomen substancie intelligitur ypostasis, quam alii differenter explicant per nomen subsistencie. Et sic Christus non est racionalis nature individua subsistencia vel suppositum, [p. 136] nisi suppositum nature increate. Ideo natura assumpta non est formaliter persona sed ydemptice vel ypostatice. Non enim est formaliter ypostasis vel subsistencia, sed substancia vel usion. Ideo in Christo sunt formaliter tres substancie, sed unica subsistencia, sed ille due nature create faciunt unam naturam completam, que est humanitas, non autem personam, cum deest complementum ex parte cause finalis.

Here the answer is that the minor is false, since no creature causes naturally except insofar as it is subjected a posteriori to the divine will, which causes first. Now the divine will cannot cause a created person out of these: for the [assumption]{g:assumpcio} impedes, or more truly helps [to bring it about] that the created nature be an increate supposit. For since every recipient is stripped of the nature of the thing received,[^6] it would be necessary for the Word either to be two persons, as Nestorius said, or, by receiving created personality, to [lay aside]{g:dimittere} the increate personality; and since each of these is supremely impossible, it follows that the nature of the exalting thing, as it were absorbing the assumed nature, does not permit it to have a created personality. And this is what they [maintain]{g:intendere} who speak of "alien [suppositation]{g:suppositacio}". And it is said that this nature is a most glorious person, but the definition of a created person does not belong to it. For which it is to be noted that "substance" is common in Greek to *ypostasis*, *usiosis*, and *usion*: and when person is defined by the name "substance", [hypostasis]{g:ypostasis} is understood, which others explain differently by the name "subsistence". And so Christ is not an individual subsistence of rational nature, or a supposit, [136] except the supposit of the increate nature. So the assumed nature is not formally a person but identically or [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice}. For it is not formally hypostasis or subsistence, but substance or *usion*. So in Christ there are formally three substances, but a single subsistence; but those two created natures make one complete nature, which is the humanity, but not a person, since the completion on the part of the final cause is lacking.[^7]

## ¶3 · 136.8–136.27

> [p. 136] Ulterius conceditur quod conversantes cum Christo putando eum esse univoce personam cum aliis hominibus ex ignorrancia sue divinitatis [acceptarent],¹ univoce personam suam in testimonium.¹ Ideo non potest iudicare² vel conversari civiliter, ut patet alibi, sed omne iudicium suum foret infinitum maioris auctoritatis quam iudicium persone create. Et ita videtur de omni actu personali quem fecit humanitus: auctoritas enim consequitur personam in dignitate et non naturam accionis pure. Et patet quod descripcio persone signata convertitur cum ista racionalis nature incommunicabilis existencia. Incommunicabilis dico quoad supposita, non naturas; et ita subsistencia est contractior quam substancia, ut sumitur ad hec tria. Et ita Trinitas commune principium et commune principatum non est persona vel subsistencia, sed persone vel subsistencie. Nec est color in illa equivocacione, quod divicie sunt substancia racionalis nature, sed substancia restricta ad subsistenciam est consequens ad omne suppositum substanciale. Tales*³ sunt multe argucie quibus decipiuntur incauti, facte pro stabilienda opinione Nestorii.

Further, it is granted that those [living their life]{g:conversari} with Christ, thinking him to be univocally a person with other men out of ignorance of his divinity, [would accept] his person univocally in testimony.[^8] So he cannot judge or live his life civilly, as is clear elsewhere, but all his judgment would be of infinitely greater [authority]{g:auctoritas} than the judgment of a created person. And so it seems of every personal act that he did [humanly]{g:humanitus}: for authority follows the person in dignity, and not the nature of the action purely. And it is clear that the [definition]{g:descripcio} of person here signified is [convertible]{g:converti} with this: "incommunicable existence of rational nature".[^9] Incommunicable, I say, as to supposits, not natures; and so subsistence is narrower than substance, as [substance] is taken with respect to these three. And so the Trinity, common principle and common principality,[^10] is not a person or subsistence, but persons or subsistences. Nor is there [colour]{g:color} in that equivocation, that riches are "substance" of a rational nature,[^11] but substance restricted to subsistence is consequent on every substantial supposit. Of this kind are many [quibbles]{g:argucie} by which the unwary are deceived, made for establishing the [opinion]{g:opinio} of Nestorius.

## ¶4 · 136.28–137.8

> [p. 136] Sed 4°. principaliter arguitur contra ydemptitatem specificam Christi cum aliis ex parte materie. Nam semen decisum⁴ a diversis sexubus et commixtum est alterius speciei quam simplex semen sexus feminei: sed⁵ Christus conceptus est pure ex semine sexus feminei,⁵ omnes autem alii ex commixtis seminibus maris et feminei: ergo materia ex qua [p. 137] factus¹ est Christus est alterius speciei quam materie proxime aliorum: et cum agens semper dat formam secundum condignitatem materie, ut patet philosophis, sequitur quod Christus sit alterius speciei quam alii secundum communem cursum nature univoce² procreati. Confirmatur per Commentatorem 8vo. Physicorum 9. 46. ponentem contra Avicennam quod si homo generaretur ex terra, ipse esset homo equivoce propter extraneacionem materie.

But fourth, [as a main head]{g:principaliter}, it is argued against Christ's [identity of species]{g:ydemptitas-specifica} with the others on the part of matter. For seed cut off from diverse sexes and mingled is of another species than the simple seed of the female sex: now Christ was conceived purely from the seed of the female sex,[^12] but all the others from the mingled seeds of male and female: therefore the matter from which [137] Christ was made is of another species than the [proximate]{g:materia-proxima} matters of the others: and since an agent always gives a form according to the condignity of the matter, as is clear to the philosophers, it follows that Christ is of another species than the others, procreated univocally according to the common course of nature. This is confirmed by the Commentator, *Physics* 8, comment 46,[^13] who holds against Avicenna that if a man were generated from earth, he would be a man equivocally on account of the [estrangement]{g:extraneare} of the matter.

## ¶5 · 137.9–137.21

> [p. 137] Hic dicitur quod illud argumentum deficit in pluribus. Primo in assumpto quod semen muliebre, quod differt a menstruo, non distat a virili semine nisi secundum maius et minus; sic quod semen virile de communi cursu nature sit in substancia spissius, complexione³ calidius, et informacione virtuosius. Sed quale magisterium Deo de beata virgine complexione³ mundissima descindere⁴ semen vel sanguinem pro mensura delectationis sue humilime ex gaudio salutacionis⁵ angelice, que excedat⁶ in bonitate⁷ complexionis⁷ in proporcione multitudinis et in limitacione virtutis omnem aliam materiam, ex qua fuit persona hominis procreata? quod quia factum est, unde non daretur illi capaci⁸ organo spiritus consimilis speciei?

Here the answer is that that argument fails in several respects. First in the [assumption]{g:assumptum}, since female seed, which differs from the menstrual blood, does not differ from male seed except according to more and less;[^14] so that male seed, in the common course of nature, is thicker in substance, hotter in [complexion]{g:complexio}, and more powerful in informing. But what feat of mastery [was it] for God to cut from the blessed virgin, of most pure complexion, seed or blood according to the measure of her most humble delight from the joy of the angelic salutation, such as would exceed in goodness of complexion, in proportion of quantity and in limitation of power, every other matter from which a person of a man was procreated?[^15] And since this was done, why would a spirit of like species not be given to that head, the organ?[^16]

## ¶6 · 137.22–137.32

> [p. 137] Deficit 2°. in sequela quando infert quod materia proxima, de qua factus est Christus, sit alterius speciei quam materie proxime aliorum. Nam⁹ si, sicut est summe⁹ possibile, Deus de lapidibus¹⁰ vel quocumque alio mixto vel simplici descidisset¹¹ materiam, et commiscendo athomos¹² in utero virginali dedisset post¹³ commixturam ydoneam formam complexionalem¹⁴ humani seminis; adhuc proxima materia, ex qua talis homo fieret, foret eiusdem racionis cum aliis, licet remota materia sit diversa: aliter enim semina hominum generata ex distinctis cibariis in specie forent eo ipso disparium specierum.

It fails, second, in [what follows]{g:sequela},[^17] when it infers that the proximate matter of which Christ was made is of another species than the proximate matters of the others. For if, as is supremely possible, God had cut matter from stones[^18] or from any other mixed or simple body, and, by mingling atoms in the virgin's womb, had given, after a suitable mixture, the complexional form of human seed; still the proximate matter from which such a man would be made would be [of the same kind]{g:eiusdem-racionis} as the others, although the remote matter is diverse: for otherwise the seeds of men generated from foods distinct in species would by that very fact be of disparate species.

## ¶7 · 137.33–138.22

> [p. 137] Ex istis 3°. colligitur quod non refert ex qua materia Deus [p. 138] adaptet¹ materiam proximam ad hominem producendum; dum tamen reducat eam ad temperamentum complexionis seminee,² et limitet³ successive vel subito infundendo humanum spiritum in corpus debite limitatum.⁴ Nec refert quo agente Deus ad illud officium utatur pro organo: aliter enim primi parentes, qui sunt principia humani generis, non convenirent cum suis generacionibus in specie vel natura: talis tamen error auctoris in principio †caret⁵ maximum in processu. Patet deduccio ex hoc quod Adam plasmatus est de limo terre forsitan sine⁶ ministerio creature, et Eua de costa viri ab eodem opifice est formata. Sicut ergo non refert utrum ignis ab igne, vel immediate a lumine producatur ad hoc quod sit de natura ignis univoce, et sic de aliis effectibus cuiuscumque speciei vel generis; sic facta de quacumque materia prima complexione et limitacione sufficienti ad animam subiectandam, non refert ad unionem compositi⁷ ministerio cuiuscumque agentis secundi hoc factum fuerit, ut⁸ sive immediato a Deo, sive ministerio angelorum, sive per se a constellacione celesti, sive a quocumque agente secundo corpus humanum productum fuerit; non refert data anima adesse hominis sicut nec refert a quo artifice creato ydolum vel aliud artificiale fuerit⁹ fabricatum.

From these, third, it is gathered that it does not matter from what matter God [138] adapts the proximate matter for producing a man; provided only that he brings it to the temperament of the complexion of seed, and limits [it],[^19] infusing the human spirit, successively or suddenly, into a duly limited body. Nor does it matter what agent God uses as an instrument for that office: for otherwise the first parents, who are the principles of the human race, would not agree with their generations in species or nature: yet such an error of the author at the beginning †caret† [becomes] the greatest in the course.[^20] The [deduction]{g:deduccio} is clear from this, that Adam was formed of the slime of the earth, perhaps without the ministry of a creature, and Eve was formed from the rib of the man by the same craftsman. As, therefore, it does not matter whether fire is produced by fire or [without intermediary]{g:immediate} by light, for it to be univocally of the nature of fire, and so of other effects of whatever species or genus; so, [the body] having been made from any matter whatever with a first complexion and limitation sufficient to be the subject of a soul,[^21] it does not matter for the union of the composite by the ministry of what secondary agent this was done — as whether without intermediary by God, or by the ministry of angels, or of itself by a heavenly constellation, or by whatever secondary agent the human body was produced; it does not matter, the soul being given, for the being of man, just as it does not matter by what created craftsman an image or other artefact was fabricated.

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Primi argumenti*: the first inference of the fourth objection (133.29–31). Its antecedent is true because body and soul are each the person of the Word (134.14–15); its consequent, "Christ recently is a supposit", is impossible.

[^2]: **T.** *Divinitate* "the divinity" (134.11), the print, with O; A B C read *deitate* "the Godhead", a pair the GLOSSARY keeps apart. *Personarum* "of persons" (134.13), the print, with O; A B C read *naturarum*, "the singularities of the natures".

[^3]: **C.** *In sancto sabato*: Holy Saturday, in the three days when body and soul were separated; even then each is the person of the Word (*simul utraque*).

[^4]: **T.** From *Quod si* (134.31) to *Tales* (136.25) O omits the passage, which Harris marks with asterisks; O reads *specificantes formas sunt multe argucie*, and a later hand in its margin noted *deficit multum* "much is lacking" (134 n. 8; 136 n. 3). Here A B C are the only witnesses.

[^5]: **T.** *Pollitice*: A's spelling of *politice*, which Harris glosses *civiliter* "civilly" (cf. 136.12); B *pollce*, C *polletice*.

[^6]: **S.** The principle that what receives lacks the nature of what it receives: Aristotle, *De anima* III.4, 429a (LEDGER I08d-1, to be checked). A *permittant*, B *permittunt* (135.26).

[^7]: **S/C.** Boethius, *Contra Eutychen* 3, on *ousia*, *ousiosis* and *hypostasis* (LEDGER I08d-2, to be checked); Wyclif's Latinised Greek is kept, in italic as Harris prints it. The "three substances" are the Godhead, the body and the soul.

[^8]: **T.** *Acceptarent* "would accept" is Harris's supplement (cf. 135.12): A B leave a gap of twelve letters, C omits from *divinitatis* to *testimonium*, and O lacks the passage (n. 4).

[^9]: **S.** The definition is Richard of St Victor's, as at 131.24–26 (LEDGER I08d-3, to be checked). *Signata*: the definition just signified.

[^10]: **C.** *Commune principatum*: neuter after *commune*; translated as printed ("principality", rule). *Principiatum* "what proceeds from a principle" is a possible reading, not adopted.

[^11]: **C.** *Substancia* also means a man's goods ("his substance"); no plausible ground (*color*, a plausible ground, the GLOSSARY's period sense) comes from that equivocation. So too *satis colorate* (134.26), "with colour enough".

[^12]: **T.** The minor premiss *sed … feminei* (136.31–32) is omitted by A B C; the print has it with O, and the syllogism needs it.

[^13]: **S.** Averroes, *In Physicorum* VIII, comm. 46, against Avicenna on the generation of animals from earth (LEDGER I08d-4, to be checked). The print's "9." is probably the manuscripts' sign for *com*-, so "comment".

[^14]: **C.** *In assumpto quod*: the *quod* clause is Wyclif's ground for rejecting the assumption, not its content; *assumptum* is the logician's sense, not the assumption of the nature. A B C read *commixione* "mixture" for *complexione* (137.13, 137.15).

[^15]: **C.** *Quale magisterium*: a rhetorical question, implying that it is no great feat for God; *magisterium* here is neither "rule" nor "the teaching office".

[^16]: **T.** *Capiti* "head", with C O, against the print's *capaci* "capable" (137.20): agreement across the families, and the reading construes (the head, as organ, receives the spirit). As printed: "to that capable organ".

[^17]: **C.** *Sequela*: the logical sense of the CONTEXTUAL row, "what follows", the step from the premisses to the conclusion about the matter.

[^18]: **S.** Cf. Matt 3:9, "God is able of these stones to raise up children to Abraham".

[^19]: **T.** *Limitet* … *limitatum* (138.3–4), the print, with O; A B C read *liniet* … *liniatum*.

[^20]: **T/S.** *Caret* is O's alone (A B C omit it); Harris daggers it and suggests *cresceret* "would grow" or *foret* "would be". The English supplies "becomes", after the saying that a small error at the beginning is great at the end (Aristotle, *De caelo* I.5, 271b8; LEDGER I08d-5, to be checked). *Auctoris*: the author of the argument.

[^21]: **C.** *Prima* is taken with *complexione* ("a first complexion"), not as *materia prima* "prime matter"; *facta* is referred to the body. *Adesse* (138.21), one word in the print, is *ad esse* "for the being". A B C read *suppositi* for *compositi* (138.17).
