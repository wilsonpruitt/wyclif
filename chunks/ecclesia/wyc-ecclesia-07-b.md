# *De ecclesia*, chapter 7, part 2: the king's command does not oppose God's law, and the privilege does not cover the case

## ¶1 · 146.29–147.24

> [p. 146] Quod autem preceptum regis in hac parte non obviat legi Dei nec veris privilegiis Westmonasterii sic ostendo: Scrutetur homo totam scripturam et non inveniet quod pro debito, pro occisione iniusta, [p. 147] immo generaliter pro quocunque peccato debet pro fugus habere refugium, quia tunc Deus foveret proditorem proprium in suo crimine; quod non potest. Unde Deuteronomii XIX° et Josue XX°, ubi datur lex profugorum et constituuntur civitates refugii, limitatur quod solum occidentes nescii et inmunes criminis debent ibi habere refugium, ne a cognacione occisi effundatur sanguis innoxius, nec habitabant ibi perpetuo, sed Levite habebant custodiam sex civitatum refugii, nec acceptarent nisi insontes qui a casu preter intentum fratrem suum occiderint; illi autem non exibant civitatem nisi ad purgandum se in loco occisionis quod ignoranter occiderint fratrem suum usque ad mortem summi pontificis. Tunc enim redirent libere in domum propriam extra civitatem refugii; patet ista lex Numeri XXXV° et Josue XX°. Si ergo in tota lege nova et veteri (ubi diffuse tangitur ista materia) non limitatur locus refugii nisi insontibus qui casualiter fratrem suum occiderint, que foret in christianis ceca presumpcio sub colore pietatis defendere hostes Dei, regis et regni? Per hec patet responsio ad argumentum quod fundatur in privilegio concesso a rege monasterio supradicto.

Now, that the king's command in this matter does not oppose [God's law]{g:lex dei}, nor the true privileges of Westminster, I show thus.[^1] Let a man search the whole of scripture, and he will not find that for debt, for unjust killing, [147] indeed in general for any sin whatever, a fugitive ought to have refuge; for then God would cherish his own traitor in his crime, which he cannot do.[^2] Hence in Deuteronomy 19 and Joshua 20, where the law of fugitives is given and the cities of refuge are appointed, it is [assigned]{g:limitare} that only those who kill unknowingly and are free of crime ought to have refuge there, lest innocent blood be shed by the kindred of the slain; nor did they dwell there for ever, but the Levites had the custody of the six cities of refuge, and were to accept none but the guiltless who had killed their brother by chance, beyond their intent; and these did not go out of the city, except to clear themselves at the place of the killing [of the charge, showing] that they had killed their brother unknowingly, until the death of the high priest. For then they would return freely to their own house outside the city of refuge; this law is clear in Numbers 35 and Joshua 20.[^3] If, therefore, in the whole law, new and old (where this matter is touched on at length), the place of refuge is assigned only to the guiltless who have killed their brother by chance, what blind presumption would it be in Christians, under [colour]{g:color} of piety, to defend the enemies of God, of the king and of the [kingdom]{g:regnum}? By these things the answer is clear to the argument that is founded on the privilege granted by the king to the aforesaid monastery.[^4]

## ¶2 · 147.25–148.24

> [p. 147] Supponitur autem quod habeat privilegium sub isto tenore verborum quod liceat sibi tueri quoscunque fugitivos ex quacunque causa, eciam in causa lese regie maiestatis, adhuc totum istud est generalis concessio sed secundum leges hominum in generali concessione principis nunquam veniet speciale, nisi quod concederetur a principe in casu quo fuerit particulariter [p. 148] explicatum. Cum ergo non sit in potestate principis concedere quod vergeret ad Dei iniuriam, ad destruccionem regni sui, ad enervacionem regalie et legum per quas regeret regnum suum, patet quod illud suppositum in casu nobis exposito non includitur in illo privilegio generali. Quid queso foret magis ad Dei iniuriam quam facere domum suam speluncam latronum? aut quid foret magis destructivum regnorum quam protegere in nidis ad hoc deputatis hostes Dei ecclesie atque regni. Ecclesiastici X°, 8 scribitur: Regnum de gente in gentem transfertur propter iniusticias, iniurias, contumelias et diversos dolos. Cum ergo secundum sanctos qui emendare potest et negligit absque dubio participem criminis ut sic se constituit, ideo patet quam patenter minaretur ruinam regno talis erroris tolerancia sive defensio. Unde alias ostensum est quod monasterium, habens facultatem ad defendendum proditores Dei, cuiusmodi sunt singuli criminosi, non habet ut sic privilegium, sed pocius corruptelam. Unde sicut monti Ethne non sonat in privilegium quod includat ultra alios montes ignem suimet consumptivum, sic nec dicto monasterio foret privilegium facultas fovendi proditorem Dei tam sui quam totius Anglie destructivum.

And suppose that it has a privilege in this form of words, that it may protect any fugitives whatever for any cause whatever, even in a case of injured royal majesty: still all this is a general [grant]{g:concessio}; but according to the laws of men a special case never comes under a prince's general grant, except what would be granted by the prince in a case that has been particularly [148] set out.[^5] Since, therefore, it is not in a prince's power to grant what would tend to the wrong of God, to the destruction of his kingdom, to the enervation of the regality and of the laws by which he should rule his kingdom, it is clear that what is supposed in the case set before us is not included in that general privilege. What, I ask, would be more to the wrong of God than to make his house "a den of thieves"? Or what would be more destructive of kingdoms than to protect, in nests appointed for this, the enemies of God, of the [church]{g:ecclesia} and of the kingdom?[^6] In Ecclesiasticus 10:8 it is written: "A kingdom is translated from one people to another, because of injustices, and wrongs, and injuries, and divers deceits." Since, therefore, according to the saints, he who can amend and neglects to do so without doubt makes himself, as such, a sharer in the crime, therefore it is clear how plainly the toleration or defence of such an error would threaten ruin to the kingdom.[^7] Hence it has been shown elsewhere that a monastery that has the power to defend traitors of God, such as every criminal is, does not, as such, have a privilege, but rather a corruption. Hence, just as it does not count as a privilege for Mount Etna that it contains, beyond other mountains, a fire that consumes itself, so neither would it be a privilege for the said monastery to have the power of cherishing a traitor of God, destructive both of itself and of all England.[^8]

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 146.29: This command was not so contrary to the law of God.
- 147.1: God's law provides no refuge for debtors or criminals.
- 147.25: It is said that Westminster has a privilege to shelter all criminals,
- 148.1: Such a privilege does not go beyond the explicit intentions of the granter,
- 148.8: or the grant would be invalid as injurious to God and the kingdom.

## Notes

[^1]: **C/T.** *Quod autem … sic ostendo*: "now", since Wyclif here proves a premiss of the second argument, that the king's command is "not opposing but agreeing with God's law" (146.16–17, 07-a). D *debito et pro occisione iniusta nimis generaliter* (146.32). *Pro fugus* (146.33–147.1) is *profugus* divided at the line (OCR scar), translated "a fugitive".

[^2]: **T/R/C.** A margin *Nota de refugiis profugorum insoncium; iniusti occidentis nullus est locus refugii* (147.1). D *quod esse non potest* (147.3). *Proditorem proprium*: his own traitor, one who betrays God himself (cf. *proditores Dei*, 148.20).

[^3]: **S/T/C.** Deut. 19:1–13, Josh. 20 and Num. 35:6–28 are cited, not quoted; the six cities, Num. 35:6. B *Josue X* (147.4); A B *quod soli*, B *occidentes vestigii* (147.6); the print is translated. *Limitatur*: GLOSSARY *limitare*, "assign". *Ad purgandum se … quod ignoranter occiderint*: we take the *quod*-clause as what they clear themselves of; *usque ad mortem summi pontificis* goes with *non exibant* (as Num. 35:25, 28).

[^4]: **G/T.** *Sub colore pietatis*: colour, a plausible ground (GLOSSARY *color*), not "pretext". D *Per hoc* (147.22). *Argumentum quod fundatur in privilegio*: the case for sanctuary from Westminster's royal charter, which the next paragraph takes up.

[^5]: **T/C/S.** D *habet privilegium sub isto colore* (147.25–26); A *teneri* for *tueri* (147.26); the print is translated. *Supponitur*: concessive, "suppose". *Lese regie maiestatis*: injured royal majesty, i.e. treason. The rule that a general grant does not reach a special case is cited from "the laws of men"; its source is to be checked (LEDGER E07b-1).

[^6]: **T/C/S.** D *istud* (148.5). *Illud suppositum*: we take it as the situation supposed in the case (the fugitives' crimes); "that supposed [privilege]" is possible but makes the privilege include itself. *Regalie*: the regality, the king's royal right (no GLOSSARY row; proposed). *Speluncam latronum*: Matt. 21:13, DR "a den of thieves" (alluded; *domum suam*, God's house).

[^7]: **S/T.** Ecclus. 10:8 as DR (Loserth 148.10–11); Wyclif *de gente* for the Vulgate's *a gente*, the same in English. D *Eccl. XX* (148.10–11); A B *transferetur*, "will be translated" (148.11). *Secundum sanctos*: the saying is John VIII in Gratian D.86 c.3, *Facientis culpam proculdubio habet, qui quod potest corrigere negligit emendare* (PL 187:406) (LEDGER E07b-2). D lacks *ideo* (148.15).

[^8]: **C/S.** *Alias ostensum est*: where Wyclif showed this is not identified (LEDGER E07b-3). *Ignem suimet consumptivum*: we take *suimet* as the mountain, "a fire that consumes [the mountain] itself", matching *tam sui … destructivum* of the monastery; "a self-consuming fire" is also possible.
