# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 5, part 5: the heresies about Christ, from the Ebionites to the Eutychians

## ¶1 · 80.13–81.26

> [p. 80] Nam aliqui ponebant Christum esse purum hominem et non Deum: aliqui autem e contra dixerunt ipsum esse purum spiritum et non substanciam corpoream ex corpore et anima compositam. In prima parte erant Ebionite tempore beati Iohannis evangeliste dicentes Christum esse purum hominem et non Deum; et ista est heresis 10 quam recitat Augustinus in libro suo de Heresibus. Post eos consecuti sunt Theodosiani heretici, qui divinitatem Christi conformiter negaverunt; et hec est Heresis 33. Post eos consecuti sunt Pauliniani, qui negaverunt Christum semper fuisse,⁹ quia non antequam fuit natus de virgine, et, ut verbis utar Augustini, ‘Christum non aliquid amplius quam hominem putaverunt’; et illa est Heresis 44. Et post eos consecuti sunt Photinus¹⁰ et Philaster¹⁰ cum suis discipulis eadem heresi dampnati, ut recitat Augustinus Heresi 49. Postea consecuti sunt Arriani et Appollinariste negantes tam Christi divinitatem quam eius¹¹ humanitatem ut¹¹ recitat Augustinus Heresi 49 et 55. Meicagismonite¹² negant cum Arrianis eandem esse substanciam Patris et Filii,¹³ ut recitat Augustinus Heresi 58. Proclianiste¹⁴ et Patriciani¹⁴ negaverunt Christi humanitatem, [p. 81] ut patet Heresibus 60 et 61. Et postea consecuti sunt Priscillianiste¹ dicentes cum Manicheis² quod nulla caro est a Deo bono sed a Deo malo, et per consequens Christus non est Deus³ et homo. Hi autem secundum Augustinum Heresi 70 apocrifa sumpserunt tanquam autentica,⁴ scripturam sacram vertentes ut hodie ad suam heresim confirmandam.⁵ Sed et Sabelliani erraverunt⁶ in divinitate Christi concedentes confuse Patrem esse Filium et Spiritum Sanctum,⁷ seducti paralogismo vocato expositorio, ut ibi recitat Augustinus. Nona heresis,⁸ quam Augustinus recitat 73, posuit divinitatem doluisse⁹⁹ cum figeretur¹⁰ caro Christi in cruce: et isti, sicut Patripassiani, haberent hodie fautores plurimos. Nam ut asserunt negantes formas universales, ista foret racio insolubilis lumini¹¹ naturali—Filius Dei patitur, et ipse est deitas; ergo deitas patitur sive dolet—.Et eadem deduccio valeret Sabellianis—Si Verbum patitur, et ipsum est eadem essencia vel substancia in numero, que¹² Pater vel Donum; ergo uterque¹³ eorum¹³ patitur; et per idem quelibet persona divina indifferenter fuit homo, nascitur de virgine et suscepit omnem predicacionem temporalem, quam Verbum suscepit—.Et¹⁴ confirmacio illius est quod maior est unio Verbi ad naturam divinam ac personarum ad invicem¹⁵ quam est corporis ad animam; sed anima naturaliter compatitur corpori; ergo multo magis tam deitas quam utraque persona compatitur Christo passo.

For some held "Christ is a mere man and not God"; others, on the contrary, said that he was a pure spirit and not a bodily substance composed of body and soul. On the first side were the Ebionites, in the time of blessed John the evangelist, saying "Christ is a mere man and not God"; and this is heresy 10, which Augustine recounts in his book *On Heresies*.[^1] After them came the heretics the Theodotians,[^2] who in like manner denied the [divinity]{g:divinitas} of Christ; and this is heresy 33. After them came the Paulianists, who denied that Christ always was, since [he was] not before he was born of the virgin, and, to use Augustine's words, "thought Christ nothing more than a man"; and that is heresy 44. And after them came Photinus and Philaster[^3] with their disciples, condemned for the same heresy, as Augustine recounts, heresy 49. Afterwards came the Arians and the Apollinarists, denying both the divinity of Christ and his [humanity]{g:humanitas}, as Augustine recounts, heresies 49 and 55. The Metangismonites deny with the Arians that the substance of the Father and of the Son is the same, as Augustine recounts, heresy 58. The Proclianites and the Patricians denied the humanity of Christ, [81] as is clear in heresies 60 and 61. And afterwards came the Priscillianists, saying with the Manichees that no flesh is from the good God but from an evil god, and consequently "Christ is not God and man". And these, according to Augustine, heresy 70, took up [apocrypha]{g:apocrifa} as [authentic]{g:autenticus}, turning holy scripture, as [men do] today, to confirm their heresy. But the Sabellians too erred about the divinity of Christ, granting [indistinctly]{g:confuse} "the Father is the Son and the Holy Spirit", led astray by the [paralogism]{g:paralogismus} called [expository]{g:sillogismus-expositorius},[^4] as Augustine recounts there. A ninth heresy,[^5] which Augustine recounts as 73, held "the divinity suffered pain when the flesh of Christ was fixed to the cross"; and these, like the Patripassians, would have very many supporters today. For, as those who deny universal forms assert, this would be an argument insoluble to the natural light: "The Son of God suffers, and he is [the Godhead]{g:deitas}; therefore the Godhead suffers, or feels pain." And the same deduction would hold good for the Sabellians: "If [the Word]{g:verbum} suffers, and he is the same [essence]{g:essencia} or substance in number as the Father or [the Gift]{g:donum}, then each of them suffers; and by the same token any divine [person]{g:persona} indifferently was man, is born of the virgin, and [took up]{g:suscipere} every temporal [predication]{g:predicacio} which the Word took up." And the confirmation of it is that the [union]{g:unio} of the Word to the divine [nature]{g:natura}, and of the persons to one another, is greater than that of the body to the soul; now the soul naturally suffers with the body; therefore much more do both the Godhead and each [other] person suffer with Christ in his passion.

## ¶2 · 81.27–82.30

> [p. 81] Sed constat nutritis in materia De Universalibus quod substancia, essencia, vel natura divina, quod idem est,¹⁶ non potest pati ab aliquo vel moveri. Nec est color—si persona, que est communis humanitas, patitur, tunc et illa—cum natura communis abstracta non recipit formaliter predicaciones huiusmodi personales. Et multo evidencius non [p. 82] oportet Patrem pati, etsi Filius, qui est alia persona et eadem essencia paciatur. Nec esset possibile Verbum pati, Patre non passo; nisi Verbum esset tam aliquid quod non est¹ Pater, quam persona alia quam est Pater. Unde, cum, Verbum sit assumpta humanitas plus distans a Patre quam corpus ab anima, patet quod nec est color concludere—Si anima compatitur corpori, cum utraque sit eadem persona hominis; quod² per idem Pater compatitur Filio—cum inter eos sit distinccio personalis. Unde tales paralogismi heretici plus placent hodie tam iuvenibus quam adultis quam argumenta solida scripturarum. Unde ignorantibus naturam universalium videtur ista deduccio sophistica insolubile argumentum—Si Petrus ridet, Paulus comedit,³ et sic de aliis actibus personalibus; et omnis homo est natura specifica; ergo illa natura specifica sic ridet⁴ aut aliter personaliter operatur—.Et tales argucie, locum et tempus occupantes, ponunt placentes⁵ plurimis in numerum evidenciarum probabilium, sed indubie ut Tharsites.⁶ Alias autem declaravi in materia De Universalibus, quod communis humanitas non ridet vel comedit, quamvis sit quelibet persona hominis, que sic facit. Correspondenter communis deitas non gignit nec gignitur, nec gaudet humanitus nec tristatur; quamvis sit Pater qui gignit, Verbum quod gignitur et secundum assumptum hominem⁷ supra alios mortales patitur et tristatur. Tales intricaciones sophisticas decet theologis⁸ evitando cognoscere, cum sint superficiales colores venenosas hereses palliantes et incautos in logica in errores plurimos inducentes. Et hec est racio quare Augustinus tam sedule recitat hereses de Christo cum suis evidenciis ut caucius caveantur.⁹

But it is evident to those nourished in the matter *On Universals*[^6] that the divine substance, essence, or nature, which is the same thing, cannot suffer from anything or be moved. Nor is there [colour]{g:color}[^7] for this: "If a person, who is the common humanity, suffers, then that [humanity] does too"; since the common nature, abstracted, does not formally receive personal predications of this kind. And much more evidently it is not [82] necessary that the Father suffer, even if the Son, who is another person and the same essence, suffers. Nor would it be possible for the Word to suffer, the Father not suffering, unless the Word were both something that the Father is not and a person other than the Father is.[^8] Hence, since the Word is the [assumed]{g:assumere} humanity, more distant from the Father than the body from the soul, it is clear that there is no colour for [inferring]{g:concludere}: "If the soul suffers with the body, since each is the same person of the man, then by the same token the Father suffers with the Son"; since between them there is a personal distinction. Hence such heretical paralogisms please both young and grown men more today than the solid arguments of the scriptures. Hence to those ignorant of the nature of universals this sophistical deduction seems an insoluble argument: "If Peter laughs, Paul eats, and so of other personal acts, and every man is [the nature of the species]{g:natura-specifica}, then that nature of the species so laughs, or otherwise acts personally." And such [quibbles]{g:argucie}, occupying place and time, rank themselves, pleasing very many, among probable [evidences]{g:evidencia},[^9] but undoubtedly as Thersites [does].[^10] And I have [made clear]{g:declarare} elsewhere, in the matter *On Universals*, that the common humanity does not laugh or eat, although it is each person of man, who does so.[^11] Correspondingly the common Godhead does not beget nor is begotten, nor does it rejoice [humanly]{g:humanitus} nor grieve; although it is the Father who begets, [and] the Word who is begotten and, as to [the assumed man]{g:homo-assumptus}, suffers and grieves above other mortals. It befits theologians to know such [sophistical entanglements]{g:intricaciones-sophistice} in order to avoid them, since they are superficial colours [cloaking]{g:palliare} poisonous heresies and leading those unwary in logic into very many errors. And this is the reason why Augustine so diligently recounts the heresies about Christ with their evidences: that they may be the more cautiously guarded against.

## ¶3 · 82.31–84.9

> [p. 82] Sed inter omnes hereses Nestoriana et Eutichiana sunt hodie eo diligencius precavende, quo theologi eciam ‘qui videntur aliquid’ sunt iam ad sapiendum predictas hereses [p. 83] proniores. Scribit enim Augustinus de Heresi 89 et 90 in fine libri sui De Heresibus, quod Nestorius episcopus contra fidem catholicam dogmatizare ausus est, Christum Dominum Deum nostrum hominem esse tantum; nec illud, quod ‘mediator Dei et hominum’ effectus est, in utero virginis de Spiritu Sancto fuisse conceptum; sed postea Deum homini¹ fuisse permixtum, nec Deum-hominem fuisse passum sepultumque² dicebat; evacuare³ contendens omne nostrum remedium, quo Verbum Dei hominem sic suscipere dignatus est in utero virginis, ut una persona fieret Dei et hominis; propter quod sic signanter natus, passus, mortuus, et pro nostris peccatis resurgens, ascendit in celum. Vidit enim iste⁴ Nestorius quod humanitas assumpta non fuit divinitas,⁵ et hinc paralogisatus credidit⁶ quod non fuit Verbum Dei, concedens cum heresi 73 quod Verbum non patitur nisi divinitas⁵ paciatur; et sic, cum illam⁷ esse personam sit suppositum,⁷ dixit quod in Christo fuerunt due persone; et per consequens Christus vel⁸ esset aggregatum ex his, vel persona Verbi non passionata humanitus, ut nec passa, mortua, nec sepulta; quia non vidit quomodo due tam distantes nature possent⁹ esse utraque eadem persona. Quidquid autem moderni dixerunt, illo modo intelligunt eum Augustinus, Boecius, Anselmus, et alii capitales doctores concorditer ante Petrum Lombardum.¹⁰ Aliter enim non tam crebro dicerent,¹¹ quod utraque natura tam corpus quam anima est eadem persona hominis; et quod in Christo tam divinitas⁵ quam humanitas est eadem persona Verbi¹²; et quod aliquid,¹³ quod nos sumus, recenter factum est¹⁴ Verbum; ut dicit Augustinus super Ps. 80. Ome. 48, 2° de Visitacione Infirmorum et alibi. Euticiani autem¹⁵ volentes declinare a Nestorianis cum Appollinaristis coinciderant in caribdim, negantes naturam [p. 84] nostram cum proprietatibus et accidentibus ipsam consequentibus per veram humanitatis assumpcionem fuisse in Christo, et per consequens omnes acciones vel denominaciones humanas negant¹ esse in Christo¹ formaliter, nescientes exinde consequi quod² toti corpori Christi mistico auferri oporteat que capiti defuissent; ut, si Christus non patitur vel meretur, multo evidencius nullum³ membrum; cum in capite exemplari oporteat quidquid perfeccionis fuerit⁴ esse in posteriores derivatum.⁴

But among all the heresies the Nestorian and the Eutychian are today to be guarded against the more diligently, as theologians too "who seem [to be] something"[^12] are now the more prone to savour the aforesaid heresies. [83] For Augustine writes of heresies 89 and 90, at the end of his book *On Heresies*,[^13] that Nestorius, a bishop, dared to teach as dogma, against the catholic faith, "Christ, our Lord God, is only a man"; nor [did he say] that that which was made "mediator of God and men" was conceived in the womb of the virgin from the Holy Spirit, but that afterwards God was mingled with the man; nor did he say that the God-man suffered and was buried: striving to empty out all our remedy, by which the Word of God deigned so to take up a man in the womb of the virgin that one person should come to be of God and of man; on account of which, so signally born, having suffered, died, and risen for our sins, he ascended into heaven. For this Nestorius saw that the assumed humanity was not the divinity,[^14] and hence, misled by a paralogism, believed that it was not the Word of God, granting with heresy 73 "the Word does not suffer unless the divinity suffers"; and so, since it had been supposed that it is a person,[^15] he said that in Christ there were two persons; and consequently Christ would either be an aggregate of these, or the person of the Word not humanly [subject to passion]{g:passionatus}, so as neither to have suffered, died, nor been buried: because he did not see how two natures so distant could each be the same person. But whatever the moderns have said, Augustine, Boethius, Anselm, and the other chief doctors before Peter Lombard with one accord understand him in that way. For otherwise they would not so often say that each nature, both body and soul, is the same person of the man; and that in Christ both the divinity and the humanity are the same person of the Word; and that something which we are has newly been made the Word,[^16] as Augustine says on Psalm 80, Homily 48, *On the Visitation of the Sick* 2, and elsewhere.[^17] And the Eutychians, wishing to turn aside from the Nestorians, had fallen with the Apollinarists into Charybdis, denying that our [84] nature, with the properties and accidents that follow on it, was in Christ through a true assumption of the humanity; and consequently they deny that all human actions or [namings]{g:denominacio} are formally in Christ, not knowing that from this it follows that whatever had been lacking to the head must be taken away from the whole [mystical body of Christ]{g:corpus-christi-misticum}: so that, if Christ does not suffer or merit, much more evidently no [member]{g:membrum} [does]; since in the exemplary head whatever there was of perfection must be derived into its posterity.[^18]

## ¶4 · 84.10–84.15

> [p. 84] Unde Iudei tempore Christi laboraverunt in ista heresi negantes Christi divinitatem; ut patet Ioh. 10. ‘Non, inquiunt, de bono opere lapidamus⁵ te, sed de blasfemia; quia tu, homo cum sis, facis te ipsum Deum.’ Sed in ista heresi exorbitant Sarraceni⁶ dicentes Iesum prophetam eximium sed non Deum.

Hence the Jews in the time of Christ laboured in this heresy, denying the divinity of Christ, as is clear in John 10: "For a good work," they say, "we stone thee not, but for blasphemy; because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God."[^19] But in this heresy the Saracens go astray, saying "Jesus is an eminent prophet, but not God".

## ¶5 · 84.16–84.29

> [p. 84] Iste sunt⁷ hereses, quas cum similibus oportet cavere de Christo, confitendo cum ecclesia⁸ duas plenas substancias esse Christum, deitatem⁹ scilicet et humanitatem; ita quod eadem persona sit perfectus Deus et perfectus homo ex anima racionali et carne in persona Verbi¹⁰ secundum ydemptitatem ypostaticam indissolubiliter coniunctis. Et hinc dicit sanctus evangelista 1 Ioh. 4.¹¹ ‘Omnis, inquit, spiritus,¹² qui confitetur Iesum Christum in carne venisse, ex Deo est; et omnis spiritus, qui solvit Iesum, ex Deo non est; et hic est antichristus de quo audistis quoniam¹³ venit, et nunc iam in mundo est.’ Oportet ergo volentem infringere¹⁴ latebras antichristi confiteri deitatem⁹ et humanitatem¹⁵ insolubiliter, personaliter, et formaliter inesse¹⁵ Domino nostro¹⁶ Iesu Christo.

These are the heresies which, with others like them, must be guarded against concerning Christ, confessing with the [church]{g:ecclesia} that two full substances are Christ, namely the Godhead and the humanity; so that the same person is perfect God and perfect man, of a rational soul and flesh joined indissolubly in the person of the Word according to [hypostatic identity]{g:ydemptitas-ypostatica}.[^20] And hence the holy evangelist says, 1 John 4: "Every spirit," he says, "which confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God: and every spirit that dissolveth Jesus is not of God. And this is Antichrist, of whom you have heard that he cometh: and he is now already in the world."[^21] He who would break into the hiding-places of antichrist must therefore confess "the Godhead and the humanity are [inextricably]{g:insolubiliter}, personally, and formally in our Lord Jesus Christ".[^22]

## Notes

[^1]: **S.** Augustine, *De haeresibus*. Wyclif's numbers are kept as he gives them; in the modern numbering the Ebionites are 10, the Theodotians 33, the Paulianists 44, the Photinians 45 (Wyclif 49), the Arians 49, the Apollinarists 55, the Metangismonites 58, the Proclianites 60, the Patricians 61, the Priscillianists 70, the Sabellians 41. To be checked (LEDGER I05e-1).
[^2]: **S.** The print *Theodosiani*; Augustine's *Theodotiani*, followers of Theodotus. The English gives the modern forms of the names throughout (the print *Meicagismonite*, A B C *Meitangismonte*: Metangismonites).
[^3]: **S.** Philaster (Filastrius of Brescia) was no heretic but the heresiologist on whom Augustine draws; the text joins him to Photinus (O *filaster*, A B C *siliaster*). To be checked (LEDGER I05e-2).
[^4]: **C.** The expository syllogism has a singular middle term ("this Godhead is the Father; this Godhead is the Son; therefore the Father is the Son"): the "paralogism" is that form applied to the divine essence.
[^5]: **C.** *Nona heresis* "a ninth heresy": the count is not Augustine's (he numbers it 73) and does not come out at nine on any plain reckoning of the heresies named above; the print is translated.
[^6]: **S.** Wyclif's own *De universalibus* (named again below); the places to be checked (LEDGER I05e-3).
[^7]: **C.** *color*: a plausible ground, here for an inference.
[^8]: **C.** *tam … quam* "both … and": the Word is something the Father is not (the assumed humanity, as the next sentence says) as well as another person. B C read *esset* for the first *est*.
[^9]: **C.** *ponunt placentes*: the print gives *ponunt* no object; taken as reflexive. Harris at the foot suggests *ponuntur placentes* "are placed, pleasing very many"; O reads *placenter* "pleasingly".
[^10]: **S.** Thersites, the railer of the Greek host (*Iliad* 2), proverbial for the worthless man who claims a place among his betters; the print's spelling *Tharsites* (O *tarlites*). Wyclif's source not identified (LEDGER I05e-4).
[^11]: **C.** The subject of *sit* is the common humanity: it is each human person, and the person is what laughs.
[^12]: **S.** Gal. 2:6 (Vulgate *qui videbantur esse aliquid*, Douay–Rheims "who seemed to be some thing"); Wyclif has the present tense and no *esse*.
[^13]: **S.** The chapters on Nestorius and Eutyches are not Augustine's own (he died before either was condemned): they are later additions to *De haeresibus* in its medieval transmission. To be checked (LEDGER I05e-5). "Mediator of God and men": 1 Tim. 2:5.
[^14]: **T.** *divinitas* here and twice below, the print with O; A B C read *deitas* "Godhead", which this edition keeps apart.
[^15]: **C.** *cum illam esse personam sit suppositum*: taken as a perfect passive with an accusative and infinitive, "since it had been supposed that it [the humanity] is a person"; *suppositum* may also be the noun, "since for it to be a person is [for it to be] a supposit". O *illam humanitatem esse personam suppositum*; A B C mark *personam* for transposition.
[^16]: **C.** Either term can be the subject: "something which we are has been made the Word" (Wyclif's order, with the humanity as subject) or "the Word has been made something which we are". O *esse* for *est*.
[^17]: **S.** Augustine on Ps. 80 (*Enarrationes in Psalmos*); "Homily 48" (the collection not named); and the pseudo-Augustinian *De visitatione infirmorum* II. To be checked (LEDGER I05e-6, I05e-7).
[^18]: **T.** *posteros* "posterity", with A B C (O *postores*, the same word), against the print's *posteriores* "later [members]"; the sense hardly differs.
[^19]: **S.** John 10:33 (Douay–Rheims). Wyclif places *non* before *de bono opere* and has no *et* before *quia*.
[^20]: **S.** The words of the Athanasian creed, *perfectus Deus, perfectus homo, ex anima rationali et humana carne subsistens*. To be checked (LEDGER I05e-8).
[^21]: **S.** 1 John 4:2–3 (Douay–Rheims); *solvit Iesum* "dissolveth Jesus" is the verb Wyclif uses of loosing the union.
[^22]: **T.** A B C have *personaliter* alone of the three adverbs; for *infringere* O reads *añfugere* (Harris: *aufugere* "flee"?).
