# *De benedicta incarnacione*, prologue: the matter, its three demands, and the thirteen chapters

## Headnote

**Occasion.** The *Tractatus de benedicta incarnacione* is an early work: a treatise of the schools, with
no word of pope, endowment or friars. Harris (preface §II) follows Shirley in placing it before 1367, in
the first years of Wyclif's doctorate (c. 1363–67), since its position on the eucharist is the first of the
three stages Woodford describes, and since five of the "heresies" said to have been noticed only when
Wyclif became doctor are maintained here [verify]. It cross-refers to the *De universalibus*, and its
opening presents it as the sequel to a *De anima* (1.3) "lately tasted". A medieval catalogue of Oriel
College calls it Wyclif's work *super sententias* [verify], and in the Oriel manuscript it stands among
commentaries on the Lombard: it is the surviving text nearest to Wyclif's lectures on the third book
of the *Sentences*.

**The text.** Edward Harris's edition for the Wyclif Society (1886) rests on five manuscripts: Vienna
384 (**A**, "Prologus magistri Johannis de Incarnacione" in red), Vienna 404 (**B**) and Vienna 387
(**C**), which Beer judged one recension from a common source; Oriel College, Oxford, xv (**O**, English,
c. 1400, in Harris's view the best and probably the oldest, nearer to C than to A or B); and London,
British Library Royal 7 B. iii (**M**), not a copy but a fifteenth-century summary, the course of the
argument with the illustrations and authorities left out. Harris's text is eclectic, "the best reading"
chosen in each case and the rejected readings of A, B, C, O set at the foot of the page, keyed by
figures that stand in the text; he keeps the manuscripts' spelling "even when manifestly incorrect".
The thirteen chapters are Wyclif's own division (1.14 below); the paragraphs are Harris's. Our copy-text
is Harris's printed text, established by transcription and read against the plates. Harris prints a
running English abstract of the treatise down the margin of every page: it is the editor's, not the
text's, and is not reproduced here. At the head of each chapter he repeats, in square brackets, the
prologue's sentence for that chapter, followed by his own English summary; the Latin sentence is
translated, the summary is not. Scripture references in the margin are Harris's, and are used in the
notes.

**The work's move.** The question is whether, and in what sense, the humanity that the Word assumed
*is* Christ, and so whether Christ is a creature. Wyclif answers from the fathers that it is and that he
is, as to his humanity; argues that the Word did not and could not lay aside the nature it assumed, even
in the three days in the tomb; holds Christ to be univocally a man with other men, of one species with
them, and subject to change as a man; and sets this against the "modern doctors", whose positions he
rehearses in order to reconcile them with the ancients.

**Where it is answered.** Harris notes that five of the propositions later listed among Wyclif's first
"heresies" (*Fasciculi zizaniorum*, p. 2) are maintained in this treatise, among them that Christ is first
matter and so the most abject of creatures (p. 25) [verify]. A direct answer to the treatise has not been
traced.

## ¶1 · 1.3–1.13

> [p. 1] Prelibato¹ tractatu De Anima qui introductorius est propter incarnacionis misterium² cognoscendum, restat tractatum De Benedicta Incarnacione operosius aggredi cum diligencia,³ reverencia, et timore. Cum diligencia,³ quia nulla materia est intellectui difficilior, et per consequens oportet mentem dare operam plus attentam: cum reverencia, quia nulla⁴ materia theologica est affectui preciosior, cum sub uno comprehendit involucro tocius creacionis⁵ et recreacionis venerabile sacramentum: et cum timore, quia, sicut in nulla materia quis compendiosius promeretur, sic nullibi facilius aut periculosius aberratur.⁶

The treatise *On the Soul*, which is introductory for the sake of knowing the mystery of the
incarnation, having been tasted beforehand,[^1] it remains to set about the treatise *On the Blessed
Incarnation* more laboriously, with diligence, reverence and fear. With diligence, because no matter is
harder for the understanding, and consequently the mind must give it a more attentive effort; with
reverence, because no theological matter is more precious to the affection, since under one
[wrapping]{g:involucrum} it comprehends the venerable sacrament of the whole creation and
re-creation; and with fear, because, as in no matter does a man earn merit more compendiously, so
nowhere does he stray more easily or more perilously.

## ¶2 · 1.14–1.14

> [p. 1] Dividitur autem tractatus iste⁷ in⁸ 13 capitula.

And this treatise is divided into thirteen chapters.

## ¶3 · 1.15–1.18

> [p. 1] Quorum primum, declarando ex testimoniis sanctorum quod humanitas sit Christus et per consequens creatura, dat sensum dictorum⁹ in scripturis et in¹⁰ symbolo¹¹ que videntur huic opposita.

Of these the first, by showing from the testimonies of the saints that the humanity is Christ, and
consequently a creature, gives the sense of the sayings in the scriptures and in the [creed]{g:symbolum}
that seem opposed to this.[^2]

## ¶4 · 1.19–1.20

> [p. 1] Secundum capitulum¹² declarat quomodo ista sentencia discrepat ab heresi Arriana.

The second chapter shows how this [teaching]{g:sentencia} differs from the Arian heresy.

## ¶5 · 1.21–1.22

> [p. 1] Tertium capitulum ostendit quod Christus fuit homo in triduo et obicit multipliciter et dissolvit.

The third chapter shows that Christ was a man during the three days,[^3] and raises objections in many
ways and resolves them.

## ¶6 · 2.1–2.2

> [p. 2] Quartum capitulum iuxta ponit sentencias modernorum doctorum, ut veritas plus lucescat.

The fourth chapter sets beside it the teachings of the modern doctors, so that the truth may shine out
the more.

## ¶7 · 2.3–2.5

> [p. 2] Quintum capitulum, supponendo quod Verbum non dimisit humanitatem pro triduo, probat¹ quod nec potuit dimittere naturam quam assumpsit.

The fifth chapter, supposing that the Word did not lay aside the humanity for the three days, proves
that neither could it lay aside the nature that it assumed.[^4]

## ¶8 · 2.5–2.8

> [p. 2] Sextum capitulum ostendit quod Christus sit univoce homo cum aliis hominibus;² et respondendo objectibus³ declarat quod Christus est, secundum multorum doctorum⁴ testimonia,⁵ creatura.

The sixth chapter shows that Christ is [univocally]{g:univoce} a man with other men, and in replying
to objections shows that Christ is, according to the testimonies of many doctors, a creature.

## ¶9 · 2.9–2.11

> [p. 2] Septimum capitulum declarando Christi mobilitatem explanat sensum scripture multis ambiguum,⁶ quomodo sapiencia summe mobilis⁷ sit habitu inventa ut homo.

The seventh chapter, by showing Christ's mobility, explains the sense of scripture, ambiguous to
many, how Wisdom, mobile in the highest degree, was "in habit found as a man".[^5]

## ¶10 · 2.12–2.13

> [p. 2] Capitulum octavum obicit tripliciter contra ydemptitatem specificam⁸ Christi cum aliis et⁹ dissolvit.

The eighth chapter objects in three ways against the specific identity of Christ with others,[^6] and
resolves the objections.

## ¶11 · 2.14–2.17

> [p. 2] Capitulum nonum epilogat posicionem de humanitate; et,¹⁰ narrando tres radices causantes modernorum discrepancias, dissolvit tres objectus eorum per¹¹ ordinem.

The ninth chapter recapitulates the position on the humanity; and, by recounting three roots that cause
the disagreements of the moderns, resolves three of their objections in order.

## ¶12 · 2.18–2.20

> [p. 2] Capitulum decimum suadet¹² 12 evidenciis quod humanitas assumpta sit Christus; et hoc¹³ roborat doctorum testimoniis et exemplis.

The tenth chapter [makes the case]{g:suadere} by twelve [evidences]{g:evidencia} that the assumed
humanity is Christ, and strengthens this by the testimonies and examples of the doctors.

## ¶13 · 2.21–2.24

> [p. 2] Capitulum undecimum solvit instancias quibus moderniores doctores videntur fulcire suam sentenciam, recitando decem ludicra que concedentes possibilitatem dimissionis annuunt consequenter.

The eleventh chapter resolves the [objections]{g:instancia} by which the more modern doctors seem to
prop up their teaching, rehearsing ten laughable things to which those who grant the possibility of
the laying-aside assent in consequence.

## ¶14 · 2.25–2.28

> [p. 2] Capitulum duodecimum recitando opiniones varias de assumpcione creature declarat quod, si assumeret multas humanitates, foret¹⁴ multi homines, sicut dictat sentencia sancti Thome.

The twelfth chapter, rehearsing the various [opinions]{g:opinio} on the assumption of a creature, shows
that, if he were to assume many humanities, he would be many men, as the teaching of Saint Thomas
dictates.[^7]

## ¶15 · 2.29–2.32

> [p. 2] Capitulum tredecimum confirmat¹⁵ aliorum sentencias quod¹⁵ tunc nec foret unus homo nec multi homines, sed¹⁶ et unicus atque multi; et sic concordat modernorum sentencias cum antiquis.¹⁷

The thirteenth chapter confirms the teachings of others, that then he would be neither one man nor many
men, but both one and many;[^8] and so it brings the teachings of the moderns into accord with the
ancients.

## Notes

[^1]: **R** *Prelibato tractatu De Anima*: the treatise "tasted beforehand" is Wyclif's own *De anima*,
    which he calls introductory to this one. No work survives under that title; it is usually
    identified with the *De compositione hominis* (ed. Beer, Wyclif Society, 1884) (LEDGER Pprol-1).
    Harris's apparatus records that A, B and C omit the initial letter (left for the rubricator).
[^2]: **C** *humanitas sit Christus*: the thesis of the whole treatise. *Humanitas* is the human
    nature that the Word assumed, body and soul; Wyclif's claim is that it *is* Christ, so that Christ,
    as to it, is a creature. *Sancti* here are the fathers (Augustine above all), as throughout.
[^3]: **G** *in triduo*: the three days between the death and the resurrection, when the body lay in the
    tomb and the soul was parted from it. The question whether Christ was then a man is the Lombard's
    (*Sent.* III d. 22, *Si Christus in morte fuit homo*, PL 192, 803; *verified*, LEDGER Pprol-4),
    and runs through chapters 3 and 5.
[^4]: **G** *dimittere*, "lay aside": to let go the nature assumed, so that the union would be dissolved.
    The noun *dimissio* (2.23) is "the laying-aside". *Assumere / assumpcio* is "assume / assumption" of
    the nature, as in the fathers; not the logician's *assumptum* (GLOSSARY §C).
[^5]: **C** *sapiencia summe mobilis*: Christ as the Wisdom of God, who in the humanity became subject
    to change (*mobilitas*, chapter 7). The quotation is Phil. 2:7, Douay–Rheims "in habit found as a
    man"; Harris gives no reference here (LEDGER Pprol-2).
[^6]: **G** *ydemptitas specifica*: identity of species, that Christ and other men are of one species
    (cf. *univoce homo*, 2.5).
[^7]: **S** Thomas, *Summa theologiae* III q. 3 a. 7, whether one divine person can assume two human
    natures, which concludes that he would then be not one man but several (LEDGER Pprol-3).
[^8]: **C** *nec … unus homo nec multi homines, sed et unicus atque multi*: Wyclif accepts both
    horns, that in the case supposed Christ would be one (as the one person) and many (as the many
    humanities), and uses this to reconcile the moderns' teaching with Thomas's.
