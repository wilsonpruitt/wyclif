# *De ecclesia*, chapter 11, part 4: the second objection: who deserves refuge; debtors, the forty days and Westminster; the third objection begun

## ¶1 · 242.19–243.10

> [p. 242] Secundo obicitur per hoc quod iuxta istam senten- ciam excluderetur libertas privilegii, eo quod nemo sciret qui essent digni aut indigni, qui puniendi aut qui dimittendi, aut qualis vel quanta debet esse remissio. In ampliacione ergo privilegii est via securior Hic dicitur negando assumptum et consequenciam sequentem; nam locus potest ex effectu probabiliter COgnoscere qui fugitivi sunt fructuose penitentes, et solum tales prius criminosi debent ex lege Dei et iuris ecclesie habere in illa refugium. Nam fugientes illuc cum ere alieno et negantes restitucionem quantumcunque habundant ostendunt se incapaces refugii, et sic de cunctis facinorosis qui non disponunt se quantum sufficiunt ad emendam. Et si obicitur quod [p. 243] ex talibus signis sensibilibus non fit demonstracio, cum potest esse sensus illusio vel aliunde decepcio, dicitur quod obieccio est inutilis, cum non requiritur talis infallibilis noticia ad elemosinas faciendas. Tunc enim periret omnis correccio et omnis sentenciacio secundum leges humanas et omnis elemosina, cum perinde homo dubitaret, si ille cui faceret elemosinam sit diabolus incorporatus vel membrum eius; cuius defensio foret contra voluntatem Dei, cedens ad perturbacionem ecclesie.

Second, [it is objected, by appeal to]{g:obicitur per} this: that according to this [teaching]{g:sentencia} the liberty of the [privilege]{g:privilegium} would be excluded, since no one would know who were worthy or unworthy, who were to be punished or who released, or of what kind or how great the remission ought to be. In the enlarging of the privilege, therefore, lies the safer way.[^1] To this [the answer is]{g:dicitur} by denying the [assumption]{g:assumptum} and the [inference]{g:consequencia} that follows; for the place can know with probability, from the effect, which fugitives are fruitfully penitent, and only such, though formerly criminals, ought by God's law and the [law]{g:ius} of the [church]{g:ecclesia} to have refuge in it. For those who flee there with another's money and refuse restitution, however much they abound, show themselves incapable of refuge; and so of all those guilty of [misdeed]{g:facinus} who do not dispose themselves to amendment as far as they are able.[^2] And if it is objected that [243] from such [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} signs no demonstration is made, since there can be illusion of the sense or deception from elsewhere, the answer is that the objection is useless, since such infallible knowledge is not required for giving [alms]{g:elemosina}. For then all correction, and all pronouncing of sentence according to human laws, and all almsgiving would perish, since a man would equally doubt whether the one to whom he gave alms were a devil incarnate or a [member]{g:membrum} of the devil; and the defence of such a one would be against the will of God, tending to the disturbance of the church.[^3]

## ¶2 · 243.11–245.25

> [p. 243] Unde cum dilatacio talis non foret ampliacio privilegii sed perversio privilegii, pars securior foret tenenda, innitendo iuri communi in cuius execucione exclusa est undequaque iniuria. Ulterius, si queratur de limitibus remissionis talium profugorum, videtur mihi quod pium esset, quoad debitum pecuniarum, docto quod fugitivi sine suo demerito sint casualiter impotentes, ut ex naufragio, incendio vel rapina, quod a suis creditoribus et specialiter a mundi divitibus et clericis dimittantur liberi, vel totaliter vel in parte; egenis autem creditoribus satisfaciant suo laboricio paulative. Unde mirum est pluribus quomodo extortus fuit sensus privilegii ad debitum pecuniarum, cum ante XXVᵘᵐ annum Edwardi IIIⁱ non esset pro tali debito ad ecclesiam fugiendum, privilegiantes autem per multos annos ante illud tempus non intenderant, sicut nec poterant, ut nullus debitor illuc fugiens teneatur satisfacere creditori. Nam XIª q. IIIª ex auctoritate beati Isidori sic habetur: Qui consentit peccantibus et defendit aliquem delinquentem maledictus erit aput Deum et homines et corripietur increpacione severissima. Hinc eciam quidam sanctissimus [p. 244] pater ait: Si quis peccantem defenderit, acrius quam ille qui peccavit coherceatur. Hinc eciam alius pater ait: Si quis alterius errori consenserit, sciat se cum illo simili modo culpabilem iudicandum. Sufficit ergo prepositis peccatum proprium etsi non cumulent alienum. Insuper aliud mirum est quomodo extortus est sensus nephandus ex dicto privilegio quod licet sceleratissimis cubare ibidem in suo crimine sine correpcione penali pro tota suo periodo, cum nec verba privilegii illud annuerint. Nam potissime extorqueretur de isto verbo quod habeant ibidem omnimodam libertatem. Sed revera ex istis verbis patenter infertur oppositum, cum libertas ad pausandum in peccatis non foret libertas, eo quod tunc spiritus infernales forent liberrimi. Et si queratur quantam libertatem habebunt ibidem, dicitur quod magna libertas est concessa a regibus ecclesie Anglicane, ultra hoc quod habent regna alia vel exprimunt iura canonica, scilicet quod quilibet fugitivus ad quamcunque ecclesiam in Anglia pro qualibet felonia habeat ibidem tempus quadraginta dierum instar Ninivitarum et penitencium in Quadragesima quibus tot tempora penitencie sunt concessa. Pro illo tamen loco excedit illud privilegium in duobus: primo in hoc quod, ubi in aliis locis restringitur situs ad basilicam Vel paucos passus extra ecclesiam, ibi possunt secure quiescere per totum precinctum Westmonasterii, ubi eciam in aliis ecclesiis limitatur causa fuge ad feloniam, ibi extenditur ad quodcunque facinus vel delictum, cum quo stat regni prosperitas et integritas regalie. Si autem placeret regi et suo consilio concedere fugitivis illius loci libertatem maiorem, gauderem et assidue procurarem, sed (si non fallor) ex debito sensu verborum privilegii non potest homo convincere ampliorem. [p. 245] Et si arguitur quod privilegium de immunitate ecclesie Anglicane repugnat iuribus canonicis supradictis, dicitur quod non, quia omnes ille leges sunt locales, obligantes tantum eos qui ipsas acceptant, tam in materia quam in forma. Anglia autem quia prius habuit consuetudinem misericordius libertantem ecclesiam, ideo dimissa martirizacione sui populi venit coronarius ad fugitivum, querens causam sui aufugii; quod si pro alio delicto quam felonia aufugerit, libertas ecclesie non ad eum. Si autem pro felonia, dum tamen non repugnat provido statui regni, licet sibi per quadraginta dies libere ibi quiescere et in fine unum ex tribus eligere, vel per stratam regiam tamquam exulans exire provinciam, vel publice exire, stando tamquam indigena iuri regni, vel tercio remanere infra basilicam sine hoc quod ultra de cibariis provincie sustentetur. Et (ut mihi videtur) privilegium illud foret misericordius et racionabilius quam privilegium notans casus in quibus salvandi in Anglia extracti sunt alibi et occisi. Ideo laudemus nostros principes, non vendicantes alieno iure, quod consuetudo Anglie non permittit; nam Angliam non obligant aliena privilegia alleganda, sed sufficit nobis fructus ulterioris privilegii, nec est aliqua lex fundabilis pro ambobus.

Hence, since such an extension would be not an enlarging of the privilege but a perversion of the privilege, the safer part should be held, relying on the common law, in whose execution injury is excluded on every side. Further, if it is asked about the [limits]{g:limites} of the remission of such refugees, it seems to me that it would be pious, as regards a debt of money, once it is shown that the fugitives are by mischance unable [to pay] without demerit of their own, as from shipwreck, fire or robbery, that they be released free by their creditors, and especially by the rich of the world and by clerics, either wholly or in part; and to needy creditors they should make satisfaction by their labour, little by little.[^4] Hence many wonder how the sense of the privilege was wrested to a debt of money, since before the twenty-fifth year of Edward III there was no fleeing to a church for such a debt; and those who [privileged]{g:privilegiare}, for many years before that time, had not intended, as neither could they, that no debtor fleeing there should be bound to satisfy his creditor.[^5] For in C. 11 q. 3, on the authority of blessed Isidore, it is had thus: "He who consents to sinners and defends anyone offending will be cursed before God and men, and will be rebuked with the severest reproof." Hence also a certain most holy [244] father says: "If anyone defends a sinner, let him be coerced more sharply than he who sinned." Hence also another father says: "If anyone consents to another's error, let him know that he is to be judged culpable in like manner with him." Their own sin, therefore, is enough for [superiors]{g:prepositi}, even if they do not heap up another's.[^6] Moreover, it is another marvel how a wicked sense has been wrested from the said privilege: that the most criminal may lie there in their crime, without penal correction, for their whole term, since not even the words of the privilege granted that. For it would be wrested above all from this word: that they have there liberty of every kind. But in truth the opposite is plainly inferred from these words, since a liberty to rest in sins would not be liberty, because then the infernal spirits would be the most free. And if it is asked how much liberty they will have there, the answer is that a great liberty has been granted by the kings to the English church, beyond what other kingdoms have or the canon laws express: namely, that every fugitive to any church in England for any felony has a period of forty days there, like the Ninevites and the penitents in Lent, to whom so long a time of penance has been granted.[^7] For that place, however, the privilege exceeds this in two things: first in this, that whereas in other places the site is restricted to the basilica or a few paces outside the church, there they can rest secure through the whole precinct of Westminster; [second, that] whereas in other churches also the cause of flight is [limited]{g:limitare} to felony, there it is extended to any misdeed or offence whatever with which the prosperity of the kingdom and the integrity of the [regality]{g:regalia} can stand. If, however, it should please the king and his council to grant the fugitives of that place a greater liberty, I would rejoice and would assiduously procure it; but (if I am not mistaken) no one can prove a more ample one from the due sense of the words of the privilege.[^8] [245] And if it is argued that the privilege of immunity of the English church conflicts with the canon laws mentioned above, the answer is that it does not, because all those laws are local, binding only those who accept them, both in matter and in form. But England, because it earlier had a [custom]{g:consuetudo} that gave the church liberty more mercifully: therefore, the martyring of its people being set aside, the coroner comes to the fugitive, asking the cause of his flight; and if he has fled for another offence than felony, the liberty of the church [does] not [extend] to him. But if for felony, provided only that it does not conflict with the provident state of the kingdom, he may rest freely there for forty days and at the end choose one of three [things]: either to leave the province by the king's highway as one going into exile; or to come out publicly, standing to the law of the kingdom as a native; or, third, to remain within the basilica, without being further sustained from the victuals of the province.[^9] And (as it seems to me) that privilege would be more merciful and more reasonable than the privilege which marks out cases in which men who were to be saved in England have been dragged out elsewhere and killed. Therefore let us praise our princes, who do not lay claim by a foreign law, which the custom of England does not permit; for foreign privileges that may be [alleged]{g:allegare} do not bind England, but the fruit of the further privilege suffices us, nor is there any law that can be founded for both.[^10]

## ¶3 · 245.26–245.33

> [p. 245] Tercio principaliter arguitur contra dicta de descripcione privilegii: videtur enim primo quod omnis lex sit privilegium, cum omnis lex individua sit lex privata, concessa ad utilitatem ecclesie vel persone. Videtur secundo quod non subiacet potestati dominorum temporalium statuere ecclesiam in privilegiis que Christus instituit, quia illa erant dona gracie que solius Dei est concedere.

Third, it is argued principally against what was said about the [definition]{g:descripcio} of privilege: for it seems, first, that every law is a privilege, since every individual law is a private law, granted for the benefit of the church or of a person. It seems, second, that it does not lie within the power of [temporal lords]{g:temporales domini} to establish the church in the privileges which Christ instituted, because those were gifts of grace which it is God's alone to grant.[^11]

## ¶4 · 246.1–246.25

> [p. 246] Tercio videtur quod omnis infirmitas vel peccatum sit privilegium, quia cedit ad utilitatem sancte matris ecclesie. Hic dicitur negando assumptum, quia lex communissima non est privilegium, nisi equivocando in predicacione secundum essenciam vocetur omne quod obiective terminat utilitatem privatam, vel privilegiat melioratum privilegium, sicut Deus dicitur gaudium et premium beatorum. Loquendo autem formaliter de privilegio, patet supra dicta descripcio in iure canonico; nam XXVª, q. I, Ideo permittente domino, exprimitur supradictum genus privilegii sub hiis verbis: Privilegia enim dicuntur quasi privata legia, eo quod privatam legem singulis generent. Quam diffinicionem Gregorius innuens ait: Si hoc neque consuetudine generali neque privilegio speciali vendicas, restat te usurpare quod fecisti. Et patet ex iure canonico genus privilegii supradictum. Superaddidi eciam differenciam pro explanacione maiori quod est concessa a principe ad utilitatem ecclesie vel persone, et potest ista differencia pertinenter capi de iure canonico XIXª, q. II, Due sunt, ubi distinguens inter legem publicam et privatam dicit quod lex privata est que instinctu spiritus sancti in corde scribitur, sicut de quibusdam loquitur Apostolus: Qui habent legem Dei scriptam in cordibus suis.

Third, it seems that every infirmity or sin is a privilege, because it tends to the benefit of holy mother church. To this the answer is by denying the assumption, because the most common law is not a privilege, unless, by equivocating in predication according to essence, everything that objectively terminates a private benefit should be called [a privilege], or [whatever] privileges the one bettered [by it], as God is called the joy and reward of the blessed.[^12] But speaking formally of privilege, the definition given above is clear in canon law; for in C. 25 q. 1, *Ideo permittente domino*, the genus of privilege stated above is expressed in these words: "For privileges are so called as being private laws, because they produce a private law for individuals." Hinting at this definition Gregory says: "If you claim this neither by general custom nor by special privilege, it remains that you have usurped what you did."[^13] And the genus of privilege stated above is clear from canon law. I also added, for fuller explanation, the [difference]{g:differencia}: that it is granted by a prince for the benefit of the church or of a person; and this difference can pertinently be taken from canon law, C. 19 q. 2, *Due sunt*, where, distinguishing between public and private law, it says that private law is that which is written in the heart by the prompting of the Holy Spirit, as the Apostle speaks of some: "Who have the law of God written in their hearts."[^14]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** A in the margin *Contra. Secunda obieccio* (242.19); side-note 242.19. *Istam sentenciam*: the teaching of 11-c, that the privilege is to be interpreted by the rules of charity, so that the unrepentant criminal is unworthy of the church's aid. *In ampliacione … securior*: the objector's conclusion: better to enlarge the privilege than to restrict it.

[^2]: **C/T.** A in the margin *Responsio* and *Nota, qui fugitivi debent gaudere de immunitate ecclesiarum, quia penitentes etc.* (242.24–25); side-note 242.24. *Consequenciam sequentem*: the inference to "the safer way". *Ex lege Dei et iuris ecclesie*: the genitive *iuris* is taken as parallel to *Dei*, "by the law of God and of the law of the church", i.e. by God's law and the church's. *Cum ere alieno*: *aes alienum*, debt; "another's money" keeps the figure. B reads *sed quantum* (242.31–32).

[^3]: **C.** *Sentenciacio*: judicial sentencing, the noun of *sentenciare*; see the report. *Membrum eius*: *eius* taken as the devil's; it could refer to the almsgiver ("his member"), less likely. *Cuius defensio*: the defence of such a person, i.e. of the devil's member, the unrepentant fugitive.

[^4]: **C.** Side-note 243.14. *Docto quod*: ablative absolute, "it having been shown that". *Casualiter*: by chance, without fault. *Laboricio*: by labour. *Clericis*: clerics among the creditors, who should forgive.

[^5]: **R.** Side-note 243.22. The twenty-fifth year of Edward III is 1351–52; that sanctuary for debt began then is Wyclif's claim, and what enactment he means is to be checked (LEDGER E11d-1). *Privilegiantes*: those who granted the privilege (here, of Westminster).

[^6]: **S/T.** C. 11 q. 3 c. 100, attributed to Isidore (Loserth, 243.29; LEDGER E11d-2). The manuscripts read *maledictus est*; Loserth corrects to *erit* from the Decretum (243.30), and his text is translated. The Decretum reads *defendit* where Wyclif has *defenderit* in the second saying (Loserth, 244.1). The two further sayings, of "a certain most holy father" and "another father", stand inside c. 100 itself (*Si quis peccantem defendit …*; *Si quis alterius errori consentit …*; Friedberg I, 671), where the Decretum has *defendit*, *consentit* (LEDGER E11d-3). A in the margin *Contra defensores peccancium* and *Ysidorus* (243.27, 29). *Prepositis*: those set over the place of refuge, the abbot and convent.

[^7]: **C/S.** *Pro tota suo periodo*: for their whole term of refuge. *Omnimodam libertatem*: the words of the Westminster privilege as Wyclif reports them (cf. 11-c, 239.6). The forty days: the term of the English common-law sanctuary; Jonah 3:4 ("Yet forty days and Ninive shall be destroyed") for the Ninevites. Side-note 244.16.

[^8]: **C/T.** Side-note 244.25. *Cum quo stat*: "with which [the prosperity of the realm] can stand", i.e. consistent with it. A and A₁ lack *homo*; A₁ reads *committere*; A and B read *convicere*, and Loserth corrects to *convincere* (244.34), translated: "prove" (convict the words of a larger sense).

[^9]: **C/T.** *Anglia autem … venit coronarius*: an anacoluthon; *Anglia* has no verb, and the sentence resumes with the coroner. *Dimissa martirizacione sui populi*: the alternative to killing the fugitive. *Libertas ecclesie non ad eum*: no verb; "extends" is supplied. A and B read *felononia* (245.9); A and A₁ lack *non* in *dum tamen non repugnat* (245.11), and the print is translated. The three choices are abjuring the realm, surrendering to trial, and remaining in the church unfed: the English law of sanctuary as in Bracton (ed. Thorne II, 382–83: abjuration before the coroner, or "food be denied him") (LEDGER E11d-4). B in the margin *Pena maledicti* (245.11); side-notes 245.7, 245.13.

[^10]: **C.** *Privilegium notans casus …*: taken as a (foreign or papal) privilege that specifies cases; the referent is uncertain. *Non vendicantes alieno iure*: "who do not claim by a foreign law"; *quod* is taken as referring to such claiming. *Ulterioris privilegii*: "the further privilege", i.e. the English one described, set beside the canon laws; the comparison is loose. *Pro ambobus*: for both England and other realms.

[^11]: **C/T.** A in the margin *Contra. Tercia obieccio. Arguitur contra descripcionem privilegii* (245.26); side-notes 245.26, 245.30. The definition is that of cap. 8: private law granted by a prince for the benefit of the church or a person. A and A₁ read *privata et concessa* (245.29). *Lex individua*: a law in a single instance, as against a law in general.

[^12]: **C/T.** Loserth's alinea letter is wanting in the manuscript (246.1). B reads *quia concessit ad* (246.2). A in the margin *Responsio* (246.3); side-note 246.1. *Lex communissima*: the most common law, which binds everything (11-e, 246.26 ff.). *Vel privilegiat melioratum privilegium*: construed as a second thing that might equivocally be called privilege, whatever privileges, *melioratum* being the one bettered; *privilegium* may instead be the predicate ("or [if] what privileges the one bettered [is called] privilege"). The likeness: God is called the reward of the blessed not formally but as object.

[^13]: **S/T.** C. 25 q. 1 c. 16, *Ideo permittente* (Loserth, 246.10; our modern form; LEDGER E11d-5). The manuscripts read *XXV, q. II*; Loserth corrects to q. 1 (246.10), translated. B reads *privatam legem* and omits *legia … legem* (246.12–13). The etymology *privata legia* (lex) is Isidore's (*Etym.* V.18, PL 82:202) (LEDGER E11d-6). B in the margin *Ethymologia privilegii* (246.4). The Gregory saying is in Gratian's dictum after c. 16, § 2 (*Quam distinctionem Gregorius innuens ait*; Friedberg I, 1011–12), not a canon; the letter is not identified (LEDGER E11d-7). A in the margin *Gregorius* (246.10).

[^14]: **S/C.** C. 19 q. 2 c. 2, *Due sunt* (Loserth, 246.21; LEDGER E11d-8). *Differenciam*: the logical differentia that completes the definition after the genus.
