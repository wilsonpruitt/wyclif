# *De eucharistia*, prologue, part 1: the compendium of chapters one to four

## Headnote

**Occasion.** Loserth (Introd. §4, pp. LX–LXII) puts it in the last four years of Wyclif's life
and concludes that it was written "before 1382–1383". It shares passages word for word with the
third part of the *Sermones* (he sets 325 beside *Sermo* III.25), so he takes the two to be of one
time; *De blasphemia*, which quotes it, is later. His third argument, that the treatise never calls
the bread *substancia* or *materialis*, words Wyclif says in *Sermo* III.54 he has stopped using,
does not hold for the printed text: Wyclif writes *substancia panis* in his own voice (34.19,
131.19). For the controversy itself Loserth follows F. D. Matthew (Introd. §1, from *EHR* 1890),
who puts the Oxford condemnation before Chancellor Berton left office on 30 May 1381 and its
beginning perhaps in 1380. Modern accounts place the treatise c. 1379–80, when his denial of
transubstantiation first became public at Oxford `[verify]`.

**The text.** Loserth's edition (Wyclif Society, 1892) prints the treatise from five
manuscripts (Introd. §5): **A** Vienna 3927; **B** Vienna 3932; **C** Vienna 1387, "the best of
the three" Vienna manuscripts; **D** Prague, University Library IV D 22 (1404–05, copied by
Andreas of Kouřim); **E** Prague, University Library XI E 3 (1460), in Loserth's judgement "the
best and most reliable text of all the five". The Vienna manuscripts form one group, the Prague
manuscripts another; the lettering "does not in any way refer to their respective value". Our
copy-text is Loserth's printed text, read from the plates. In the printed scripture references
the chapter numbers are Wyclif's; the verse numbers are Loserth's.

**The volume.** Before the treatise stands a medieval compendium (*Sentencia tractatus de
Eukaristia in compendio*, pp. 1–10), which came down with the work and summarises its nine
chapters; this chunk gives its summary of chapters 1–4. The volume also prints *De eucharistia et
penitencia sive de confessione* (pp. 327–343), whose manuscripts carry their own sigla (Introd.
§6), and the first part of the *Questio ad fratres de sacramento altaris* (pp. 347–348); each has
its own headnote.

**The move.** The compendium shows how the case is built. The first chapter clears the ground: it
answers the pagan and the believing objections, distinguishes bodily from spiritual eating, and
argues that the body of Christ is not seen or sensed in the host with the bodily senses but remains
there invisibly. The second asks what the consecrated host is, sets the Roman church's answer,
"bread and wine", against "quantity" and "quality", and holds the decree *Ego Berengarius* (*De
cons.* D. 2 c. 42) by distinguishing identical from tropical predication. The third argues that
transubstantiation cannot be founded in scripture and that an accident cannot be without a subject.
The fourth turns to the words of consecration, how they are true, who can consecrate, and the order
in which the questions must be asked. Its adversary throughout is the "modern" doctrine of
transubstantiation and the friars who teach it `[verify]`.

**Where it is answered.** Wyclif's eucharistic teaching was condemned at Oxford by the
chancellor's commission in 1381 and at the Blackfriars Council in London in 1382 `[verify]`. The
two Prague manuscripts witness its reading in Bohemia, where Hus and his successors took up the
question `[verify]`. No complete English translation is known to us `[verify]`.

## ¶1 · 1.2–2.5

> [p. 1] Sentencia tractatus de Eukaristia in compendio sic habetur: Capitulum primum premittens distinccionem trimembrem de rebus huius sacramenti solvit tria argumenta paganica. Secundo recitat tria argumenta fidelium et dissolvit. Et ponendo distinccionem inter esum corporalem et spiritualem obicit quod homo videt corpus Christi in sacramento oculo corporali, et solvit. Secundo obicitur per hoc quod multi sancti doctores crediderant quod sensierunt corpus Domini in Eukaristia; et solvendo narrat quomodo finguntur miracula et quomodo populus ex ignorancia consequencie facte de miraculis est seductus. Tercio obiciendo per hoc miraculum narratum a beato Gregorio dicit quod per hoc non docetur quod hostia sit corpus Christi nec quod ille de quo fingitur miraculum propterea fuit sanctus. Et probat tripliciter quod homo non videt corpus Christi in Eukaristia oculo corporali et discurrit per alios sensus deducendo ad inconveniens, concesso quod corpus Domini sacramentaliter senciatur. Ulterius probat quadrupliciter ex testimonio sanctorum et legum ecclesie quomodo corpus Domini manet invisibiliter in hostia consecrata. Finaliter obicit contra hoc tripliciter per leges ecclesie quod corpus [p. 2] Domini est ibi sensibile. Et dissolvit, primo per decretum pape quomodo agendum est effuso sanguine de calice, secundo per confessionem Beringarii, et tercio per decretum Jeronimi; et solvit per duplicem maneriem predicandi.

The [teaching]{g:sentencia}[^1] of the treatise on the eucharist is held in compendium thus. The first
chapter, premising a three-membered distinction concerning the things of this sacrament,
resolves three pagan arguments. Second, it recites three arguments of the faithful and resolves
them.[^2] And, laying down a distinction between bodily and spiritual eating, it objects that a man
sees the body of Christ in the sacrament with the bodily eye, and resolves. Second, [it is objected,
by appeal to]{g:obicitur-per} this: that many holy doctors had believed that they perceived the
body of the Lord in the eucharist; and in resolving it relates how miracles are feigned, and how
the people have been led astray through ignorance of the [inference]{g:consequencia}[^3] drawn from
miracles. Third, objecting by appeal to the miracle related by blessed Gregory,[^4] it says that
this does not teach that the host is the body of Christ, nor that he of whom the miracle is feigned
was holy on that account. And it proves in three ways that a man does not see the body of Christ in
the eucharist with the bodily eye, and runs through the other senses, deducing [the argument] to an
[absurdity]{g:inconveniens},[^5] granted that the body of the Lord is perceived sacramentally. Further
it proves in four ways, from the testimony of [the saints]{g:sancti} and of the laws of the [church]{g:ecclesia},
how the body of the Lord remains invisibly in the consecrated host. Finally it objects against this
in three ways, by appeal to the laws of the church, that the body [2] of the Lord is
[perceptible]{g:sensibilis} there. And it resolves [them]: first [the argument] from the decree of the pope
on what is to be done when the blood is spilt from the chalice,[^6] second [that] from the confession of
Berengar,[^7] and third [that] from the decree of Jerome;[^8] and it resolves by a twofold
[kind]{g:maneries} of predicating.[^9]

## ¶2 · 2.6–3.5

> [p. 2] Capitulum secundum narrat triplicem sentenciam de quidditate hostie consecrate, quarum prima dicit quod est quantitas, secunda quod est qualitas, et tercia quod est panis et vinum. Et obiciendo contra duas priores quomodo se ipsas destruunt declarat tripliciter quomodo tercia fuit sentencia Romane ecclesie. Quam confirmat tripliciter supra priorem sentenciam in hoc quod plus concordat racioni, sanctis doctoribus et scripture; de sanctis doctoribus allegat triplex testimonium positum in decretis. Quantum ad testimonium scripture notat sex loca in quibus fit mencio de hoc sacramento. In quorum quatuor fit mencio de eius institucione; et sic concludit quod decretum ecclesie in Beringario est tenendum. Sed obicit per hoc, primo quod videtur dictum decretum contradicere sibi ipsi; et dissolvit notans distinccionem inter predicacionem ydempticam et tropicam, in quo exponit textum Apostoli I. Cor. Xᵒ, 16, declarans quod ipse vocat hoc sacramentum panem et calicem; secundo obicit per hoc quod videtur corpus Christi debere nunc vocari panem et vinum, cum scriptura sacra sic loquitur. Et solvit ponens primo exemplum de logica beati Bernhardi concedentis quod manus quibus Deus formavit celum et terram fuerunt crucifixe. Secundo ostendit ex scriptura Genes. VIᵒ, quod non licet nobis tales conclusiones elicere; et exponendo scripturam docet secundum quam mensuram debent concedi dicta doctorum et quomodo in periculosa [p. 3] materia de Eukaristia est loquendum. Tercio et ultimo obicit quod novella ecclesia ponit transsubstanciacionem, et antiqua ecclesia eque vel magis autentica ipsam negat. Et diffuse prosequitur de nomine transsubstanciacionis et declarat cui ecclesie pocius est credendum.

The second chapter relates a threefold teaching on the [quiddity]{g:quiditas}[^10] of the
consecrated host, of which the first says "it is quantity", the second "it is quality", and the
third "it is bread and wine". And, objecting against the two former how they destroy themselves,
it [makes clear]{g:declarare} in three ways how the third was the teaching of the Roman church.
This it confirms in three ways over the former teaching, in that it agrees more with reason, with
the holy doctors and with scripture; from the holy doctors it [alleges]{g:allegare} a threefold
testimony set down in the decrees. As to the testimony of scripture, it notes six places in which
mention is made of this sacrament, in four of which mention is made of its institution; and so it
[concludes]{g:concludere} that the decree of the church in [the case of] Berengar is to be held. But
it objects, first, by appeal to this: that the said decree seems to contradict itself; and it
resolves [this], noting a distinction between [identical and tropical predication]{g:predicacio-ydemptica},[^11] in which it
expounds the text of the Apostle, 1 Cor. 10:16,[^12] making clear that he calls this sacrament
"bread" and "chalice"; second, it objects by appeal to this: that it seems the body of Christ ought
now to be called bread and wine, since holy scripture speaks so. And it resolves, putting forward
first an example from the [logic]{g:loyca} of blessed Bernard, who grants that "the hands by which God formed
heaven and earth were crucified".[^13] Second, it [shows]{g:ostendere} from scripture, Gen. 6,[^14] that it is not
lawful for us to draw out such conclusions; and, expounding scripture, it teaches according to what
measure the sayings of the doctors ought to be granted, and how one is to speak in the perilous [3]
matter of the eucharist. Third and last, it objects that the newer church posits
[transubstantiation]{g:transsubstanciacio}, and the ancient church, equally or more
[authentic]{g:autenticus}, denies it. And it pursues at length [the question] of the name of transubstantiation, and
makes clear which church is rather to be believed.

## ¶3 · 3.7–3.24

> [p. 3] Capitulum tercium declarat per sex argumenta quod transsubstanciacio non est fundabilis in scriptura. Posterius declarat per octo argumenta quod accidencia non possunt per se esse. Et tercio recitando doctores dat viam ad eliciendum conclusiones mirabiles. Primum argumentum prime partis fundatur super isto quod Deus inprovide sic mutaret. Secundum argumentum fundatur super hoc quod tunc annichilaretur substancia panis et vini; quod probat multipliciter consequenter. Et quantum ad secundum punctum deducit per multa media philosophica per deduccionem ad inconveniens et per multiplex testimonium Augustini quod nullum accidens potest esse sine subiecto; et arguendo doctores novellos obicit per Hugonem de Sancto Victore et solvit. Et ex ista varietate elicit tres conclusiones mirabiles et finaliter dat sensum ad quem doctores sancti et decreta debent intelligi.

The third chapter makes clear by six arguments that transubstantiation cannot be founded in
scripture. Next it makes clear by eight arguments that [accidents]{g:accidens} cannot be
[of themselves]{g:per-se}. And third, reciting the doctors, it gives a way to draw out marvellous
conclusions. The first argument of the first part is founded on this: that God would thus make the
change improvidently. The second argument is founded on this: that then the substance of bread and
wine would be annihilated; which it proves in many ways in what follows. And as to the second point
it deduces, through many philosophical [middle terms]{g:media}, by [deduction]{g:deduccio} to an
absurdity and by the manifold testimony of Augustine,[^15] that no accident can be without a
subject; and in arguing against the [newer doctors]{g:novelli} it objects by appeal to Hugh of St Victor, and
resolves.[^16] And from this variety[^17] it draws out three marvellous conclusions, and finally
gives the sense in which the holy doctors and the decrees ought to be understood.

## ¶4 · 3.25–4.25

> [p. 3] Capitulum quartum declarat, primo quomodo verba Christi quibus hoc sacramentum conficitur figurative debent intelligi, secundo quomodo Christus habens nobiscum multiplex esse humanitus quietabit beatos visione corporali post diem iudicii, et tercio quomodo est in sacramento altaris dispariter ab esse quod habet in aliis sacramentis, et quomodo hoc sacramentum tripliciter differenter alia antecellit. Secundo principaliter movendo tria dubia prosequitur primum, [p. 4] quod querit de veritate verborum sacramentalium, quando proposicio est vera et quare non debet concedi quod quelibet creatura est Christus, et quomodo nulla forma verborum est ad confeccionem Eukaristie necessario requisita, et quomodo quatuor evangeliste variant, et quomodo ecclesia in multis locis dat quintum modum variandi, et quomodo postpositis ritibus est ad sentenciam attendendum. Tercio principaliter obicit tripliciter et dissolvit, primo quomodo non sequimur Christum conficiendo et sumendo hostiam in prandendo, secundo obicit per hoc quod Christus fregit panem antequam consecravit. Et dissolvit narrans causam triplicem quare Spiritus Sanctus sic variavit in suis evangelistis et quare singulariter in benediccione calicis fit mencio de testamento. Et tercio obicit per hoc quod laycus videtur posse conficere; in qua materia distinguit de posse, explanat sentenciam beati Ambrosii, et descendendo ad tres opiniones quas recitat ecclesia IIIᵒ Decretalium invehit contra illas et preponit sentenciam Romane ecclesie ex sanctitate et facto Beringarii, et redeundo declarat quomodo oportet precognoscere questionem quid est de hostia antequam noscantur due alie questiones, et sic ostendit quod non tantum valet missa vel hostia sacerdotis reprobi ut devoti.

The fourth chapter makes clear, first, how the words of Christ by which this sacrament is
[consecrated]{g:conficere} ought to be understood figuratively; second, how Christ, having a
manifold [being]{g:esse} with us [humanly]{g:humanitus}, will give rest to the blessed by bodily
vision after the day of judgement; and third, how he is in the sacrament of the altar in a manner
unlike the being that he has in the other sacraments, and how this sacrament excels the others in
three different ways. Second, [as a main head]{g:principaliter}, raising three doubts, it pursues
the first, [4] which asks about the truth of the sacramental words: when the proposition is true,
and why it ought not to be granted that "every creature is Christ", and how no form of words is
necessarily required for the consecration of the eucharist, and how the four evangelists vary, and
how the church in many places gives a fifth way of varying, and how, the rites being set aside, one
is to attend to the sense.[^18] Third, as a main head, it objects in three ways and resolves
[the objections]: first, how we do not follow Christ in consecrating and taking the host while
dining; second, it objects by appeal to this: that Christ broke the bread before he consecrated.
And it resolves [this], relating a threefold cause why the Holy Spirit varied thus in his
evangelists, and why mention of the testament is made in the blessing of the chalice alone.
And third, it objects by appeal to this: that a layman seems able to consecrate; on which matter it
distinguishes concerning "can", explains the teaching of blessed Ambrose, and, descending to the
three [opinions]{g:opinio} that the church recites in the third [book] of the [Decretals]{g:decretalis},[^19] inveighs against them and prefers the
teaching of the Roman church from the holiness and the act of Berengar;[^20] and, returning, it
makes clear how one must know beforehand the question "what it is" concerning the host, before the two other
questions are known,[^21] and so it shows that the mass or host of a reprobate priest is not worth
as much as [that] of a devout one.

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Sentencia*, CONTEXTUAL (teaching / sentence): here the teaching of the treatise, its
    content summarised; at 4.9 (*ad sentenciam attendendum*, n. 18) the word is the sense of the
    words as against the rites.

[^2]: **C.** The compendium's verbs have no expressed subject: grammatically the subject is
    *capitulum*, the chapter, throughout each paragraph, and the English keeps "it" rather than
    supply Wyclif. *Solvit* and *dissolvit* are both "resolves" (they are synonyms here).

[^3]: **T.** *consequencie* is Loserth's correction; the manuscripts read *commune* ("Codd.:
    commune facte. Correxi", 1.12). *Consequencia* is the inference the people draw from a miracle to
    the presence of the body.

[^4]: **S.** A miracle that Gregory himself relates (*narratum a beato Gregorio*), presumably in the
    *Dialogues* (bk. IV treats the mass); but ch. 1 says only *ut narrat beatus Gregorius* (20.6) and names no miracle; Loserth compares the Maurist notes on Gregory's *Liber sacramentorum* (*Opp.* III, 563), so the *Dialogues* are not established (LEDGER Kprola-1).

[^5]: **C.** *Inconveniens* here in its argumentative sense, "absurdity" (the *reductio*), as in
    *deduccionem ad inconveniens* at 3.18.

[^6]: **S.** Gratian, *De cons.* D. 2 c. 27 (*Si per negligenciam aliquid de sanguine stillaverit*),
    quoted under Pius's name in ch. 1 (24.10–16; Friedberg, c. 27 *Ex decreto Papae Pii*) (LEDGER Kprola-2).

[^7]: **S.** *Ego Berengarius*, Gratian, *De cons.* D. 2 c. 42 (modern form ours), quoted in ch. 1 (25.19–26.7)
    (LEDGER Kprola-3).

[^8]: **S.** Gratian, D. 95 c. 6 (*Ecce ego dico*), under Jerome's name, quoted in ch. 1 (27.1–7); not *De cons.* D. 2 c. 49
    (LEDGER Kprola-4).

[^9]: **C.** *Dissolvit, primo per decretum pape … tercio per decretum Jeronimi*: the three *per*
    phrases name the grounds of the three objections just raised *per leges ecclesie* (1.22–23), not
    the means of resolving them. Chapter 1 argues *contra* from Pius's decree on the spilt blood (p. 24,
    *Sed contra ista arguitur per easdem leges ecclesie; nam … dicit Pius papa: Si per negligenciam …*),
    then from *Ego Berengarius* (p. 25) and from Jerome (27.1–7), and resolves them by the twofold kind
    of predicating. Loserth's punctuation allows either reading; the English follows the chapter.

[^10]: **G.** *Quidditas*, "quiddity": what a thing is. The second chapter's question is what the
    consecrated host is in its nature.

[^11]: **G.** *Predicacio ydemptica / tropica*: predication by identity (the subject is the very
    thing the predicate names) against figurative predication. "Identical" is the term of art, not
    "the same".

[^12]: **S.** 1 Cor. 10:16 (DR): "The chalice of benediction which we bless, is it not the
    communion of the blood of Christ? And the bread which we break, is it not the partaking of the
    body of the Lord?" The manuscripts give the chapter only (*Cor. X*); the verse is Loserth's.

[^13]: **S.** Bernard, cited in ch. 2 (39.17–20) as the sermon *De exaltacione sancte crucis*, inc. *De virga Jesse*: *Manus ille que fundaverunt celos extense sunt in cruce*; Loserth (39.19) takes it rather for the sermon *De duobus discipulis euntibus in Emaus* (*Opp.*, p. 1555); the sentence is in fact in a sermon of that title and incipit printed among Peter Damian's (*Sermo* 47, PL 144, 761C–766B; at 763D *Manus illae, quae fundaverunt coelos, extensae sunt in cruce*), not in Bernard's works (LEDGER
    Kprola-5).

[^14]: **S/T.** The chapter only, as printed (D reads *Gen. d°*, p. 2 n. to l. 30); the verse in view is Gen. 6:6 (*tactus dolore cordis intrinsecus*; ch. 2, 40.16 and 41.28), so
    identified in ch. 2 (LEDGER Kprola-6). ABCE read *ostendunt* for *ostendit*.

[^15]: **S.** Augustine; ch. 3 (72.4–73.4) gives three places: *De quantitate animae* 4, *Soliloquia* II.12.22–13.23 (Wyclif II.12 and 13) and *Ep.* 187.6.18 to Dardanus (Wyclif *Ep.* XL) (LEDGER
    Kprola-7).

[^16]: **C/S.** *Arguendo doctores novellos*: *arguere* with an accusative is "to argue against,
    convict". Hugh of St Victor, *De sacramentis* II.8.9, quoted in ch. 3 (73.29–74.20) (LEDGER Kprola-8).

[^17]: **T.** *varietate* as printed (E, by Loserth's silence, with it); ABCD read *vanitate*, "from this vanity" (3.22). The print
    construes (the variety of the doctors' opinions) and stands.

[^18]: **C.** *Sentencia* here is the sense of the words, set against the rites (*postpositis ritibus*),
    not a position taught (cf. 1.2, n. 1); the row *sentencia (verborum)* "sense".

[^19]: **S.** *Decretales* III, i.e. X 3.41.6 (*Cum Marthe*, Innocent III), which recites three
    opinions on the water mixed with the wine in the chalice. Modern form ours; ch. 4 quotes it at 103.10–104.12 (LEDGER Kprola-9).

[^20]: **C.** *Ex sanctitate et facto Beringarii*: the holiness may be the Roman church's rather
    than Berengar's (from [its] holiness and from the act of Berengar). The English keeps the
    print's order and leaves the reference open. E reads *Romane curie*.

[^21]: **S.** The order of knowing of Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics* II.1 (the question "what it
    is" before the others), which ch. 4 cites by name (109.27–110.5, *ut patet IIº Posteriorum in principio*)
    (LEDGER Kprola-10).
