# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 1, part 1: that Christ is a creature, from the testimonies of Augustine

## ¶1 · 3.0–3.0

> [p. 3] [Declarando ex testimoniis sanctorum quod humanitas sit Christus et per consequens creatura, dat sensum dictorum in scripturis et in symbolo que videntur huic opposita. Discussion as to the sense in which it is catholic truth to say: Christ is a creature.]

[By showing from the testimonies of the saints that the humanity is Christ, and consequently a creature,
he gives the sense of the sayings in the scriptures and in the creed that seem opposed to this.]

## ¶2 · 3.2–3.20

> [p. 3] Quia autem spiritualiter viantibus in discendo necesse est primo removere prohibens disciplinam; ac in scriptura sacra et sacris¹ doctoribus de Christo¹ sunt multa scripta² que videntur inperitis contraria; ideo pro solucione eorum in primis supponitur³ quod Christus sit tres nature incommunicantes: scilicet, deitas, corpus, et anima. Patet sic. Christus est Deus et homo perfectus, ut ex fide supponitur: omnis Deus est deitas: omnis homo perfectus est tam corpus quam anima: ergo conclusio. Confirmatur per Augustinum super Ioh. Ome. 47.⁴' Si, inquit, caro animam posuit, quomodo Christus animam posuit? numquid⁵ caro Christus?' Et respondet:' Ita plane et caro Christus et anima Christus et Verbum Christus: nec tamen hec tria⁶ tres Christi, sed unus Christus. Hominem interroga et de eo fac gradum⁷ ad ea que super te sunt, et⁸ si nondum intelligenda, saltem credenda. Quomodo enim unus homo anima et corpus? sic unus Christus Verbum et homo.' Nec credo quod nos ignari ad tantum desipimus quod dicamus hunc sanctum tam crebro sentenciam hereticam inculcare.

Now since for [those on the road]{g:viantes} in the spirit it is necessary, in learning, first to remove
what hinders instruction; and in holy scripture and in the holy doctors there are many things written
of Christ that seem to the unskilled to be contrary; therefore, for their resolution, it is supposed
in the first place that Christ is three natures that do not communicate with one another,[^1] namely
the [Godhead]{g:deitas}, a body and a soul. It is clear thus. Christ is God and perfect man, as is
supposed [as faith teaches]{g:ex-fide}; every God is Godhead; every perfect man is both body and soul;
therefore the [conclusion]{g:conclusio}. It is confirmed by Augustine on John, homily 47:[^2] "If," he
says, "the flesh laid down its soul, how did Christ lay down his soul? Is the flesh, then, Christ?" And
he answers: "Yes, plainly: both the flesh is Christ, and the soul is Christ, and the Word is Christ;
and yet these three are not three Christs, but one Christ. Question man, and from him make a step to
the things that are above you, and if they are not yet to be understood, at least to be believed. For
how is one man soul and body? so is one Christ Word and man." Nor do I believe that we in our
ignorance are so far out of our wits as to say that this saint so often presses a heretical
[teaching]{g:sentencia}.

## ¶3 · 3.21–4.5

> [p. 3] Ex istis plane⁹ sequitur quod Christus est creatura. Nam omnis natura corporea vel spiritus creatus est creatura: Christus est tam natura corporea quam spiritus creatus, cum sit homo perfectus: ergo Christus est creatura. Sed quia sunt multe scripture que videntur isti sentencie repugnare, ut recitat Magister 3° Sentenciarum Dist. 2ᵃ, ideo ut omnes communiter¹⁰ dissolvantur¹¹ sub uno epilogo; dicitur quod omnes recte intelligentes Christum non esse creaturam [p. 4] concipiunt quod Christus secundum deitatem non est creatura: sicut omnes recte concipientes¹ Christum esse creaturam concipiunt eum secundum humanitatem esse creaturam. Et ista² glosa, si non fallor, satisfacit sub communi involucro omnibus auctoribus in oppositum allegandis.

From these [it follows]{g:sequitur} plainly that Christ is a creature. For every corporeal nature or
created spirit is a creature; Christ is both a corporeal nature and a created spirit, since he is
perfect man; therefore Christ is a creature. But because there are many scriptures that seem to be
[incompatible]{g:repugnare} with this teaching, as the Master rehearses in the third book of the
*Sentences*, distinction 2,[^3] therefore, so that they may all be resolved together under one
recapitulation, [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that all who rightly understand that Christ is not a creature
[4] conceive that Christ as to the Godhead is not a creature, just as all who rightly conceive that
Christ is a creature conceive that he is a creature as to the humanity.[^4] And this [gloss]{g:glosa},
if I am not mistaken, satisfies under one common wrapping all the authors who are to be
[alleged]{g:allegare} on the opposite side.

## ¶4 · 4.6–4.27

> [p. 4] Sed ut illa³ materia particularius denudetur, oportet primo fundare sentenciam huius glose. Et occurrit michi⁴ primo disputacio Augustini ad Felicianum hereticum; ubi post duplicem nativitatem Christi et duplicem eius substanciam vel naturam declaratam tam ex scriptura sacra quam similitudine naturali, sic⁵ ait inter cetera: ‘Non⁶ alius homo corpus, alius animus, quamvis aliud corpus, aliud animus; unus tamen atque idem homo et corpus dicitur et animus. Sic, inquit, in mediatore⁷ Dei et hominum: aliud Dei filius, aliud hominis filius, unus tamen Christus Iesus ex utroque fit: aliud, inquam, pro discrecione⁸ substancie, non⁶ alius pro unitate persone.’ Et per hoc solvit scripturas de Christo,⁹ quarum alique locuntur de ipso secundum deitatem et alie secundum humanitatem. Et posiciones¹⁰ secundum habitudinem¹¹ concedit simpliciter; unde subdit: ‘nec ideo dicimus non pacientem quia ipse faciebat celos,¹² nec ideo non facientem quia non alius tollerabat. Nam si dominice incarnacionis misterium consideremus,¹³ idem sibi in hac humilitate et auctor¹⁴ et opus est.’ Unde et post declaratur esse possibile quod eadem persona simul et semel sed secundum diversam naturam mortem paciatur et vivat, sicut idem sol secundum diversos situs simul tempore diem causat et noctem.

But so that that matter may be laid bare more particularly, it is necessary first to ground the
teaching of this gloss. And there occurs to me first Augustine's disputation against Felician the
heretic,[^5] where, after the twofold birth of Christ and his twofold substance or nature have been
shown both from holy scripture and from a natural likeness, he says this among other things: "The body
is not one man and the mind another, although the body is one thing and the mind another; yet one and
the same man is called both body and mind. So," he says, "in the mediator of God and men: the Son of
God is one thing, the son of man another, yet one Christ Jesus is made out of both: one *thing*, I say,
for the distinction of substance, not one *person* and another, for the unity of the person."[^6] And
by this he resolves the scriptures about Christ, of which some speak of him as to the Godhead and
others as to the humanity. And the propositions according to their [condition]{g:habitudo} he grants
[without qualification]{g:simpliciter};[^7] hence he adds: "nor do we on that account say that he did
not suffer, because he himself was making the heavens; nor that he was not making them, because it was
no other who endured. For if we consider the mystery of the Lord's incarnation, in this humility the
same one is to himself both author and work." Hence afterwards too it is shown to be possible that the
same person at one and the same time, but as to a different nature, should suffer death and live, as
the same sun, as to different positions, causes day and night at the same time.

## ¶5 · 4.28–5.10

> [p. 4] Secundum testimonium Augustini est in Ep. 12¹⁵ ad Pascencium et in Epistula 40 ad Dardanum ubi cap. 7° ita inquit: ‘Cum enim Christus sit Deus et homo, Deus utique est; unde dicit: “Ego et Pater unum sumus” Ioh. 10: homo autem; unde dicit: “Pater maior me est,” Ioh. 14: idemque Filius Dei unigenitus a Patre, et filius hominis ex semine David¹⁶ secundum carnem est. Utrumque in illo considerandum [p. 5] est cum loquimur vel cum scriptura de ipso loquitur: et quid et¹ secundum quid de illo dicitur intuendum est. Nam sicut unus homo est anima rationalis² et caro, sic unus Christus est Verbum et homo. Proinde quod ad Verbum attinet, creator est Christus; “omnia enim per ipsum facta sunt;” quod vero³ ad hominem attinet, Christus creatus est; “factus enim est⁴ ex semine David secundum carnem,” et “in similitudinem hominum⁵ factus est.”’ Et post diffuse prosequitur declarando que scripture sibi conveniunt secundum deitatem et que secundum humanitatem.

The second testimony of Augustine is in Letter 12 to Pascentius and in Letter 40 to Dardanus, where in
chapter 7 he says this:[^8] "For since Christ is God and man, he is certainly God; hence he says, 'I
and the Father are one' (John 10:30); and he is man; hence he says, 'the Father is greater than I'
(John 14:28); and the same one is the only-begotten Son of God from the Father, and the son of man of
the seed of David according to the flesh. Both are to be considered [5] in him when we speak, or when
scripture speaks of him; and both what is said of him and in what respect it is said are to be looked
to. For as one man is a rational soul and flesh, so one Christ is Word and man. Accordingly, as far as
pertains to the Word, Christ is creator, 'for all things were made by him' (John 1:3); but as far as
pertains to the man, Christ was created; 'for he was made of the seed of David according to the flesh'
(Rom. 1:3), and 'was made in the likeness of men' (Phil. 2:7)." And afterwards he goes on at length,
showing which scriptures befit him as to the Godhead and which as to the humanity.

## ¶6 · 5.11–5.26

> [p. 5] Nec est maior color quod sit creator quia secundum deitatem, quin per idem sit creatura quia secundum humanitatem, specialiter cum sit illa completa humanitas. Quia certum est ex testimonio⁶ Augustini ad Felicianum quod totus homo assumptus passus est mortem, sicut sola caro iacuit in sepulcro, ut testatur Augustinus Ome. 47 ubi super: ‘Confiteris,⁷ inquit, in illum⁷ Christum credere qui est mortuus et sepultus: ergo et sepultum esse Christum non negas. Et tamen sola caro sepulta est: si enim erat ibi anima, non erat mortuus: si autem vera mors erat, ut vera sit eius resurreccio,⁸ sine anima erat in sepulcro, et tamen sepultus est Christus. Ergo Christus erat etiam sine anima caro, quia non erat sepulta nisi caro.’ Unde paulo ante ipse Christus dictus est sola caro. ‘Quomodo,⁹ inquit, probas? Audeo dicere: sola caro⁹ Christi dictus est Christus;’ et probatur¹⁰ per hoc, quod Christus iacuit in sepulcro.¹¹

Nor is there greater [colour]{g:color}[^9] that he is creator because [he is so] as to the Godhead,
than, by the same token, that he is a creature because [he is so] as to the humanity,
especially since he is that complete humanity. For it is certain from the testimony of Augustine
against Felician that the whole man assumed suffered death, just as the flesh alone lay in the tomb, as
Augustine witnesses in homily 47, where, above:[^10] "You confess," he says, "that you believe in that
Christ who died and was buried; therefore you do not deny that Christ was buried. And yet the flesh
alone was buried; for if the soul was there, he was not dead; but if it was a true death, so that his
resurrection may be true, he was in the tomb without the soul, and yet Christ was buried. Therefore
Christ was flesh even without the soul, because nothing was buried but the flesh." Hence a little
before, Christ himself is said to be flesh alone. "How," he says, "do you prove it? I dare to say: the
flesh of Christ alone is called Christ;" and it is proved by this, that Christ lay in the tomb.

## ¶7 · 5.27–6.29

> [p. 5] Tercium testimonium Augustini est Ome. 8. super illud Ioh. 2°. ‘Quid michi et tibi, mulier? nondum venit hora mea.’ ‘Dominus,¹² inquit, Iesus Christus Deus erat et homo: secundum quod Deus erat,¹³ matrem non habebat: secundum quod homo erat, matrem habebat. Mater ergo erat carnis, mater humanitatis, mater infirmitatis quam suscepit propter nos. Miraculum autem quod facturus erat [p. 6] secundum¹ deitatem facturus erat et¹ non secundum infirmitatem;² secundum quod Deus erat, non secundum quod infirmus natus erat: sed “infirmum³ Dei forcius hominibus.” Miraculum autem⁴ exigebat mater: at⁵ ille tanquam non cognosceret⁶ viscera humana operaturus facta divina, tanquam diceret; “quod de me fecit miraculum non tu⁷ genuisti: sed quia tu⁸ genuisti infirmitatem meam, tunc te⁹ cognoscam, cum ipsa infirmitas pendebit in cruce:” hoc est enim, “nondum venit hora mea.” Unde et commemorans illam nominacionem extraneam “mulierem” qua¹⁰ vocavit matrem in nupciis Galilee, pendens in cruce dixit matri: “Mulier, ecce filius tuus!” Ioh. 19. Non quod Christus aliquando non cognoscit matrem; in predestinacione, inquit Augustinus, noverat matrem, eciam¹¹ antequam ipse Deus eam crearet, de qua ipse homo crearetur: tunc tamen in effectu¹² agnovit, quando illud quod peperit moriebatur.¹³ Non¹⁴ enim moriebatur per quod facta erat Maria, sed moriebatur¹⁵ quod factum erat ex Maria: non¹⁴ moriebatur eternitas divinitatis, sed moriebatur infirmitas carnis: filius itaque virginis secundum quod creator celi et terre et dominus mundi creator fuit Marie, Deus¹⁶ fuit Marie; secundum autem quod dictum est “factum ex muliere, factum sub lege:” ipse enim¹⁷ dominus Marie est filius Marie; ipse creator Marie creatus ex Maria. Noli mirari: nam ideo David est filius, quia Marie est filius. Audi apostolum dicentem “qui factus est ei¹⁸ ex semine David secundum carnem”: audi eum et dominum David, ‘dixit dominus domino¹⁹ meo: “sede a dextris meis.” Unde¹⁹ et ipse Iesus²⁰ hoc proposuit Iudeis et eos inde convicit.’

The third testimony of Augustine is homily 8, on that text of John 2, "Woman, what is that to me and
to thee? my hour is not yet come."[^11] "The Lord Jesus Christ," he says, "was God and man. In that he
was God, he had no mother; in that he was man, he had a mother. She was the mother, therefore, of the
flesh, the mother of the humanity, the mother of the weakness that he took upon him for our sake. But
the miracle that he was about to do [6] he was about to do as to the Godhead, and not as to the
weakness; in that he was God, not in that he was born weak; but 'the weakness of God is stronger than
men' (1 Cor. 1:25). And the mother was demanding a miracle; but he, as though he did not acknowledge
the human womb when about to work divine deeds, as though he were saying, 'What works the miracle in me
you did not beget; but because you begot my weakness, then shall I acknowledge you, when that very
weakness shall hang on the cross'; for that is what 'my hour is not yet come' means. Hence too,
recalling that strange name, 'woman', by which he had called his mother at the wedding in Galilee,
hanging on the cross he said to his mother, 'Woman, behold thy son' (John 19:26). Not that Christ at
any time does not acknowledge his mother:[^12] in predestination, says Augustine, he knew his mother,
even before he himself, as God, created her from whom he himself, as man, was to be created; yet then
he acknowledged her in effect, when that which she bore was dying. For it was not that by which Mary
was made that was dying, but that which was made of Mary that was dying; it was not the eternity of
the [divinity]{g:divinitas} that was dying, but the weakness of the flesh. So the son of the virgin, in
that he is creator of heaven and earth and lord of the world, was the creator of Mary, was the God of
Mary; but in that he is what was said, 'made of a woman, made under the law' (Gal. 4:4), he, the lord of
Mary, is the son of Mary; he, the creator of Mary, was created of Mary. Do not wonder: for he is
David's son for this reason, that he is Mary's son. Hear the apostle saying, 'who was made to him of
the seed of David according to the flesh' (Rom. 1:3); hear him also as David's lord: 'The Lord said to
my Lord: Sit thou at my right hand' (Ps. 109:1). Hence Jesus himself too put this to the Jews, and from
it convicted them (Matt. 22:42–45)."

## ¶8 · 6.30–7.12

> [p. 6] Et isti regule innititur Augustinus utrobique, ut in libris contra Maximinianum; sicut Ieronimus in Epistula ad Paulam [p. 7] et Eustochium, et alii probabiles doctores. Unde libro primo contra Maximinianum sic loquitur Augustinus: ‘Dicis Patrem infactum quasi Filius factus sit, per quem facta sunt omnia. Scito factum esse Filium sed in¹ forma servi, qui in forma¹ Dei usque adeo non est factus, ut per illum facta sunt omnia.’ Unde et² infra 2° dicit quod Deus Pater non creatorem creavit, sed creatorem³ constituit iuxta illud Psⁱ. 8ⁱ. quod⁴ secundum apostolum et secundum Augustinum⁴ ad litteram verificatur de Christo: ‘minuisti eum⁵ paulo minus ab angelis, gloria et honore coronasti eum, Domine,⁶ et constituisti eum super opera manuum tuarum: omnia subiecisti sub pedibus.’⁷

And Augustine leans on this rule everywhere, as in the books against Maximinus,[^13] as does Jerome in
the letter to Paula [7] and Eustochium,[^14] and other approved doctors. Hence in the first book against
Maximinus Augustine speaks thus: "You say that the Father is unmade, as if the Son were made, through
whom all things were made. Know that the Son was made, but in the form of a servant, he who in the form
of God is so far from being made that through him all things were made." Hence also below, in the
second book, he says that God the Father did not create a creator, but appointed a creator,[^15]
according to that text of Psalm 8, which according to the apostle and according to Augustine is
verified of Christ to the letter: "Thou hast made him a little less than the angels; thou hast crowned
him with glory and honour, O Lord, and hast set him over the works of thy hands; thou hast subjected
all things under his feet."[^16]

## Notes

[^1]: **C** *tres nature incommunicantes*: three natures of which none is predicated of, or shared
    with, another: Godhead, body, soul. *Christus sit tres nature* keeps Wyclif's bare predication,
    "Christ is three natures", on which the whole argument that follows turns; it is not "has".
[^2]: **S** Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 47.12 (on John 10:17–18), PL 35, 1737–38;
    *verified* against the PL: Wyclif's text differs in small points (*de eo* for *de teipso*; *hec
    tria* for *tria haec*) and omits *est* (LEDGER P01a-1). "Homily" renders *omelia*, Wyclif's word
    for a tractate. The Latin prints a single quotation mark before *Si*.
[^3]: **S** Peter Lombard, *Sententiae* III d. 11, *Utrum Christus sit creatura, vel creatus, vel
    factus* (PL 192, 777–78), *verified*: that is where the Master rehearses the scriptures for and
    against. The print's "distinction 2" has no variant in the apparatus; it is the manuscripts'
    reading, kept (LEDGER P01a-2).
[^4]: **C** *secundum deitatem … secundum humanitatem*: "as to the Godhead … as to the humanity". The
    rule of reduplication that governs the whole chapter: a predicate is true of Christ *as to* one of
    his natures.
[^5]: **S** The *De fide contra Felicianum Arianum*, printed among Augustine's works (PL 42, 1157–72) but
    the work of Vigilius of Thapsus; Wyclif takes it for Augustine's. The passage quoted (*Non alius
    homo corpus …*) is *verified* in the form quoted by Benedict of Aniane, *Disputatio adversus
    Felicianam impietatem* (PL 103, 1403), which cites it as Augustine's (LEDGER P01a-3).
[^6]: **C** *aliud … non alius*: the neuter *aliud* marks a different thing (substance), the masculine
    *alius* a different person. Augustine's point is lost in English without the gloss "one *thing*
    … not one *person* and another", so it is supplied in italics.
[^7]: **C** *posiciones secundum habitudinem concedit simpliciter*: he grants without qualification
    the propositions [made] according to the respective condition, i.e. the paired statements "he
    suffered" and "he made the heavens" are each granted outright of the one Christ, as the quotation
    that follows shows; *habitudo* is the condition under which each is said. The apparatus notes
    that A, B and C abbreviate *posiciones* beyond certain reading.
[^8]: **S** The letter to Dardanus is Augustine, *Epistula* 187 (*De praesentia Dei*), §8, PL 33, 836
    (*verified*; Wyclif's text omits *observandum* for *considerandum* and adds *et* before *secundum
    quid*); its old number 40 is of Wyclif's day. The letter "12 to Pascentius" is one of the exchange
    with Pascentius the Arian, *Epistulae* 238–241 in the received numbering (LEDGER P01a-4).
[^9]: **C** *color*: a plausible ground (the period sense, as in "colour of title"), the GLOSSARY's
    ruling; the first occurrence in the chunk.
[^10]: **S** Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 47.13 (not "above", *ubi super*, but a few
    lines after the passage already quoted), PL 35, 1738; *verified* (*fuerat* for *erat*; *non est
    sepulta* for *non erat sepulta*). "A little before" (*paulo ante*) refers to §12, *caro Christus*
    (LEDGER P01a-5).
[^11]: **S** Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 8.9 (on John 2:1–4), PL 35, 1455–56; the
    opening *verified* (PL *et Deus erat et homo*; *secundum divinitatem* where Wyclif has *secundum
    deitatem*). The rest of the long quotation, to *convicit*, runs on from §9 and is not checked word
    for word (LEDGER P01a-6).
[^12]: **T** *non cognoscit*: present where Augustine's sense asks for the past; O reads *cognoscit*,
    A B C *agnosceret*. The print is kept.
[^13]: **T** *contra Maximinianum*: the print's spelling, in both places, of Augustine's *Contra
    Maximinum Arianorum episcopum* (PL 42, 743–814); the English gives the name. The quotation (*Scito
    factum esse Filium …*) is *verified*: it stands in *Contra Maximinum* at the chapter numbered 17,
    where Augustine recalls what he had said in the debate; whether the print's "first book" is right
    is left open (LEDGER P01a-7).
[^14]: **S** Jerome, the letter to Paula and Eustochium: which letter Wyclif means is not clear (perhaps
    the preface to the commentary on Ephesians, addressed to them); to-verify (LEDGER P01a-8).
[^15]: **S** Augustine, *Contra Maximinum* II (*creatorem constituit*): to-verify (LEDGER P01a-9).
[^16]: **S** Ps. 8:6–8, Douay–Rheims; Wyclif's text adds *Domine* and ends *sub pedibus* without
    *eius*. "According to the apostle": Heb. 2:7–8, which applies the psalm to Christ. Harris's margin
    gives Ps. viii. 5, 6 and Heb. ii. 7.
