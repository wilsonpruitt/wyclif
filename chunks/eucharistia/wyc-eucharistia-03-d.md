# *De eucharistia*, chapter 3, part 4: against quantity as the subject, and the qualities that stand of themselves

## ¶1 · 65.29–66.5

> [p. 65] Sed contra istud arguitur primo per hoc quod nulla quantitas potest taliter maiorari, nam vel manet quantitas que maioraretur vel non. Si manet, vel est permixta cum quantitate que advenit; et patet [p. 66] quod tunc ad omnem punctum fit nova quantitas antiqua corrupta; si vero manet inpermixta, non illa fit maior sed generaliter nova que non est sacramentum, ex quibus fit sacra et non sacra quantitas bipartita.

But against this [it is argued]{g:arguitur-per} first by appeal to the fact that no [quantity]{g:quantitas} can be enlarged in such a way; for either the quantity that would be enlarged remains or it does not. If it remains, either it is mixed with the quantity that comes to it, and it is clear [66] that then at every point a new quantity comes to be, the old one having been corrupted; but if it remains unmixed, it is not that quantity that becomes greater, but [there is] generally a new one, which is not the [sacrament]{g:sacramentum}; and from these a quantity comes to be in two parts, [sacred]{g:sacer} and not sacred.[^1]

## ¶2 · 66.6–66.21

> [p. 66] Secundo arguitur per hoc quod rarefacta quantitate ad omnem punctum, cum experimentum docet quod potest rarefieri, oportet quod antiqua quantitas que fuit sacramentum totaliter corrumpatur et una quantitas non sacramentum totaliter generetur. Non enim fingendum est aliquid quod consecret continue novam quantitatem; cum tamen non desinet corpus Christi esse ibi beatum accidentibus sic remanentibus sub forma panis et vini, quia aliter trepidaret christianus per quantumlibet breve tempus post consecracionem accipere sacramentum, cum ex sibi dubio calor vel aliud agens mutando subiectum fecit accidens succedere, quod non fit sacramentum, cum prius causaliter sit sacramentum sensibile quam corpus Christi sit ibi sacramentaliter, ut patet de natura confeccionis.

Second, it is argued by appeal to the fact that, when the quantity is rarefied at every point (since experience teaches that it can be rarefied), the old quantity which was the sacrament must be wholly corrupted, and one quantity which is not the sacrament wholly generated. For one must not [feign]{g:fingere} something that continually consecrates a new quantity; since nevertheless [the body of Christ]{g:corpus-christi} does not cease to be there, blessed, while the [accidents]{g:accidens} so remain under the form of [bread and wine]{g:panis}; for otherwise a Christian would tremble to receive the sacrament for however short a time after the consecration, since, [for all he knows]{g:ex-sibi-dubio},[^2] heat or another agent, by changing the subject, has made an accident succeed which does not become the sacrament; since the [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} sacrament is causally prior to the body of Christ's being there [sacramentally]{g:sacramentaliter}, as is clear from the nature of [consecration]{g:confeccio}.

## ¶3 · 66.22–66.33

> [p. 66] Tercio arguitur per hoc quod signata a terra pedali cui linitur pedalis quantitas, illa terra est et foret formaliter pedalis in illo casu possibili quo Deus anichilaret quantitatem illam sed servaret terram omnino inmotam in situ eodem; sed tunc remaneret formaliter pedalis, ergo est dare unam pedalitatem intrinsecam priorem illa linita qua formaliter est pedalis; et illa non potest per se esse, cum sit istam terram sic esse quantam; et distinccionem illius nemo potest destruere; et eadem est consideracio de qualitate quod non habet propriam raritatem, densitatem et similes qualitates distinctas a subiecto.

Third, it is argued by appeal to the fact that, a foot-long [piece of] earth being designated, a,[^3] on which a foot-long quantity is [smeared]{g:linire}, that earth is, and would be, formally foot-long in that possible case in which God [annihilated]{g:anichilare} that quantity but kept the earth altogether unmoved in the same place; now then it would remain formally foot-long; therefore [one may posit]{g:est-dare} an intrinsic [foot-length]{g:pedalitas}, prior to that smeared one, by which it is formally foot-long; and that [foot-length] cannot be [of itself]{g:per-se}, since it is this earth's being so [quantified]{g:quantus}; and no one can destroy its distinctness. And the same consideration holds of [quality]{g:qualitas}: that it does not have its own rarity, density and like qualities distinct from the subject.[^4]

## ¶4 · 67.1–67.15

> [p. 67] Item, secundum falsigraphos substancia potest manere quanta sine quantitate distincta et sic de omni respectu; nec est racio quin per idem sic senciendum est de toto genere qualitatis, ergo non est dare accidens preter substanciam; cum ergo constat ex sensu quod remanent post consecracionem res materiales, restat illis concedere quod remanent materiales substancie. Quid ergo foret istud corpus remanens nisi panis et vinum, ut dicit decretum Romane ecclesie? Cum autem hoc sit argumentum ad hominem, peto maiorem ut datam. Minor patet ex hoc quod eque ad quantitatem potest esse motus ut ad qualitatem; eque ponunt philosophi et probant raciones alia accidencia distingui ut qualitates, eque ponunt qualitates non posse separari a subiecto ut alia accidencia.

Again, according to the [false writers]{g:falsigraphus}[^5] [substance]{g:substancia} can remain quantified without a distinct quantity, and so of every [relation]{g:respectus}; nor is there a reason why, by the same token, the same should not be thought of the whole genus of quality; therefore one may not posit an accident besides substance. Since, therefore, it is evident from sense that material things remain after the consecration, it remains for them to grant that material substances remain. What, then, would that remaining body be but bread and wine, as the [decree]{g:decretum} of the Roman [church]{g:ecclesia} says?[^6] And since this is an [argument to the man]{g:argumentum-ad-hominem}, I ask that the major be taken as granted. The minor is clear from this: that there can be motion to quantity equally as to quality; equally do the philosophers posit, and arguments prove, that the other accidents are distinguished [from substance] as qualities are; equally do they posit that qualities cannot be separated from a subject, as [they posit of] the other accidents.

## ¶5 · 67.16–67.24

> [p. 67] Unde hic mirantur philosophi quare theologi ponunt omnem qualitatem posse per se esse et non formam substancialem materialem, que (in quantum substancia) pocius haberet racionem per se standi et sic materia prima posset esse informis quod negant. Si dicitur quod Aristoteles vel racio illud dictat, eque vel plus dictat quod qualitas non poterit per se esse; si dicitur quod fides hoc postulat, pure petitur quod probarent.

Hence here the philosophers wonder why the theologians posit that every quality can be of itself, and not a material [substantial form]{g:forma-substancialis}, which ([insofar as]{g:in-quantum} [it is] substance) would rather have the account of standing of itself; and so [prime matter]{g:materia-prima} could be without form, which they deny.[^7] If it is said that Aristotle or reason dictates that, it dictates equally or more that a quality could not be of itself; if it is said that faith demands this, what they ought to prove [is begged]{g:petitur} outright.

## ¶6 · 67.25–68.11

> [p. 67] Mirantur secundo quam graciam meruit qualitas, quam approbant posse per se esse, cum eque sicut potest salvari figuram esse figuratum et sic de pulcritudine cum naturali potencia et aliis donis Dei, sic potest probabiliter teneri de toto genere qualitatis. Et tamen paucas alias qualitates ponunt de tercia specie qualitatis, cum eque propter variacionem modi habendi substancie sine aliquo accidente quod poterit per se esse potest salvari substancia utrobique; [p. 68] nam sicut lux consequitur ad solem ut passio ad subiectum, ita consecuntur qualitates prime ad elementa; et sicut ad presenciam lucis solis cum capacitate in medio naturaliter sequitur lumen eius vel illuminacio, ita secundum varietatem mixtionis elementorum in ceteris mixtis consecuntur qualitates secunde, aliis respicientibus lucem vel factum preteritum ut calores et passiones, et aliis respicientibus figuras, mollicies et duricies, levitas et asperitas; poneret ergo sic opinans omnes qualitates quas ponunt philosophi et nullas posse per se esse vel omnes.

They wonder, second, what favour quality has earned, which they allow to be able to be of itself; since, just as it can be maintained that [figure]{g:figura} is the thing figured[^8] (and so of beauty, with natural power and the other gifts of God), so it can be held [with probability]{g:probabiliter} of the whole genus of quality. And yet they posit few other qualities of the third species of quality,[^9] since equally, because of the variation in the substance's mode of having [itself], without any accident that could be of itself, the substance can be maintained [as the account] on both sides; [68] for as light follows on the sun as an [attribute]{g:passio} on its [subject]{g:subiectum}, so the primary qualities follow on the elements; and as at the presence of the sun's light, with a capacity in the medium, its radiance (*lumen*) or illumination naturally follows, so according to the variety of the mixture of the elements in the other [mixed bodies]{g:mixtum} the secondary qualities follow, some regarding light or a past deed, as heats and passions,[^10] and others regarding figures, as softnesses and hardnesses, smoothness and roughness.[^11] One holding this [opinion]{g:opinio}, then, would posit all the qualities that the philosophers posit, and either none of them able to be of itself, or all.

## ¶7 · 68.12–69.5

> [p. 68] Mirantur tercio quod sic opinantes ponunt unionem, continuacionem, contiguacionem et substancia esse qualitates que poterunt per se esse; propter hoc principium non potest esse motus a contradictorio in contradictorium sine generacione vel corrupcione nove rei; et non dicunt sic de motu, tempore et ceteris respectibus; nam si ista tria poterunt per se esse, tunc illorum est dare unionem distinctam, et sic de continuacione et contiguacione sine fine. Non enim videtur racio quod ad continuandum A cum B sequitur continuacio que sit qualitas distincta potens per se esse, quin per idem ad continuandum continuaciones illas sequeretur alia et sic in infinitum; et posita carencia continuacionis cum inmediacione continuabilium ponitur contiguacio sine nova re denominante formaliter, que poterit per se esse; et iterum in confinio continuatorum forent infinite continuaciones superficiales, lineares vel punctuales; quod negat opinans nec dicet racionem quod anichilacio, transsubstanciacio et similia sint veritates distincte et [p. 69] sic nec substancie nec accidencia, cum poterunt per se esse, quin per idem est dare veritates alias predicamentales duodecim generum, ut olim posuerunt philosophi. Et patet quante error fictus de hoc sacramento perturbat philosophos.

They wonder, third, that those who so opine posit the [union]{g:unio}, [continuation]{g:continuacio} [and] [contiguity]{g:contiguacio} of substance[^12] to be qualities that could be of themselves, on account of this principle: "there cannot be motion from contradictory to contradictory without the generation or corruption of a new thing"; and they do not say so of motion, time and the other relations. For if these three could be of themselves, then one may posit a distinct union of them, and so of continuation and contiguity without end. For there seems no reason why, for continuing A with B, a continuation should follow which is a distinct quality able to be of itself, without another, by the same token, following for continuing those continuations, and so to infinity. And, the lack of continuation being posited together with the immediacy of the things that can be continued, contiguity is posited without a new thing formally [naming]{g:denominare} it which could be of itself; and again, at the boundary of the things continued there would be infinite continuations of surfaces, lines or points; which the one who so opines denies. Nor will he give a reason why [annihilation]{g:anichilacio}, [transubstantiation]{g:transsubstanciacio} and the like should be distinct truths, and [69] so neither substances nor accidents, since they could be of themselves, without its being possible, by the same token, to posit other [truths of the categories]{g:veritas-predicamentalis} of twelve genera, as the philosophers once posited.[^13] And it is clear how greatly the error feigned about this sacrament troubles the philosophers.

## ¶8 · 69.6–69.21

> [p. 69] Item, iuxta hanc viam non convinceretur philosophice aliqua materialis substancia, quia omnis experiencia de motu vel transmutacione subiecti aut aliquo allegando posset salvari sine tali materiali principio, ergo frustra est ponere talem inperfectam substanciam, cum nec racio nec experiencia hanc convincit. Sic enim possent sompniari infinite substancie sine racione movente. Assumptum patet iuxta sic loquentes, cum ponunt accidencia posse per se agere et pati eque efficaciter ut modo sine substrata materiali substancia. Cum ergo iuxta philosophorum principia frustra fit per plura, quod fieri potest per pauciora et natura agit breviori modo quam poterit, videtur esse phisici negare huiusmodi materialem substanciam sensibilem et ponere sufficienter ad finem nature aliqua predicamenta accidentis.

Again, on this way no material substance would be proved philosophically, since every experience of the motion or transmutation of a subject, or of anything one might [allege]{g:allegare}, could be saved without such a material principle; therefore it is in vain to posit such an imperfect substance, since neither reason nor experience proves it. For in this way infinite substances could be dreamt up without a reason that moves [one to them]. [The assumption]{g:assumptum} is clear according to those who speak so, since they posit that accidents can of themselves act and be acted on just as effectively as now, without a material substance lying under them. Since, then, according to the principles of the philosophers, what can be done by fewer is done in vain by more, and nature acts in the shorter way it can,[^14] it seems to belong to the natural philosopher to deny such perceptible material substance, and to posit some [categories]{g:predicamentum} of accident as sufficient for the end of nature.

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 65.29: Answer: 1. If the quantity were altered, the subject would no longer be the same.
- 66.4: 2. Such an alteration may take place through rarefaction caused by heat or other agency.
- 66.21: 3. Although the quantity be changed, the subject remains quantitative; this shews that quantity is only a condition o the subject.
- 67.1: If substance can be quantitative without distinct quantity, accident does not exist without substance; and if substance remains, why is it not of bread and wine?
- 67.10: This is merely an argumentum ad hominem, resting on a false premiss.
- 67.16: The postulates of our opponents land them in the contradiction that matter may exist without form.
- 67.25: Why is quality to be specially distinguished above other accidents?
- 68.12: Other inconsistencies as to union &c., which our opponents distinguish from the other predicaments.
- 69.6: It accidents can exist of themselves, we need not postulate substance at all.

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** *Generaliter nova* (66.3) is translated as printed; D reads *sed generatur* ("but a new one is generated"), which gives a plainer sense. The argument: either the old quantity is corrupted at every point, or a new, unconsecrated quantity is joined to the consecrated one.
[^2]: **C.** *Ex sibi dubio*: "for all he knows", literally "from [what is] doubtful to him"; the same idiom as *ex tibi dubio* (70.25).
[^3]: **C.** *Signata a terra pedali* (66.22): the *a* is taken as the logicians' letter-label for the case (as *A* and *B* at 68.21), "a foot-long earth being designated *a*". If it is the preposition the phrase does not construe.
[^4]: **C.** The subject of *non habet* (66.32) is taken as quality: rarity, density and the like are not qualities distinct from the subject. It could also be the earth, "that it [the subject] has no rarity of its own distinct from the subject"; the sense is the same.
[^5]: **G/S.** *Falsigraphi*, "false writers", is the frozen row. The view that substance is quantified without a quantity distinct from it is not ascribed here; it is commonly identified as Ockham's (LEDGER K03d-1, to be checked).
[^6]: **S.** The decree of the Roman church that bread and wine remain is *Ego Berengarius* (*De cons.* D. 2 c. 42, our modern form), cited throughout the treatise (LEDGER K03d-2, to be checked).
[^7]: **C.** *Quod negant*: the theologians deny that prime matter can be without form, and their premiss implies it. *Pure petitur quod probarent*: "what they ought to prove is simply begged".
[^8]: **C.** *Figura* here is shape, the fourth species of quality, and *figuratum* the shaped body: the claim is that a shape is nothing but the thing shaped. The frozen rendering is kept because Wyclif uses the same pair of the sacrament.
[^9]: **S.** The species of quality are Aristotle's (*Cat.* 8): the third is the passible qualities and passions, the fourth form and figure (LEDGER K03d-3, to be checked).
[^10]: **T/C.** *Calores* (68.8) is the print; E reads *colores*, "colours", which answers better to *lucem*. *Passiones* here are the Categories' passions (the third species of quality), not the *passio* "attribute" of 68.1.
[^11]: **C.** *Levitas* beside *asperitas* is *lēvitas* "smoothness", not *levitas* "lightness": smooth and rough go with figure (Aristotle, *Cat.* 8).
[^12]: **T.** The print has *contiguacionem et substancia* (68.13), which does not construe; C has *subiecta* in an erasure, and Loserth's footnote gives *recte: contiguacionem substancie*, which is translated. **G.** *Continuacio* here is the act of making bodies continuous, as in the *De incarnacione* row.
[^13]: **C.** *Duodecim generum* (69.2): "of twelve genera", as printed; which philosophers counted twelve categories is not said.
[^14]: **T.** The print reads *breviori modo quam poterit* (69.18); ABCD read *quo*, "in the shorter way by which it can", which gives the commonplace (nature does nothing in vain, and by the shortest way) and is translated. The axiom *frustra fit per plura* is the schools' commonplace (LEDGER K03d-4, to be checked).
