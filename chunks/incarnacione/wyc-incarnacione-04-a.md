# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 4, part 1: the moderns on predication in the three days: Bonaventure and the Subtle Doctor

## ¶1 · 49.0–49.0

<!-- the editor's bracketed argument at the chapter head: a Latin summary, then his own English
     summary in the same bracket (see the volume CARD for what is translated) -->

> [p. 49] [Iuxta ponit sentencias modernorum doctorum ut veritas plus lucescat. The opinions of Bonaventura, Duns Scotus, and St. Anselm, as to the nature of Christ during the three days’ entombment, examined.]

[It sets beside them the [teachings]{g:sentencia} of [the modern doctors]{g:moderni-doctores}, so that the truth may shine out the more.]

## ¶2 · 49.1–49.3

> [p. 49] Sed ut magis appareat, quomodo communicacio ydiomatum pro triduo Christo conveniat, iuxta posite sunt sentencie modernorum.

But that it may appear more fully how the [communication of idioms]{g:communicacio-ydiomatum} befits Christ for [the three days]{g:triduum}, the teachings of the moderns are set beside [ours].[^1]

## ¶3 · 49.4–49.23

> [p. 49] Scribit enim Bonaventura super Distinccionem 12.¹ 3ⁱⁱ Sentenciarum, quod triplex² est predicacio, scilicet actualis, aptitudinalis,³ et mixtim. Actualis, quando subiectum est ens actu et forma predicati sibi inest; ut cum dicitur homine existente ‘homo est animal.’ Predicacio secundum puram aptitudinem est, quando nec subiectum nec forma predicati est in actu, sed necessaria⁴ est ordinacio unius ad alterum fundata super principia nature; sicut nulla rosa existente ‘rosa est flos.’ Quando subiectum autem est in actu, et forma predicati non inest sibi actualiter sed secundum necessariam ordinacionem fundatam super principia nature, tunc est predicacio mixtim, id est, partim actualis et partim aptitudinalis. Et sic concedit quod Christus fuit homo in triduo, quia subiectum, quod est Verbum Dei, tunc fuit in actu, et forma humanitatis servata fuit in aptitudine coniunccionis anime ad corpus. Nec vidi planiorem sentenciam alicuius doctoris in illa materia; quia indubie, quicunque concedit quod corpus et anima fuerunt partes Christi in triduo, habet concedere consequenter quod Christus habet correspondenter esse hominis ex eisdem.

For Bonaventure writes on Distinction 12 of the third [book] of the *Sentences* that [predication]{g:predicacio} is threefold, namely actual, habitudinal, and mixed.[^2] Actual, when the [subject]{g:subiectum} is [a being]{g:ens} in act and the form of the predicate is in it; as when it is said, a man existing, "man is an animal". Predication according to pure aptitude is when neither the subject nor the form of the predicate is in act, but there is a necessary [ordering]{g:ordinacio} of the one to the other founded on the principles of nature; as, no rose existing, "a rose is a flower".[^3] And when the subject is in act, and the form of the predicate is not in it actually but according to a necessary ordering founded on the principles of nature, then the predication is mixed, that is, partly actual and partly aptitudinal. And so he grants that Christ was man in the three days, because the subject, which is [the Word]{g:verbum} of God, was then in act, and the form of [the humanity]{g:humanitas} was preserved in the aptitude of the conjunction of the soul to the body. Nor have I seen a plainer teaching of any doctor in that matter; because undoubtedly whoever grants that the body and soul were parts of Christ in the three days has to grant consequently that Christ correspondingly has the [being]{g:esse} of a man from the same.[^4]

## ¶4 · 49.24–50.21

> [p. 49] Sed Doctor Subtilis dilatat⁵ ibidem⁶ amplius istam materiam. Dicit enim quod in triduo fuit ita; quod ‘Christus est Christus, et quod Verbum habet tam corpus quam animam [p. 50] sibi unitam, et tamen non tunc fuit ita quod Christus est homo; quia humanitas dicit ultra naturas imperfectas et ultra unionem respectivam unitatem¹ absolutam, sicut est in numeris. Et illa unitas non componit cum aliis; quia tunc esset² processus in infinitum. Et sic in triduo corruptum est aliquid absolutum, quia humanitas; sic quod nulla pars eius remanserat, cum sit³ forma totalitatis³ non habens partes quantitativas vel qualitativas. Racio autem quare non sequitur—Christus est Christus: ergo Christus est homo—est ista. Christus secundum Damascenum ca°. 49 est nomen ypostaticum⁴ duarum naturarum⁵ completarum singulatim,⁶ scilicet humanitatis et deitatis. Cum ergo oportet racionem subiecti talis esse in se veram, antequam aliquid de eo enuncietur; quia aliter esset hec vera—maius Deo⁷ est— quod est falsum,⁸ cum subiectum includit repugnanciam: patet quod hec—Christus est homo—⁹ formata pro triduo⁹ foret falsa; quia humanitas non tunc infuit Verbo Dei. Ideo, sicut,¹⁰ nullo homine existente, homo albus¹¹ est homo albus,¹² nec ex hoc sequitur quod homo sit homo albus¹²: sic pro triduo Christus est Christus; sicut Deus-homo est¹³ Deus-homo, et tamen Deus non est tunc homo.’

But [the Subtle Doctor]{g:doctor-subtilis} widens this matter more amply in the same place.[^5] For he says that in the three days it was so that "Christ is Christ" and that "the Word has both body and soul [50] united to him", "and yet it was not then so that Christ is man; because the humanity [signifies]{g:dicere}, beyond the imperfect natures and beyond the [relational]{g:respectivus} [union]{g:unio}, an absolute unity, as is the case in numbers. And that unity does not compose with other things; because then there would be a process to infinity. And so in the three days something absolute was corrupted, namely the humanity; so that no part of it had remained, since it is a [form of totality]{g:forma-totalitatis}, not having quantitative or qualitative parts.[^6] And the reason why it does not follow, 'Christ is Christ: therefore Christ is man', is this. Christ, according to Damascene, c. 49, is a hypostatic name of two natures, complete each singly, namely of the humanity and [the Godhead]{g:deitas}.[^7] Since therefore the notion of such a subject must be true in itself before anything is enunciated of it (because otherwise this would be true, 'something greater than God is', which is false, since the subject includes an [incompatibility]{g:repugnancia}),[^8] it is clear that this, 'Christ is man', formed for the three days, would be false; because the humanity was not then in the Word of God. Therefore, just as, no man existing, a white man is a white man, and yet [it does not follow]{g:sequitur} from this that a man is a white man: so for the three days Christ is Christ; as [God-man]{g:deushomo} is God-man, and yet God is not then man."

## ¶5 · 50.22–51.5

> [p. 50] Ulterius quo ad denominacionem Verbi ab illis partibus ita scribit: ‘Verbum, inquit, quamvis in triduo habuit¹⁴ corpus et animam sibi unitam a quibus potuit denominari: non placuit tamen doctoribus quod denominetur ab eis, et forte non sine causa: cum partes non denominant, nisi ut subsistunt naturaliter in suo toto. Sed tunc defuit humanitas: ideo non denominavit eum carneum vel carnalem.¹⁵ Verumptamen si essent¹⁵ nomina imposita, posset denominari ab illis; sed¹⁶ oporteret quod essent denominativa, que non saperent vicium.¹⁷ Unde sicut substancia¹⁷ non sufficit denominari¹⁸ alba ab albedine,¹⁸ nisi mediante subiecto: sic [p. 51] nec Christus denominari carnalis, nisi mediante humanitate: ut, si Christus assumpsisset solum quantitatem, adhuc¹ non esset quantus propter defectum subiecti materialis.’ Hoc Latinum huius doctoris est michi² et forte aliis difficilius quam³ sentencia Augustini.³

Further, as to the [naming]{g:denominacio} of the Word from those parts, he writes thus: "The Word, he says, although in the three days he had body and soul united to him, from which he could [be named]{g:denominare}, yet it did not please the doctors that he be named from them, and perhaps not without cause: since parts do not name except as they subsist naturally in their whole. But then the humanity was lacking: therefore it did not name him fleshy or carnal.[^9] Nevertheless, if names were imposed, he could be named from them; but they would have to be denominatives that did not savour of vice. Hence, just as a substance does not suffice to be named white from whiteness except by means of a subject, so [51] neither [does] Christ [suffice] to be named carnal except by means of the humanity: so that, if Christ had [assumed]{g:assumere} quantity alone, he would still not be of a quantity, for lack of a material subject." This Latin of this doctor is to me, and perhaps to others, more difficult than the teaching of Augustine.[^10]

## ¶6 · 51.6–51.15

> [p. 51] Sed pro eius intellectu notandum quod ipse admisit predicacionem secundum habitudinem⁴ sicut Doctor alius.⁵ Et illud antiquitus est famosum,⁶ ut: ‘Quandocunque predicatur idem de se vel per se superius⁷ de suo per se⁸ inferiori⁹ est predicacio vera,⁹ licet neutrum extremum sit in actu. Ut verum est, dicit Doctor, quod—Cesar est homo—eciam nullo homine existente.’ Sed quando predicatur formaliter res alterius generis, tunc limitat ad predicacionem actualem; ut, ‘si homo est qualis,¹⁰ quantus, pater, agens, et cetera; tunc est actu.’

But for understanding him it is to be noted that he admitted predication according to [respect]{g:habitudo}, as the other Doctor [did].[^11] And that is famous of old, thus: "Whenever the same is predicated of itself, or what is per se higher of its per se lower, the predication is true, although neither extreme is in act. As it is true, says the Doctor, that 'Caesar is a man', even no man existing." But when a thing of another genus is predicated formally, then he [limits]{g:limitare} [it] to actual predication; as, "if a man is of a quality, of a quantity, a father, acting, and so on, then he is in act."

## ¶7 · 51.16–51.26

> [p. 51] Et cum illo concordat Anselmus in De Concordia 5° ca°. ita dicens: ‘Quippe non idem est, rem esse preteritam, et, rem preteritam esse preteritam; aut,¹¹ rem esse presentem, et, rem presentem esse presentem; aut,¹¹ rem esse futuram, et, rem futuram esse futuram: sicut non est idem, rem esse albam, et, rem albam esse albam: lignum enim non¹² de necessitate semper est album, quia¹³ aliquando antequam fieret album, potuit non fieri album; et¹⁴ postquam fieret album,¹⁴ potest fieri non album: sed lignum album de necessitate semper est album, quia non potest¹⁵ fieri, ut album simul sit non album.’

And with that Anselm agrees in *De concordia*, c. 5, saying thus: "For indeed it is not the same, 'the thing is past' and 'the past thing is past'; or 'the thing is present' and 'the present thing is present'; or 'the thing is future' and 'the future thing is future': just as it is not the same, 'the thing is white' and 'the white thing is white'. For wood is not of necessity always white, because at some time, before it became white, it could have not become white; and after it became white, it can become not white: but white wood is of necessity always white, because it cannot come about that white at the same time is not white."[^12]

## ¶8 · 51.27–52.12

> [p. 51] Volunt ergo isti doctores quod necessaria sit predicacio aptitudinalis secundum esse possibile in causis secundis, quandocunque predicatur idem de se vel per se superius de suo per se inferiori. Nec ex hoc concluditur existencia talis rei. Nec contendo multum circa hanc logicam, cum nescio¹⁶ [p. 52] eam efficaciter impugnare. Sed considero quod si moderni dignarentur¹ attendere ad scripturas patrum priorum, non tantum mirarentur de hoc quod dico: Omne preteritum vel futurum esse pro suo tempore: quia ex illo dicto sequitur nedum omne preteritum vel futurum sed eciam omne possibile esse² pro semper. Et sic videtur quod infiniti homines, sicut quelibet signanda individua, sunt ubique; nam mille homines sunt mille homines, et sic infinitum,³ nullo homine existente. Nec videtur racio quare semper, quin⁴ per idem ubique et sic quilibet⁵ infinitum bonus foret infinitum bonus, et sic de quacunque denominacione possibili.

These doctors therefore hold that aptitudinal predication according to possible being in second causes is necessary, whenever the same is predicated of itself, or what is per se higher of its per se lower. Nor is the existence of such a thing [inferred]{g:concludere} from this. Nor do I contend much about this logic, since I do not know how [52] to attack it effectively. But I consider that if the moderns deigned to attend to the writings of the earlier fathers, they would not wonder so much at what I say, "Every past or future thing is for its own time": because from that saying it follows [not only]{g:nedum} that every past or future thing but also that every possible thing is for always.[^13] And so it seems that infinite men, like any [individuals]{g:individuum} one might designate, are everywhere; for a thousand men are a thousand men, and so to infinity, no man existing. Nor does there seem to be a reason why [this holds] always without, by the same token, [holding] everywhere; and so anyone infinitely good would be infinitely good, and so of any possible naming.[^14]

## ¶9 · 52.13–52.27

> [p. 52] Sed dimittendo istud argumentum, ad propositum quod sequitur: Christum fuisse⁶ hominem in triduo. Nam Christus est per se in specie humana, ut patet posterius: ergo est per se homo: et per consequens, si est Christus, tunc est homo. Confirmatur. Sicut conceditur quod Christus est Christus, sic concedi debet quod iste homo est iste homo, demonstrando Iesum.⁷ Sed impossibile est istum hominem esse istum hominem, nisi iste homo sit⁸ homo. Ergo concesso quod Christus est Christus pro triduo, concedendum est ipsum esse hominem pro eodem. Unde dicit Doctor quod,†⁹ ‘si hec esset per se Christus est homo et sufficiens quod causet inherenciam predicati ad subiectum, sine hoc quod subiectum dicat compositum unum hoc Christus est homo fuisset semper vera. Sed magis, inquit, credo quod requiritur hoc et plus.’⁹†

But leaving that argument, [I come] to the matter in hand, which follows: "Christ was man in the three days". For Christ is per se in the human species, as is clear later: therefore he is per se man: and consequently, if he is Christ, then he is man. This is confirmed. Just as it is granted that Christ is Christ, so it ought to be granted that this man is this man, pointing to Jesus. Now it is impossible that this man be this man unless this man is man. Therefore, granted that Christ is Christ for the three days, it must be granted that he is man for the same [time]. Hence the Doctor says that † "if this were per se, 'Christ is man', and sufficient that it cause the inherence of the predicate in the subject, without the subject's signifying one composite, this, 'Christ is man', would always have been true. But rather, he says, I believe that this is required, and more." †[^15]

## ¶10 · 52.28–53.14

> [p. 52] Item sicut conceditur quod pro triduo ille homo est ille homo, ita concedi debet ut convertibile per se, quod ille [p. 53] homo componitur ex hoc corpore et hac anima. Ex quo plane sequitur quod ille homo sit homo, sicut ille homo est Deus-homo. Nam Christus et Deus-homo convertuntur¹ simpliciter et ex equo:¹ et per consequens, sicut conceditur quod Christus est Christus, sic concedi debet quod Deus-homo est Deus-homo. Sicut ergo conceditur quod rosa est flos propter possibilitatem² licet nulla existat; sic evidencius videtur concedendum quod hec persona est homo, posito quod essemus in triduo cum sit nedum remota potencia, sed disposicio propinqua et necessaria, ut resurgat. Unde propter essenciam materialem perpetuam videtur Ieronimus³ in Ep. ad Paulam et Eustochium concedere quod Christus a principio seculi erat Christus: sed Doctor ille concedit ad sensum alium.

Again, just as it is granted that for the three days that man is that man, so it ought to be granted, as [convertible]{g:convertibilis} per se, that that [53] man is composed of this body and this soul. From which it plainly follows that that man is man, just as that man is God-man. For "Christ" and "God-man" are convertible [without qualification]{g:simpliciter} and on equal terms: and consequently, just as it is granted that Christ is Christ, so it ought to be granted that God-man is God-man.[^16] Just as therefore it is granted that a rose is a flower on account of possibility, though none exists, so it seems more evidently to be granted that this [person]{g:persona} is man, supposing that we were in the three days, since there is not only a remote [potency]{g:potencia} but a proximate and necessary disposition that he rise again.[^17] Hence on account of the perpetual material [essence]{g:essencia} Jerome seems, in the Letter to Paula and Eustochium, to grant that Christ was Christ from the beginning of the world: but that Doctor grants it in another sense.[^18]

## ¶11 · 53.15–53.27

> [p. 53] Item cum Christus secundum illum doctorem habet omnes partes⁴ quas habet post resurreccionem, non deficit ad hoc quod tunc⁵ sit homo nisi unio respectiva: sed nullum tale respectivum est de essencia substancie ut patet alibi De Continuacione: ergo corpus et anima unita Verbo pro triduo sufficiunt⁶ denominare ipsum esse hominem. Minor patet ex hoc: quod addita unione illa ad alia principia existencia⁷ pro illo triduo,⁷ foret consequens formaliter, quod Christus sit homo: ergo tunc non deficit⁸ nisi⁹ dicta unio; nec valet dicere quod humanitas deficit;⁸ quia humanitas est quod ipse est homo: ergo non est previe requisita¹⁰ ad hoc quod ipse sit homo, quia tunc idem esset previe requisitum ad se ipsum.

Again, since Christ, according to that doctor, has all the parts which he has after the resurrection, nothing is lacking for his being man then except the relational union: but no such relational thing is of the essence of substance, as is clear elsewhere, in *De continuacione*:[^19] therefore the body and soul united to the Word for the three days suffice to name him as being man. The minor is clear from this: that, that union being added to the other principles existing for that three days, it would be [consequent]{g:consequens} formally that Christ is man: therefore nothing is then lacking but the said union; nor does it avail to say that the humanity is lacking; because the humanity is that he is man: therefore it is not [required beforehand]{g:previe-requisitum} for his being man, because then the same thing would be required beforehand for itself.[^20]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** 49.1 *communicacio ydiomatum*: the communication of idioms, the exchange of predicates between the natures in the one person. *Sentencie* is "teachings", positions held (as in the bracket); "[ours]" is supplied for the bare *iuxta posite sunt*.
[^2]: **T.** 49.6 *habitudinalis*, with A B C O, against the print's *aptitudinalis*, Harris's emendation from the definitions that follow (*secundum puram aptitudinem*, 49.8; *partim aptitudinalis*, 49.15–16), which he says confirm it. The manuscripts' word construes, a predication by relation (cf. *secundum habitudinem*, 51.7). 49.4 *Distinccionem 12*: O reads *22*, the distinction on Christ in the three days; Bonaventure, *In III Sent.* d. 22, to be checked (LEDGER I04a-1). O *duplex* for *triplex*.
[^3]: **C.** 49.10 *ordinacio unius ad alterum*: "ordering", the necessary relation of predicate to subject, not "ordinance" (the political works' sense). O reads *nata* for *necessaria*.
[^4]: **C.** 49.23 *esse hominis*: "the being of a man", that he is a man, not existence as such.
[^5]: **S.** The Subtle Doctor is Duns Scotus; *ibidem*, on the same distinction of book III, d. 22 (q. un.), in his *Ordinatio* or *Lectura*. The quotations are to be checked (LEDGER I04a-2).
[^6]: **T.** 50.3 *unitatem absolutam*: A B C *humanitatem*; 50.7 *forma totalitatis*: A B C *totalitas* ("since it is a totality"). The print is rendered at both.
[^7]: **S.** John Damascene, *De fide orthodoxa* (c. 49 in Burgundio's numbering, book III), on "Christ" as the name of the hypostasis; to be checked (LEDGER I04a-3). 50.11 *singulatim*: A B C *significancium*, "a hypostatic name signifying two complete natures"; O *ypostasis* for *ypostaticum*.
[^8]: **C.** 50.14 *maius Deo est*: "something greater than God is (exists)": a subject whose notion is impossible makes nothing true of it; A B C read *maius de eo*.
[^9]: **C.** 50.28 *ideo non denominavit eum*: the subject is taken as the humanity, lacking; it may be the parts ("[they] did not name him"). *Ideo* is read on the plate. *Carneus* "fleshy" names from the matter; *carnalis* "carnal" savours of vice (50.31).
[^10]: **C.** 51.3–5 *Hoc Latinum huius doctoris*: Wyclif's remark on Scotus's wording, harder than Augustine's teaching; B adds *michi* in the margin.
[^11]: **C.** 51.7 *secundum habitudinem*: *habitudo* in its logical sense, "respect", the relation under which a predicate holds. Harris asks at the foot whether *aptitudinem* should be read (cf. p. 49). *Doctor alius*: Bonaventure.
[^12]: **S.** Anselm, *De concordia praescientiae ... cum libero arbitrio*, given as c. 5; the place is to be checked (LEDGER I04a-4). O omits 51.18–19 *rem ... aut*; at 51.23 A B C read *postquam est album*.
[^13]: **C.** 52.4–6 *ex illo dicto sequitur*: *illo dicto* is taken as the doctors' rule (the same predicated of itself is true without existence), which commits them to more than Wyclif's thesis on past and future; it could be read as Wyclif's own saying.
[^14]: **C.** 52.9–12 *Nec videtur racio quare semper, quin per idem ubique*: the rule has no reason to hold for all time and not, by the same token, for all places. *Quilibet infinitum bonus*: A (first hand) B C read *cuilibet*.
[^15]: **T.** 52.23–27: Harris's daggers mark the passage as corrupt: "a most difficult place, much corrupted, as the frequent erasures and varied readings of the manuscripts show". He prints A B C; O reads *si hic esset per se Christus est homo et sufficiat quod causet inherenciam predicati ad subiectum cum hoc quod subiectum dicat conceptum unum hic Christus est homo fuisset semper vera* ..., "with the subject signifying one concept". The print is rendered as it stands. Scotus, in the place cited above (LEDGER I04a-5).
[^16]: **T.** 53.4 *simpliciter et ex equo*: B *ex equo simpliciter*; O *simpliciter et e contrario*, "without qualification and conversely".
[^17]: **C.** 53.9 *remota potencia*: "potency" in Aristotle's sense, set against the proximate disposition to rise again; not "power".
[^18]: **S.** Jerome, letter to Paula and Eustochium, place not given; on what Christ was "from the beginning of the world", to be checked (LEDGER I04a-6). A B C *Ieronimum*.
[^19]: **S.** *De continuacione*: a treatise of Wyclif's own, cited "elsewhere"; the work and the place are to be checked (LEDGER I04a-7).
[^20]: **C.** 53.20 *Minor patet*: the proof given supports the first premiss (nothing lacking but the union); Wyclif calls it the minor. 53.25 *non est previe requisita*: the subject is taken as the humanity; Harris's foot ("*requisita* in all the manuscripts, that is, *unio*") takes it as the union. C omits 53.23–24 *nisi ... deficit*.
