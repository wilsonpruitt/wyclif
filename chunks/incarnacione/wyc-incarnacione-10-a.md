# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 10, part 1: the moderns' negation, and the first arguments against it

## ¶1 · 160.0–160.0

<!-- the editor's bracketed argument at the chapter head: a Latin summary, then his own English
     summary in the same bracket (see the volume CARD for what is translated) -->

> [p. 160] [Suadet 12 evidenciis quod humanitas assumpta sit Christus; et hoc roborat doctorum testimoniis et exemplis. The proposition that the humanity assumed is Christ proved by twelve arguments; and supported by the authority of the Fathers.]

[It [makes the case]{g:suadere} with twelve pieces of [evidence]{g:evidencia} that [the humanity]{g:humanitas} [assumed]{g:assumere} is Christ; and it strengthens this with the testimonies and examples of the doctors.]

## ¶2 · 160.10–161.2

> [p. 160] Quia maior pars variacionis in ista materia stat in methaphisica de humanitate assumpta; ideo expedit iuxta ponere sentencias contrarias supradictis. Dicunt enim moderni concorditer¹ quod humanitas Christi nec est homo, nec suppositum, vel persona, licet sit substancia racionalis composita² ex corpore² et anima, que sunt partes hominis Iesu Christi; sed dimissa illa natura suppositacioni proprie,³ ut⁴ possit per possibile⁴ quod maneat non assumpta, tunc foret homo et modo non est homo. Ideo dicunt quod diffinicio hominis negacionem includit formaliter, ita quod descriptive homo sit substancia ex corpore et anima intellectiva composita⁵ non suppositata suppositacione⁶ aliena. Et quia ultima differencia deficit nature assumpte cum [p. 161] suppositatur suppositacione Verbi; ideo¹ stante assumpcione ypostatica non est homo.

Because the greater part of the variation in this matter lies in the metaphysics of the assumed humanity, it is therefore expedient to set beside [them] the [teachings]{g:sentencia} contrary to those stated above. For [the moderns]{g:moderni} say with one accord[^1] that the humanity of Christ is neither man, nor [supposit]{g:suppositum}, nor [person]{g:persona}, although it is a rational substance composed of body and soul, which are parts of the man Jesus Christ; but, that [nature]{g:natura} [laid aside]{g:dimittere} to its own [suppositation]{g:suppositacio}, as it can be, by a possible case, that it remain unassumed, then it would be a man; and now it is not a man.[^2] Therefore they say that the [definition]{g:diffinicio} of man includes a negation formally, so that, by way of definition, man is a substance composed of body and intellective soul, not supposited by an alien suppositation.[^3] And because the last [difference]{g:differencia} is lacking to the assumed nature when [161] it is supposited by the suppositation of the [Word]{g:verbum}, therefore, while the hypostatic [assumption]{g:assumpcio} stands, it is not a man.

## ¶3 · 161.3–161.19

> [p. 161] Ista sentencia² iam famosa est michi et forte aliis magis difficilis quam aliqua sentencia antiquorum doctorum superius inculcata. Primo ergo videtur quod Christi humanitas non suppositatur suppositacione aliena: nam³ nec est nec fit suppositum: et omne suppositari est fieri vel esse suppositum: ergo non suppositatur. Preterea si aliqua suppositacione suppositatur cum illa nulli⁴ alii creature⁴ conveniat, videtur quod sit suppositacio sibi propria, et sic non aliqualiter aliena. Confirmatur ex hoc quod suppositacio qua suppositatur formaliter est solummodo temporalis et per consequens dependet ab illa humanitate, sicut accidens respectivum dependet essencialiter ab extremo. Quod si dicatur suppositacionem illam esse suppositum Verbi, tunc indubie cuiuslibet suppositi creati suppositacio dependet a suppositacione aliena, quia a supposito increato et ad illud finaliter terminatur; et per consequens nullum esset suppositum creatum.

This teaching, now well known, is to me, and perhaps to others, more difficult than any teaching of the ancient doctors pressed home above.[^4] First, then, it seems that the humanity of Christ is not supposited by an alien suppositation: for it neither is nor becomes a supposit: and every being-supposited is becoming or being a supposit: therefore it is not supposited. Moreover, if it is supposited by some suppositation, since that belongs to no other [creature]{g:creatura}, it seems that it is a suppositation proper to it, and so not in any way alien. This is confirmed from this: the suppositation by which it is formally supposited is only temporal, and consequently depends on that humanity, as a [relational]{g:respectivus} [accident]{g:accidens} depends essentially on its extreme.[^5] But if it is said that that suppositation is the supposit of the Word, then undoubtedly the suppositation of any [created]{g:creatus} supposit depends on an alien suppositation, since it is ultimately terminated from the uncreated supposit and at it; and consequently there would be no created supposit.

## ¶4 · 161.20–162.11

> [p. 161] Sed hic dicit Doctor Subtilis super 3° Sentenciarum Dist. 1ᵃ Questione articuli 3ⁱ quod est dare triplicem⁵ negacionem, sicut et triplicem dependenciam pertinentem. Aliquid enim⁶ actualiter dependet a suppositacione aliena, quando est natura completa suppositi nature superioris; ut humanitas Christi⁷ non est suppositum, sed natura suppositi Verbi; et si Verbum faceret ipsam esse non naturam persone⁸ superioris, tunc esset suppositum et persona. Nichil⁹ ergo deficit ad hoc quod sit persona nisi ista negacio quod non est natura suppositi superioris nature. Ideo suppositacio, quam habet in potencia terminatur suppositacione Verbi, que non actu sed potencia est aliena a suppositacione nature humane. 2ᵃ dependencia vocatur potencialis;¹⁰ quando scilicet [p. 162] natura racionalis dependet a supposito increato a quo potest assumi, sed nondum assumitur in unitatem persone; sed et talem negacionem oportet naturam creatam habere, ut sit persona¹ hominis. 3ᵃ est dependencia obediencialis,² qua omne suppositum substancie essencialis dependet a qualibet persona increata, sed eciam³ potest assumi in unitatem suppositi increati, ut sit communicacio⁴ ydiomatum, et ut ipso facto natura persone increate vere Deus sit lapis vel lignum vel cuiuscunque speciei substancie⁵ individuum; sicut Deus iam est individuum speciei⁵ humane, licet natura talis non possit esse⁶ persona divina.

But here [the Subtle Doctor]{g:doctor-subtilis}, on the third [book] of the *Sentences*, dist. 1, question, article 3, says that [one may posit]{g:est-dare} a threefold negation, as also a threefold pertinent dependence.[^6] For something actually depends on an alien suppositation when it is the complete nature of a supposit of a higher nature; as the humanity of Christ is not a supposit but the nature of the supposit of the Word; and if the Word made it not to be the nature of a higher person, then it would be a supposit and a person. Nothing, therefore, is lacking for it to be a person but this negation, that it is not the nature of a supposit of a higher nature. Therefore the suppositation which it has in [potency]{g:potencia} is terminated by the suppositation of the Word, which is alien from the suppositation of the human nature not in act but in potency.[^7] The second dependence is called potential: namely when [162] a rational nature depends on an uncreated supposit by which it can be assumed, but is not yet assumed into the unity of person; but a created nature too must have such a negation, that it may be a person of a man.[^8] The third is obediential dependence, by which every supposit of an essential substance depends on any uncreated person, but also can be assumed into the unity of an uncreated supposit, so that there is a [communication of idioms]{g:communicacio-ydiomatum}, and so that by that very fact the nature of an uncreated person, truly God, is a stone or wood or an [individual]{g:individuum} of any species of substance; as God now is an individual of the human species, although such a nature cannot be a divine person.[^9]

## ¶5 · 162.12–162.25

> [p. 162] Dimissa autem contencione verbali, quod suppositacio Verbi non est aliena a suppositacione nature assumpte, cum illa non sit; arguitur primo contra illud⁷ per hoc quod omnis homo-non-Deus per se est homo: omnis natura humana completa non assumpta est homo: ergo omnis talis natura per se est homo, et per consequens non potest vicissim nunc esse homo et nunc non-homo. Maior licet sit famosa apud philosophos potest⁸ ex hoc patescere, quia aliter humanitas non esset species vel quiditas cuiuscunque persone⁹ hominis; cum potest manere idem quod est modo¹⁰ et non homo. Non ergo predicatur homo in quid de aliquo supposito¹¹ humano; et per idem nulla potest esse per se species in genere substancie, cum omnis substancia potest esse idem quod est, et non esse individuum huius speciei.

But, setting aside the verbal contention that the suppositation of the Word is not alien from the suppositation of the assumed nature, since the latter is not,[^10] [it is argued]{g:arguitur-per} first against it by appeal to this: every man-not-God is [of itself]{g:per-se} a man: every complete human nature not assumed is a man: therefore every such nature is of itself a man, and consequently it cannot by turns be now man and now not-man. The major, although it is well known among the philosophers, can be made plain from this: that otherwise the humanity would not be the species or [quiddity]{g:quiditas} of any person of a man, since it can remain the same as it is now and [be] not a man. Therefore "man" is not predicated in quid of any human supposit; and by the same token no species can be of itself in the genus of substance, since every substance can be the same as it is and not be an individual of this species.[^11]

## ¶6 · 162.26–163.13

> [p. 162] Item impossibile est aliquid esse accidentaliter alicuiusmodi¹² nisi sibi insit accidens a quo est huiusmodi formaliter sicut patet ponenti respectus et accidencia distincta: sed omnis homo iuxta hanc viam accidentaliter est homo: ergo est dare formam accidentalem qua est homo formaliter. Sed et hoc concedunt plurimi dicentes quod in assumpcione ypostatica negacio supradicta corrumpitur et in dimissione [p. 163] ypostatica generatur: et per illam est homo formaliter aliquid positivum, ut nichil, in quantum caret visu vel non habet hoc vel illud, est aliquid. Ex quo sequitur quod nichil in quantum homo vel persona hominis est aliquid vel alicuiusmodi¹ positivum; cum quicquid aut cuiusmodicunque² homo nunc est, assistente negacione quod non suppositatur² suppositacione aliena, potest esse adveniente suppositacione aliena ipso facto non³ homine vel persona. Quod est contra Augustinum 7° De Trinitate 9° volente, quod persona in quantum huiusmodi, est aliquid, non ad aliquid. Si ergo nemo potest fieri qualis aut quantus nisi moveatur, multo magis nemo potest fieri homo cum hoc quod non exinde moveatur; aliter enim non esset generacio.

Again, it is impossible for something to be accidentally of some kind unless an accident is in it by which it is formally of this kind, as is clear to one who posits [relations]{g:respectus} and accidents distinct [from their subjects]: now every man, according to this way, is accidentally a man: therefore one may posit an accidental form by which he is formally a man. But most concede this too, saying that in the hypostatic assumption the negation stated above is corrupted, and in the hypostatic laying-aside [163] it is generated: and by it man is formally something positive, as nothing, [insofar as]{g:in-quantum} it lacks sight or does not have this or that, is something.[^12] From which [it follows]{g:sequitur} that nothing, insofar as [it is] man or the person of a man, is something or of some positive kind; since whatever, or of whatever kind, a man now is, with the negation present that he is not supposited by an alien suppositation, he can be, with an alien suppositation supervening, he being by that very fact not a man or person.[^13] Which is against Augustine, *De Trinitate* 7, [chapter] 9, who holds that a person, insofar as [it is] such, is something, not relative to something.[^14] If, then, no one can become qualified or [quantified]{g:quantus} unless he is moved, much more can no one become a man without being moved thereby; for otherwise there would not be generation.

## ¶7 · 163.14–163.33

> [p. 163] Item eo ipso quod forma inseparabilis inest⁴ mere formaliter alicui, ipsum denominat⁵: sed humanitas est forma inseparabiliter existens cuicumque nature humane: ergo si inest alicui singulari nature ipsam denominat⁵: sed non potest denominare quicquam primo formaliter nisi hominem: ergo, si inest nature create formaliter, ipsa est homo. Cum⁶ ergo humanitas manet continue in natura humana assumpta, videtur quod stante assumpcione erit homo continue sicut prius. Minor videtur ex hoc quod omnis homo est formaliter humanitate homo, sicut mobile movetur motu formaliter, album denominatur albedine, et paciens passione. Humanitas ergo manens post unionem denominat⁵ formaliter Christum esse hominem et non dicta negacio: ymo cum ipse sit homo⁷ nobiscum univoce et in⁷ sua racione, non oportet negacionem ponere, sed ipse humanitate est homo formaliter, videlicet⁸ quod eadem sit racio cuiuslibet fratris sui. Ipse enim non potest suppositari suppositacione aliena, nec negacione; sed humanitate oportet ipsum esse singulare speciei humane formaliter: ergo per idem et alios univoce consimilis speciei.

Again, by the very fact that an inseparable form is merely formally in something, it [names]{g:denominare} it: now the humanity is a form existing inseparably in any human nature: therefore, if it is in any singular nature, it names it: now it cannot name anything primarily [and] formally except a man: therefore, if it is formally in a created nature, that [nature] is a man. Since, then, the humanity remains continually in the assumed human nature, it seems that, while the assumption stands, it will be a man continually, as before. The minor appears from this: every man is formally a man by humanity, as a [movable]{g:mobile} is moved formally by motion, a white thing is named by whiteness, and what is acted on by [being-acted-on]{g:passio}.[^15] The humanity, therefore, remaining after the [union]{g:unio}, formally names Christ to be a man, and not the said negation: indeed, since he is a man [univocally]{g:univoce} with us and in his own account, it is not necessary to posit a negation, but he is formally a man by humanity, namely so that the account of each of his brothers is the same.[^16] For he cannot be supposited by an alien suppositation, nor by a negation; but by humanity he must be formally a singular of the human species: therefore by the same token others too, univocally of a like species.

## ¶8 · 164.1–164.27

> [p. 164] Item iuxta dicta De Universalibus omnis humanitas est suppositum humanum esse hominem: sed, posito quod Verbum assumat ypostatice naturam Petri, manebit ista assumpta humanitas: ergo manebit quod idem suppositum est homo; et sic personam manentem personam Verbum assumeret. Pro¹ assumpto non est facile fingere, quid sit humanitas, quam Apostolus vocat ‘formam servi,’² nisi esset³ hominis vel humana essencia. Et ex isto⁴ videtur quod alia nova humanitas incepit⁵ esse homo, quando Christus incepit esse homo, humanitate⁵ Petri extincta per assumpcionem; quia quod Verbum est homo, tunc incipit⁶ esse et quod Petrus est homo tunc desinit. Ex quo patet quod non assumpsit naturam Petri, cum illa natura manet modo⁷ tantum distans a Verbo ut prius. Nam illa res, que prius⁸ fuit suppositum Petri, nec est natura nec persona divina, nec est homo modo sicut nec Christus est natura humana: per antecedens⁹ vero ista res et Christus conveniebant in specie sic quod uterque fuit homo ut suppono¹⁰: ergo maior fuit conveniencia tunc quam modo. Nec valet dicere quod Christus est homo formaliter non negacione¹¹ nec humanitate, sed ypostatica unione; quia sic foret racio qua¹² Christus est homo multum equivoca a racione qua quicumque alius est homo¹³; quod indubie sequitur¹³ multiplici racione. Nam quilibet alius homo dicitur formaliter homo negative¹⁴ et per consequens racione increata; Christus autem dicitur homo per unionem ypostaticam, que nulli alii potest competere.

Again, according to what is said in *De universalibus*, every humanity is "a human supposit's being a man": now, supposing that the Word hypostatically assumes the nature of Peter, this assumed humanity will remain: therefore it will remain that the same supposit is a man; and so the Word would assume a person remaining a person.[^17] For [the assumption]{g:assumptum} it is not easy to feign what the humanity is, which the Apostle calls "the form of a servant", unless it were the essence of a man, or a human essence.[^18] And from this it seems that another, new humanity began to be a man when Christ began to be a man, the humanity of Peter being extinguished through the assumption; since "the Word is a man" then began to be, and "Peter is a man" then ceases. From which it is clear that he did not assume the nature of Peter, since that nature now remains as far distant from the Word as before.[^19] For that thing which before was the supposit of Peter is neither a nature nor a divine person, nor is it now a man, just as Christ is not a human nature either: and beforehand this thing and Christ [agreed]{g:convenire} in species, so that each was a man, as I suppose: therefore there was greater [agreement]{g:conveniencia} then than now.[^20] Nor does it avail to say that Christ is formally a man not by negation nor by humanity, but by hypostatic union; since thus the account by which Christ is a man would be very equivocal from the account by which anyone else is a man; which undoubtedly follows for manifold reasons. For any other man is called formally a man negatively, and consequently by an uncreated account; but Christ is called a man through the hypostatic union, which can belong to no other.[^21]

[^1]: **T.** "With one accord": A B omit *concorditer*, C reads *conco* (160.13). The moderns' position, set out here and through the next paragraphs, is that the humanity is a man only by a negation (not being supposited by another's suppositation).

[^2]: **C/T.** *Suppositacioni proprie* is dative: the nature let go to a suppositation of its own; O reads *prope* "near". *Ut possit per possibile* (160.17) is the print; A B C all read *posito* (C *ut posito possibile*, B *ait posito per possibile*, A *ut posito proporcionale*): one recension against O, and the print stands. "And now it is not a man": while it is assumed.

[^3]: **G/T.** *Diffinicio … descriptive*: the noun and then the adverb of *descripcio*, both "definition". The negation stands in the definition as its last difference. O reads *composit* (160.21); A has *suppositata* corrected over an erasure, B C read *supposicione* "supposition" for *suppositacione* (160.21).

[^4]: **T.** *Inculcata* "pressed home". O reads *sentenciam* (161.3) and *et iam* for *nam* (161.6).

[^5]: **C/T.** A relation depends on its extreme, the term it is toward; so a temporal suppositation would depend on the humanity it belongs to. O reads *nulli nature* "to no nature" for *nulli alii creature* (161.9).

[^6]: **S/T.** Scotus, on *Sentences* III, d. 1, q. 1, on the threefold dependence (LEDGER I10a-1, to be checked); Wyclif's "question, article 3" is given as printed. B omits *dare* (161.21); O omits *enim* (161.23) and *Christi* (161.25).

[^7]: **C/T.** *Potencia* in Aristotle's sense, potency against act. The foot notes that every manuscript abbreviates *persone* (161.27), and that the abbreviation can also mean *per se*: "not to be of itself a superior nature". O reads *vel* for *Nichil* (161.27).

[^8]: **T.** *Potencialis* "potential" (161.32) is O's reading, printed; A B C read *personalis* "personal". The print stands (one recension against O), and "potential" answers the *in potencia* of the first dependence. At 162.4 A B C read *pars hominis* "a part of a man" for *persona hominis*.

[^9]: **T/C.** *Obediencialis* (162.4) is O's alone: A B leave a space of twelve letters, C omits without one. [^10]: **C.** *Cum illa non sit*: the suppositation of the assumed nature is not, so nothing is "alien" to it; the plain existential sense.

[^11]: **C/T.** *Predicatur in quid*: predicated as answering "what is it?", the essential predication of a species. O omits *potest* (162.19) and *supposito* (162.23); B reads *per se* for *persone* (162.20); C *numero* for *modo* (162.21).

[^12]: **C/T.** A privation such as blindness is not something positive; the argument says the moderns' negation makes "man" no more positive than that. A B C read *alicuius* for *alicuiusmodi* (162.26–27, 163.5).

[^13]: **T.** A B C read *supponitur* for *suppositatur* (163.7); O reads *in* for *non* (163.8). *Ipso facto non homine vel persona* is an ablative absolute.

[^14]: **S.** Augustine, *De Trinitate* VII, that "person" is said of a thing in respect of itself, not relatively (LEDGER I10a-2, to be checked; Wyclif's "chapter 9").

[^15]: **G/T.** *Passio* here is the category against action: what is acted on is so by its being-acted-on, as a movable by motion. A B C omit *inest* (163.14) and read *denominant* (163.15, 17, 25); B adds *et* before *Cum* (163.19).
