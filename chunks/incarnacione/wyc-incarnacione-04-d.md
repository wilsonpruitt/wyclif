# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 4, part 4: the Son of God a dead person; the willing soul; the dead body that is God

## ¶1 · 61.21–62.11

> [p. 61] Item logice¹⁶ potest deduci illud, posito quod solum¹⁷ Christus¹⁸ sit¹⁹ mortuus de specie humana: tunc Filius Dei est mortuus, licet non sit homo ut conceditur¹⁹: ergo Filius Dei est persona mortua. Consequencia videtur tenere,²⁰ eo quod persona est superius ad hominem secundum tenentes istam sentenciam, et in talibus eodem distrahente addito conformiter utrobique tenet consequencia, ut: Si Petrus est homo mortuus, tunc est animal mortuum et corpus mortuum. Si ergo Christus sit persona mortua, cum non superest que persona nisi persona Verbi, sequitur quod sit persona Verbi [p. 62] mortua; et per consequens est persona Verbi, que moriebatnr; et sic persona Verbi est Christus. Si enim caput pedes et alia derelicta pro triduo sint partes hominis mortui, et solum Filius Dei sit homo mortuus; sequitur quod sunt¹ partes Filii Dei. Unde propter istam unionem ypostaticam Verbi ad² illud sanctum corpus sepultum in triduo dicit Doctor Solempnis 12° Quodlibeto: quod illud servatum est nedum incorruptum sed incorruptibile iuxta illud Psalmi 15' Non dabis sanctum tuum videre corrupcionem.' Nec video quomodo esset naturaliter inditum³ homini timere mortem, nisi poterit pati mortem. Non enim ordinat natura hominem timere eventum, qui⁴ non potest sibi accidere.

Again, this can be deduced logically, supposing that, of the human species, only Christ is dead, although, as is granted, he is not a man: then the Son of God is a dead man: therefore the Son of God is a dead [person]{g:persona}.[^1] The [inference]{g:consequencia} seems to hold, because person is superior to man according to those who hold this [teaching]{g:sentencia}, and in such [cases], the same [diminishing qualifier]{g:distrahens} being added in conformity on both sides, the inference holds; as: If Peter is a dead man, then he is a dead animal and a dead body.[^2] If, therefore, Christ is a dead person, since no person remains but the person of [the Word]{g:verbum}, [it follows]{g:sequitur} that he is the person of the Word, [62] dead; and in consequence he is the person of the Word that was dying; and so the person of the Word is Christ.[^3] For if the head, the feet, and the other [parts] left behind for [the three days]{g:triduum} are parts of a dead man, and only the Son of God is a dead man, it follows that they are parts of the Son of God. Hence, because of this hypostatic [union]{g:unio} of the Word to that holy body buried in the three days, the Solemn Doctor says in his twelfth *Quodlibet* that it was preserved [not only]{g:nedum} uncorrupted but incorruptible, according to that of Psalm 15: "Thou wilt not give thy holy one to see corruption."[^4] Nor do I see how it would be naturally implanted in man to fear death, unless he could suffer death. For nature does not ordain a man to fear an event that cannot befall him.

## ¶2 · 62.12–63.4

> [p. 62] Item si anima Christi desiit esse anima Verbi Dei, cum tunc et ante scivit omnia, sequitur quod hoc scivit continue. Queritur⁵ utrum voluntarie⁶ reliquit corpus in morte vel non. Quod sic patet: quia fuit obediens usque ad mortem inclusive et post perpetuo. Nec illud debet verti in dubium⁷ cum scivit hoc esse de necessario Dei beneplacito ad exaltacionem sui et sui generis. Unde Augustinus Ome. 119.⁸' Quis, inquit, ita dormivit quando voluit sicut Christus?⁸ Oblatus est enim, quia voluit, ut dicitur Isᵉ. 52. Si volens⁹ fuerit ypostatice⁹ separata, cum ex hoc perdidit graciam unionis; sequitur quod sic volendo peccavit, quia voluntarie defecit a gracia; quod nephas¹⁰ est dicere. Et consequencia videtur ex hoc¹⁰: quod anima propior¹¹ est Deo, cum est anima Dei, quam foret ab eo ypostatice separata: quia aliter esset possibile Deo¹³ plus vel tantum diligere ipsam animam alterius sicut suam: quod non puto. Cum ergo anima Iesu gratis elongavit se a Verbo postquam fuit sibi carior ypostatice copulata: sequitur quod de tanto graciam perdidit et per consequens sic peccavit. Nam nulli dubium, quin maxima gracia possibilis creature est quod sit pars qualitativa [p. 63] vel humanitas Dei sui. Si enim magna gracia est quod Deus velit¹ adoptare hominem in filium et heredem, longe maior est quod velit creaturam eternaliter predestinare in suam propriam animam vel humanitatem.

Again, if the soul of Christ ceased to be the soul of the Word of God, since then and before it knew all things, it follows that it knew this continuously. It is asked whether it left the body in death willingly or not. That it did is clear: because he was obedient unto death inclusively, and afterwards perpetually.[^5] Nor ought this to be called into doubt, since it knew this to be of God's necessary good pleasure, for the exaltation of himself and of his kind. Hence Augustine, Homily 119: "Who," he says, "so slept when he willed as Christ?" For he was offered because he willed, as is said in Isaiah 52.[^6] If, willing, it was hypostatically separated, since by this it lost [the grace of union]{g:gracia-unionis}, it follows that by so willing it sinned, because it willingly fell away from grace; which it is impious to say. And the inference seems [to hold] from this: that the soul is nearer to God when it is the soul of God than it would be [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} separated from him: because otherwise it would be possible for God to love the soul of another more than or as much as his own: which I do not think.[^7] Since, therefore, the soul of Jesus freely distanced itself from the Word after it had been dearer to him hypostatically joined, it follows that to that extent it lost grace and in consequence so sinned. For no one doubts that the greatest grace possible to a [creature]{g:creatura} is that it be a [qualitative part]{g:pars-qualitativa} [63] or [the humanity]{g:humanitas} of its God. For if it is a great grace that God should will to adopt a man as son and heir, far greater is it that he should will to predestine a creature eternally to be his own soul or humanity.

## ¶3 · 63.5–64.15

> [p. 63] Item iuxta sepe dicta et diffusius declarata impossibile est aliquam substanciam esse² materiam vel³ formam vel ex eis compositam, nisi sit singularis essencia vel persona per se supposita speciei: sed tam corpus Christi quam eius anima fuit pro illo⁴ triduo individua substancia: igitur⁵ fuit essencia vel persona per se supposita speciei: et cum nullum suppositum potest manere aliud⁶ suppositum, sequitur quod utraque illarum naturarum manet idem suppositum quod fuit,⁷ anima et corpore, copulatum:⁷ sed⁸ tunc fuit suppositum hominis,⁸ cum cuiuslibet hominis personalitas sit supposicio utriusque partis qualitative: igitur⁵ utraque natura manet adhuc idem⁹ suppositum hominis, et per consequens eadem persona Verbi. Et illa racio foret evidens cuicumque cui assumptum principium est famosum: ‘Unde quia de virtute sermonis’: sequitur ex hoc principio cum veritate fidei quod tam corpus Christi quam eius anima quantumlibet a se invicem¹⁰ separata sint, utrumque¹¹ eadem persona hominis, que fuerant,¹² copulata. Ideo concedendum est quod pro triduo ypostatice sint unita et patet quomodo Verbum non potest assumere corpus mortuum, nisi mediante assumcione anime naturaliter precedente. Nec alteram illarum naturarum potest assumere,¹³ nisi faciat se esse¹⁴ illam naturam, cum nulla tali distinguit¹⁵ essencialiter vel specialiter suppositacio a natura. Et patet 3°. quod Verbum non posset assumere corpus et animam separata, nisi faceret se esse¹⁴ eandem personam que fuerunt tam corpus [p. 64] quam anima copulata. Ideo non sollicitor quid sequeretur,¹ si Verbum assumeret unione² ypostatica tam corpus quam animam proditoris et dimitteret ipsa³ suppositacioni proprie in finali resurreccione. Nam certum videtur michi⁴ quod peccatum inde foret penitus deletum, eo quod ypostatica assumcio esset⁵ efficacius medium ad tergendum peccatum quam lavacrum baptismale: sed istud delet omne peccatum suppositi vel nature: ergo multo magis personalis assumcio. Et ex alio latere puto me esse securum quod peccatum finalis impenitencie⁶ subiectatum in anima proditoris⁷ non potest deleri, vel⁸ post eius incorrupcionem⁸ redire. Ideo non superest nisi fugere tales casus. Nec est negandum, quin illud corpus mortuum⁹ sit suppositum, sicut et spiritus; cum utrumque¹⁰ habet supposibilem operacionem secundum idem suppositum;⁹ quia persona Filii est utrumque.¹⁰

Again, according to what has often been said and more fully made clear, it is impossible that any substance be matter or form or composed of them, unless it is a singular [essence]{g:essencia} or person supposited by itself under a species: now both the body of Christ and his soul were for those three days an [individual]{g:individuum} substance: therefore [each] was an essence or person supposited by itself under a species: and since no [supposit]{g:suppositum} can remain another supposit, it follows that each of those [natures]{g:natura} remains the same supposit that it was, soul and body, when joined: now it was then the supposit of a man, since the personality of any man is the supposition of both qualitative parts: therefore each nature remains still the same supposit of a man, and in consequence the same person of the Word.[^8] And that argument would be evident to anyone to whom the assumed principle is well known: "Hence because [in the proper sense of the words]{g:de-virtute-sermonis}": it follows from this principle, with the truth of faith, that both the body of Christ and his soul, however far separated from each other they may be, are each the same person of a man that they had been, joined.[^9] Therefore it must be granted that for the three days they are hypostatically united; and it is clear how the Word cannot [assume]{g:assumere} a dead body except by means of the assumption of the soul naturally preceding. Nor can he assume either of those natures unless he makes himself to be that nature, since in no such [case] is [suppositation]{g:suppositacio} distinguished essentially or specifically from nature. And it is clear, third, that the Word could not assume body and soul separated unless he made himself to be the same person that both body [64] and soul, joined, were.[^10] Therefore I am not anxious about what would follow if the Word assumed by hypostatic union both the body and the soul of [the traitor]{g:proditor} and [laid them aside]{g:dimittere} to their own suppositation at the final resurrection.[^11] For it seems certain to me that the sin would thereby be wholly deleted, because hypostatic assumption would be a more effective means for wiping away sin than the baptismal washing: now that deletes every sin of the supposit or of the nature: therefore much more the personal assumption. And on the other side I think I am secure that the sin of final impenitence, subjected in the soul of the traitor, cannot be deleted, or return after its incorruption.[^12] Therefore nothing remains but to flee such cases. Nor is it to be denied that that dead body is a supposit, as is the spirit too; since each has an operation [that can be supposited]{g:supposibilis} as to the same supposit; because the person of the Son is each.[^13]

## ¶4 · 64.16–65.17

> [p. 64] Et si obiciatur quod pie aures abhorrent quod Deus universitatis¹¹ sit corpus vel cadaver mortuum; dicitur quod Deus non est cadaver, cum non sit mortuum corpus et putidum¹² sed ad sensum proprium incorruptum. Quamvis enim laceratum sit lancea et flagellis causantibus solucionem¹³ continuitatis, non tamen crura eius fracta per milites, nec carnes eius canibus¹⁴ aut avibus¹⁴ laniate; quia Exod. 12 mistice est preceptum; ‘os non comminuetis ex eo.’ Nec horreret, sed accenderet pias mentes¹⁵ quod Dominus celi et terre, qui¹⁶ dignatus est nasci parvulus, vagiens in cunabulis,¹⁷ ac reponi coram bestiis in presepe, dignatus¹⁸ est idem et non alius iacere mortuus in sepulcro. Sed quia putrefaccio et incineracio corporis mortui non profuisset suo generi, cum corpus mortuum non mereretur¹⁹ sibi vel aliis: ideo inconsonum et superfluum fuisset quod dictas infirmitates [p. 65] post completam victoriam assumpsisset. Nec auderem¹ dicere quod corpus mortuum foret Deus, nisi scriptura sacra tam patenter illud assereret, Ioh. 19: nec crederem² capiti meo de exposicione sensus litteralis eius scripture, nisi Augustinus, Hugo de Sancto Victore, et ceteri superiores doctores illud patenter assererent: nec diligerem Verbum Dei pro tunc ut Deum meum, nisi pro tunc esset spiritus descendens ad inferos et cum hoc natura divina gubernans omnia.³ Nec video quomodo posset sumpsisse naturam⁴ porci, serpentis,⁴ vel alterius creature quam hominis, ut patet posterius. Nam si aliquid potuisset⁵ esse monstrum, potissime fuisset⁶ chimera vel tragelaphus habens caput asininum, caudam equinam, pectus leoninum.⁷ Et sic⁸ esset veracissime⁸ de Iesu nostro in casu, quo omnem naturam aliam⁹ assumeret et post assumpcionem continuaret. Vacuum ergo¹⁰ et periculosum est raciones tales asserere.

And if [it is objected]{g:obicitur} that pious ears shrink from [hearing] that the God of the universe is a body or a dead corpse, [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that God is not a corpse, since he is not a dead and putrid body but, in the proper sense, incorrupt.[^14] For although it was torn by the lance and by scourges causing a dissolution of [continuity]{g:continuacio}, yet his legs were not broken by the soldiers, nor his flesh torn by dogs or birds; because in Exodus 12 it is mystically commanded: "You shall not break a bone of him."[^15] Nor should it horrify, but kindle, pious minds that the Lord of heaven and earth, who deigned to be born a little child, wailing in the cradle, and to be laid before beasts in a manger, deigned, the same and no other, to lie dead in the sepulchre. But because the putrefaction and reduction to ashes of the dead body would not have profited his kind, since a dead body does not merit for itself or for others, therefore it would have been unfitting and superfluous that he should have assumed the said [weaknesses]{g:infirmitas} [65] after the complete victory.[^16] Nor would I dare to say that a dead body was God, unless holy scripture so plainly asserted it, John 19; nor would I trust my own head about the exposition of the literal sense of that scripture, unless Augustine, Hugh of St Victor, and the other higher doctors plainly asserted it; nor would I love the Word of God for that time as my God, unless for that time he were a spirit descending into hell and with this the divine nature governing all things.[^17] Nor do I see how he could have taken the nature of a pig, of a serpent, or of any creature other than man, as is clear later. For if anything can be a monster, it would most of all be a chimera or a tragelaphus, having an ass's head, a horse's tail, a lion's breast.[^18] And so it would most truly be with our Jesus in the case in which he assumed every other nature and after the assumption continued [it]. It is therefore empty and dangerous to assert such arguments.
