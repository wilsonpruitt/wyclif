# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 8, part 5: the virgin birth, the reach of secondary agents, and the third objection

## ¶1 · 138.23–140.21

> [p. 138] Et si dicatur quod Deus, non potest per se, nec cum tam disparibus secundis agentibus, tales effectus producere; tum,¹⁰ quia nec est¹¹ auctenticum nec expertum; sed raciones seminales forent superflue limitate¹²: quoad illud videtur michi quod talis contencio de Dei persona sit satis superflua, ubi non evidenter scimus racionem pro vel contra producere: unde leve verbum multorum est et satis inevidens—‘ego non sum expertus hoc, vel ego non inveni¹³ hoc scriptum in historiis michi auctenticis: ergo non est verum’.—Et ex [p. 139] alio latere sollicitando fingere quod agens secundum potest naturaliter in talia¹ inexperta excedit limites philosophi naturalis. Sed inter alia opera, que de Deo credimus, partus virginis tenet locum satis probabilem in lumine² naturali; cum in multis aliis speciebus animalium narrantur femelle sine commixtione³ seminis vel tactu maris concipere. Sed admiracio ineffabiliter nobis mirabilis est quod post limitacionem⁴ subitam corporis capti⁵ de pura virgine copulatus sit Deus⁶ pro instanti generacionis⁶ nature complete, et hoc ypostatica unione. Ideo⁷ hoc oportet nos ex fide supponere credendo quod nullum aliud tale novum potest fieri, licet hoc fuerit in natura creata analogice exemplatum de ypostatica unione⁷ mentis cum corpore. Et hinc tam sepe exemplificat Augustinus cum simbolo Athanasii quod ‘sicut anima racionalis et caro unus est homo, ita⁸ Deus et homo unus est⁸ Christus.’ Decuit enim auctorem nature specialiter post peccatum hominis remissibile per talem assumpcionem viri⁹ ex femina speciem hominis innovare: ut, sicut primo formatus est mas ex non-homine, secundo¹⁰ femina ex homine¹⁰ sed ex mare solummodo, tercio autem ex his sexubus commixtis sunt alii communiter procreati; sic quarto pro complemento iusticie¹¹ per se stantis in quatuor¹¹ compleat Deus virum celestem pure ex femina; cuius necessitas patet alibi. Nec sequitur ex illis superfluitas limitacionis racionis seminalis diversis mixtorum generibus, ut experiencia philosophorum testatur, quod multa genera animalium, in quibus est distinccio sexuum, tam ex putrefaccione quam ex semine procreantur. Et tamen, cum hoc dicunt philosophi quod nec sexuum distinccio, nec racionis seminalis limitacio est aliquo modo superflua; sic¹² latet in visceribus nature, quid Deus disposuit secundum potenciam obediencialem creature sue per se facere, et quid agens [p. 140] creatum a seculis multis absconditum sufficit preter communem cursum nature producere. Et circa¹ possibilitatem talium sollicitantur multi superflue: ut illi, quod demones² possunt in aspectu placido celi, mediantibus virtutibus mixtorum sicut herbarum et lapidum, mirabilia vulgo inopinabilia per se efficere: illi quod celum secundum diversas figuras et aspectus astrorum per se³ potest in talia: et hi quod homines possunt cum iuvamine demonum et constellacione celestium mirabilia inopinata producere. Et in speculacione talium theologizant speculativi inaniter. In practica vero mathematici consumunt tempus figuras celi superflue prestolantes, et demones periculosius consulentes. Sufficit ergo pio theologo quod sciat in genere talia mirabilia ab agente secundo posse fieri: 2° quod sciat istis agentibus secundis a Deo, a quo est omnis potencia, terminos secundum maximum et minimum limitari; nec⁴ creaturam aliquam posse in aliquid, nisi secundum eius ordinacionem et benevolenciam limitantem: et 3°. quod Deus ex condicione omnipotencie⁵ facit eo ipso quodlibet dandum opus quo vult ipsum a quoquam fieri, et istam condicionem potencie non potest communicari alteri.

And if it is said that God cannot, [of himself]{g:per-se} or with such disparate secondary agents, produce such effects after all,[^1] because it is neither authentic nor experienced, but [otherwise] the [seminal reasons]{g:raciones-seminales} would be limited superfluously:[^2] as to that, it seems to me that such contention about the [person]{g:persona} of God is quite superfluous, where we do not evidently know how to produce a reason for or against. Hence it is a light word of many, and quite without evidence: "I have not experienced this, or I have not found this written in histories authentic to me: therefore it is not true." And on the [139] other side, to be anxious to feign that a secondary agent can naturally [act] on such untried things exceeds the limits of the natural philosopher. But among the other works which we believe of God, the virgin's childbearing holds a place quite probable in the natural light, since in many other species of animals females are related to conceive without mixture of seed or touch of the male.[^3] Now the marvel, ineffably wonderful to us, is that after the sudden limiting of the body taken from the pure virgin, God was joined to the complete [nature]{g:natura} at the instant of generation,[^4] and this by hypostatic [union]{g:unio}. Therefore we must suppose this from faith, believing that no other such new thing can be made, although it has been analogically exemplified in created nature by the hypostatic union of mind with body.[^5] And hence Augustine, with the [creed]{g:symbolum} of Athanasius, so often gives the example that "as the reasonable soul and flesh is one man, so God and man is one Christ."[^6] For it was fitting that the author of nature, especially after man's sin, which could be remitted, should renew the species of man through such an [assumption]{g:assumpcio} of a [man]{g:vir} (*vir*) from a woman: so that, as first the male was formed from no man, second the female from man, but from the male only, and third the others have commonly been procreated from these sexes mingled; so fourth, for the completion of the justice that stands of itself in four, God should complete the heavenly man (*vir*) purely from a woman; the necessity of which is clear elsewhere.[^7] Nor does it [follow]{g:sequitur} from these that the limiting of the seminal reason to the diverse kinds of mixed bodies is superfluous, as the experience of the philosophers testifies that many kinds of animals in which there is distinction of the sexes are procreated both from putrefaction and from seed.[^8] And yet, although the philosophers say this, that neither the distinction of the sexes nor the limiting of the seminal reason is in any way superfluous, so it lies hidden in the bowels of nature what God has disposed to do of himself according to the obediential [potency]{g:potencia} of his [creature]{g:creatura},[^9] and what, hidden for many ages, a created [140] agent is sufficient to produce beyond the common course of nature.[^10] And about the possibility of such things many are superfluously anxious: as those [who hold] that demons can, under a favourable aspect of the heaven, by means of the [powers]{g:virtus} of mixed bodies such as herbs and stones, of themselves effect marvels unimaginable to the common people; those, that the heaven, according to the diverse figures and aspects of the stars, can of itself [act] on such things; and these, that men can, with the help of demons and the constellation of the heavenly bodies, produce unexpected marvels. And in speculation on such things the speculative theologize vainly. But in practice the [mathematicians]{g:mathematici} consume their time, superfluously waiting on the figures of the heaven, and more perilously consulting demons.[^11] It suffices, therefore, for the devout theologian that he know in general that such marvels can be done by a secondary agent; second, that he know that for these secondary agents terms are limited by God, from whom is all power,[^12] as to maximum and minimum; and that no creature can [act] on anything except according to his [ordinance]{g:ordinacio} and limiting benevolence; and third, that God, by the condition of omnipotence, does every work that is to be given by the very fact that he wills it to be done by anyone, and this condition of power cannot be communicated to another.[^13]

## ¶2 · 140.22–141.15

> [p. 140] Quantum ad testimonium Commentatoris patet quod dictum Averrois⁶ non debet esse alicui philosopho, et minus theologo, testis auctenticans, cum tam in philosophia quam fide presumptive⁷ vel invide sepius⁷ deviavit. Fautor autem Commentatoris, cui placeret⁸ dictum suum palliare, posset fingere ipsum de Avicenna concipere; quod posset esse homo, cuius proxima materia subiecta anime foret terra, et per consequens corpus alterius speciei quam mixtum organicum⁹ a sibi simili in specie procreatum; et cum forme dantur secundum disposicionem materie, si haberet¹⁰ animam, ipsa foret ab humanitate nostra disparis racionis. Nam in tam [p. 141] vilem materiam non inducit natura formam¹ simee vel pigmee:¹ vel 2°. quod natura plus ingenians² circa produccionem hominis quam animalis alterius imperfecti, quod corpus sicut et anima³ non posset produci naturaliter nisi a racionali supposito producatur. Ideo non sequitur—animal imperfectum potest ex putrefaccione produci: ergo homo— qui est finis uniuscuiusque alterius animalis, quia effectum preciosiorem virtus regitiva universitatis plus curat. Unde figuracionem embrionis oportet fieri a virtute informativa ab anima hominis per species triplices dirivata, per fomentum caloris consimilis in matrice, et per nutricionem concepti de sanguine menstruo parientis. Quod si alius sit modus producendi quoad corpus hoc est auctori nature specialiter reservatum. Sed totum illud vix moveret protervum ad credendum possibilitatem negative que petitur.

As to the testimony of the Commentator, it is clear that the saying of Averroes ought not to be an authenticating witness for any philosopher, and less for a theologian, since both in philosophy and in faith he has quite often strayed, presumptuously or enviously.[^14] And a supporter of the Commentator whom it pleased to [cloak]{g:palliare} his saying could feign that he was speaking of Avicenna['s view]: that there could be a man whose [proximate matter]{g:materia-proxima}, subject to the soul, would be earth, and consequently a body of another species than the organic mixed body procreated from one like itself in species; and since forms are given according to the disposition of matter, if it had a soul, that soul would be of a kind disparate from our [humanity]{g:humanitas}. For into such [141] vile matter nature does not induce the form of an ape or a pygmy: or second, [that he meant] that nature, taking more pains over the production of man than of another, imperfect animal, [brings it about] that the body, as well as the soul, could not be produced naturally unless it is produced by a rational [supposit]{g:suppositum}.[^15] Therefore it does not follow: "an imperfect animal can be produced from putrefaction: therefore a man [can]", [man] who is the end of every other animal; since the ruling power of the universe takes more care for the more precious effect. Hence the shaping of the embryo must be done by an informing power derived from the soul of the man through threefold species, by the fostering of like heat in the womb, and by the nourishment of what is conceived from the menstrual blood of the mother.[^16] And if there is another mode of producing as to the body, this is specially reserved to the author of nature. But all that would hardly move an [obstinate]{g:protervus} man to believe the possibility that is sought negatively.[^17]

## ¶3 · 141.16–142.9

> [p. 141] Tercii autem dicunt quod nemo post primum parentem potest produci nisi materialiter⁴ producatur ab homine propter amorem humani generis nutriendum. Nam racione stipitis et cognacionis oportet nutriri amorem inter homines ultra bruta, quia aliter fuisset facile post peccatum Adam totam suam generacionem sicut genus Caim⁵ exstinguere et aliud genus hominum ex disparato principio⁶ procreare⁶ sine hoc quod Christus paciatur pro salvanda specie. Et tunc esset homo species imperfecta, sine qua mundus posset existere; cum, nullo homine existente, celum posset ut vermes hominem⁷ de novo producere; †et multo magis elementum⁸ quod est⁹ gracia hominis tamquam finis non esset de substancia universali;¹⁰ et, sicut¹¹ situs spere¹² corruptibilium posset vacuus a corpore.†¹³ Sed cum iste tractatus multum¹⁴ distat a puncto proposito, redeundo dicitur quod philosophicum et facile est catholicum sustinere quod primus homo fiebat de terra, secundus sexus [p. 142] ab illo, et tercius ab ambobus; et sic gradatim de genere consequente quousque novus homo atque novissimus eiusdem speciei cum aliis fiebat ex femina; et quinto¹ omnis homo secundum animam fit ab illo circulariter a quo ultimate reficitur: quinarius autem et senarius sunt numeri circulares. Non enim credo quod species humana peribit, vel quod possit sub tanto auctore eciam quoad perfeccionem illam² deficere.

And the third say that no one after the first parent can be produced unless he is materially produced by a man, for the sake of nourishing the love of the human race. For by reason of stock and kinship love must be nourished among men beyond [what there is among] the brutes; since otherwise it would have been easy after Adam's sin to extinguish his whole generation, like the race of Cain, and to procreate another race of men from a disparate principle, without Christ's suffering for the saving of the species. And then man would be an imperfect species, without which the world could exist; since, with no man existing, the heaven could produce a man anew, as [it does] worms; †and much more, the element which is for the sake of man as its end would not be of the universal substance; and, as the place of the sphere of corruptible things could [be] empty of body.†[^18] But since this treatise stands far from the point proposed, returning: [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that it is philosophical, and easy for a catholic, to hold that the first man was made from earth, the second sex [142] from him, and the third from both; and so by degrees, of the kind that follows, until the new and newest man, of the same species with the others, was made from a woman; and fifth, every man as to the soul is made by him circularly, by whom he is ultimately remade: and five and six are circular numbers.[^19] For I do not believe that the human species will perish, or that it could, under so great an author, fail even as to that perfection.[^20]

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** "After all" renders *tamen*, with C O, against the print's *tum* "both" (138.25): agreement across the families, and the reading construes (*tamen* closes the objection, which denies the possibility just granted at 138.12–22); *tum* has no second *tum* to answer it. As printed: "both because it is neither authentic nor experienced, and …".

[^2]: **S.** *Raciones seminales*: Augustine's term for the causes God set in created things at the beginning, from which the kinds unfold in time (e.g. *De Genesi ad litteram* IX.17.32; LEDGER I08e-1, to be checked). The objection: if a man could be made otherwise than from seed, the seminal reasons would have been limited to sexual generation for nothing. *Limitate* (138.26), the print, with O; A B C read *linite* (as at 138.3–4).

[^3]: **S.** The schools' animal lore of females conceiving without the male (mares by the wind, Aristotle, *Historia animalium* VI.18; vultures, Ambrose, *Hexaemeron* V.20.64–65; LEDGER I08e-2, to be checked). A B C read *commixione* (139.6).

[^4]: **C.** *Nature complete* is taken as dative after *copulatus sit* "was joined to the complete nature"; it could be genitive with *generacionis*, "at the instant of the generation of the complete nature". A B read *Deus per generacionem*, C *Deus per generacionis* (139.9).

[^5]: **T.** *Ideo … unione* (139.10–13) is O's alone (A B C omit it); the print follows O, and the step "therefore … from faith" stands only there. Wyclif calls the union of mind with body itself "hypostatic"; the creed's likeness follows.

[^6]: **S.** The *Quicunque vult* (the "Athanasian Creed"), v. 37 (LEDGER I08e-3, to be checked); Augustine's use of the likeness, e.g. *In Iohannis evangelium* tr. 78.3 and *Epistula* 137.3.11, is to be checked under the same row. O omits *ita … est* (139.15).

[^7]: **T/S.** The four ways of making man (from no man, from a man alone, from man and woman, from a woman alone) are Anselm's, *Cur Deus homo* II.8 (LEDGER I08e-4, to be checked). *Iusticie per se stantis in quatuor* (139.22), the print, with O; A B C read *persone stantis in quarto*, "for the completion of the person standing in the fourth [place]". O reads *utri* for *viri* (139.18); O omits *secundo … homine*, and C reads *secundo ex femina ex homine* (139.19–20).

[^8]: **S.** Animals generated from putrefaction as well as from seed: Aristotle, *Historia animalium* V.1, 539a and *De generatione animalium* III.11 (LEDGER I08e-5, to be checked).

[^9]: **C.** *Potenciam obediencialem*: the potency sense of the CONTEXTUAL row: the creature's passive capacity to receive whatever God works in it beyond its nature. A B C read *sicut* for *sic* (139.30).

[^10]: **C.** *Absconditum* is taken with *quid* ("what, hidden for many ages"); it could go with *agens* ("a created agent hidden for many ages"). The phrase echoes Col 1:26, "the mystery which hath been hidden from ages and generations".

[^11]: **C.** *Mathematici*: the astrologers, who wait on the configurations of the heavens; not mathematicians in the modern sense.

[^12]: **C.** *Potencia* leans here to the other sense of the CONTEXTUAL row, "power" (God's), as in *condicionem potencie* (140.20). C reads *et sic*, O *ut*, for *nec* (140.16).

[^13]: **C.** *Eo ipso … quo vult*: correlatives, "by the very fact that he wills". *Quodlibet dandum opus*, "every work that is to be given", is construed as printed. God's omnipotence does every work immediately whenever a creature does it, and this cannot be shared.

[^14]: **S.** The Commentator's testimony is the one cited at 137 (Averroes against Avicenna on the generation of animals from earth; LEDGER I08d-4). *Presumptive vel invide sepius* (140.25), the print; O *p̃supue ve inde septus*.

[^15]: **C/S.** Two ways the supporter could cloak the saying, both hanging on *posset fingere*: that Averroes spoke of Avicenna's man from earth, which would be of another species (Avicenna, *De diluviis*; LEDGER I08e-6, to be checked); or that nature takes such pains over man that only a rational supposit can produce his body. The second has no main verb in the print; "[brings it about]" is supplied. "Forms are given according to the disposition of matter" is the Avicennian maxim (LEDGER I08e-7, to be checked).

[^16]: **C.** *Per species triplices*: construed as printed, "through threefold species"; what the three are is not said. *Parientis*: the woman giving birth.

[^17]: **C.** *Possibilitatem negative que petitur*: the possibility which the objection asks to be shown, here argued only by setting aside the reasons against it; the construal is uncertain.

[^18]: **T.** Harris daggers the passage *et multo magis … a corpore* (141.26–29) and notes *hic locus corruptus videtur* "this place seems corrupt" (141 n. 13). It is translated as printed. A B C omit *elementum* and read *universi* "of the universe" for *universali*; A C omit *est*; O omits *sicut*. A's *hominem* (141.26) is supplied in the margin by another hand.

[^19]: **S/C.** Five and six are "circular" because their powers end in the same figure (25, 36, 125, 216: Boethius, *De institutione arithmetica* II; LEDGER I08e-8, to be checked). The fifth way closes the circle: every soul is made by God, by whom it is in the end remade. A B C read *ergo* for *quinto* (142.3). *Catholicum* (141.31) is taken as the subject of *sustinere*, "for a catholic"; it could be a third predicate, "philosophical, easy [and] catholic".

[^20]: **T.** *Illam* (142.8), the print, with O, where Harris asks whether *ullam* "any" is meant; A B C read *secundam* "the second [perfection]".
