# *De ecclesia*, chapter 20, part 2: the office of the valiant woman: good to her husband, wool and flax, the merchant's ship, rising in the night (Prov. 31:11–15)

## ¶1 · 476.1–476.11

> [p. 476] Quarto infertur quod uxor tanti domini cuius sunt omnia et ipsa cum eo una persona (ut docetur Matthei XIX°, 5), erit sicut vitis habundans in lateribus domus sue, ideo subditur quod spoliis non indigebit. Non, inquit Augustinus, quia non querit spolia, cum diffusa per mundum eius trophea accipit tamquam sua, quia Sapiencie X°, 19 scribitur: Iusti tulerunt spolia impiorum. Et hinc nimirum dicit sponsa, Psalmo CXVIII°, 162: Letabor ego super eloquia tua, sicut qui invenit spolia multa. Et sic ista quadruplex proposicio declarat in parte predictum coniugium.

Fourthly it is inferred that the wife of so great a lord, whose are all things, she being one person with him (as is taught in Matthew 19:5), will be "as a fruitful vine, on the sides of [her] house"; therefore it is added that "he shall have no need of spoils".[^1] "Not," says Augustine, "because she does not seek spoils, since she receives as her own his trophies spread through the world"; for in Wisdom 10:19 it is written: "The just took the spoils of the wicked." And hence, no wonder, the [bride]{g:sponsa} says in Psalm 118:162: "I will rejoice at thy words, as one that hath found great spoil." And so this fourfold proposition declares in part the aforesaid marriage.[^2]

## ¶3 · 476.15–477.25

> [p. 476] a viro reddet ei bonum et non malum omnibus diebus vite sue. Operatur, inquit Augustinus, viro suo bona eterna continue, quia (ut mihi videtur) omnes predestinati semper prosunt ecclesie sine dampnacionis sue demerito vel ecclesie detrimento. In hoc enim differt peccatum predestinati a peccato presciti quod primum est necessario remissibile et secundum dampnabile. Primum per accidens prodest ecclesie et peccanti (ut patet de peccato Petri) et secundum licet per accidens faciat ad bonum ecclesie, tamen continue sibi nocet, quia sicut quicquid predestinatus hic fecerit tendit ad sui salvacionem, sic quicquid prescitus hic fecerit tendit ad sui dampnacionem. Quod autem quidam dicunt peccatum predestinati dampnabile, equivocatur in nomine dampnabile sumpto large vel proprie. Large dicitur dampnabile culpandum quod perpetuo dampnaretur, si non foret Christi gracia [p. 477] preservatum, et sic omnis peccator foret dampnabilis. Sed proprie solum illud dicitur dampnabile ad quod est Dei ordinacio, ut dampnetur, et sic nunquam hec mulier vel aliquod eius membrum fecit malum dampnabile sponso suo. Et (ut notat Augustinus) hec mulier fecit non sibi principaliter, sed sponso bonum quodcunque fecerit tamquam viro et capiti modo quo loquitur Apostolus IIª, Cor. V°, 15: Qui vivit iam non sibi vivat, sed ei qui mortuus est pro omnibus et resurrexit, sic quod ecclesia et quodlibet eius membrum quicquid operatum fuerit hoc facit, ut ei placeat cui se probavit. Omnes enim alii querunt que sua sunt, non que domini Jesu Christi. Et hec est racio (ut dixi superius), quare Christus non usus est proprietate civili in se nec in suis apostolis. Nec moveat quod nemo debet bonum reddere huic viro propter hoc quod omnis homo est servus sibi inutilis, cum fecerit omnia que rector iste precepit, ut dicitur Luce XVII°, 10; et Psalmo XV°, 2 dicitur: Dixi domino: Deus meus es tu, quoniam bonorum meorum non eges. Verum est quod Christus in persona propria non eget modo humanitus neque divinitus, sed nos egemus ut faciamus omnia nostra opera primo sibi, hoc est ad honorem suum et utilitatem sui corporis in quo eget.

by her husband, "will render him good, and not evil all the days of her life." "She works," says Augustine, "eternal goods for her husband continually"; for (as it seems to me) all the [predestinate]{g:predestinatus} always profit the [church]{g:ecclesia}, without demerit of their damnation or detriment to the church.[^4] For in this the sin of a predestinate [person] differs from the sin of a [foreknown]{g:prescitus} [person], that the first is necessarily remissible and the second damnable. The first by accident profits the church and the sinner (as is clear from the sin of Peter); and the second, although by accident it makes for the good of the church, yet continually harms the sinner himself; since, as whatever a predestinate [person] has done here tends to his [salvation]{g:salvacio}, so whatever a foreknown [person] has done here tends to his damnation.[^5] And as for what some say, that the sin of a predestinate [person] is damnable: there is equivocation in the name "damnable", taken broadly or properly. Broadly, that blameworthy [thing] is called damnable which would be damned perpetually were it not [477] preserved by the grace of Christ; and so every sinner would be damnable. But properly only that is called damnable for which there is an [ordinance]{g:ordinacio} of God that it be damned; and so this woman, or any [member]{g:membrum} of hers, never did evil damnable to her [bridegroom]{g:sponsus}.[^6] And (as Augustine notes) whatever good this woman has done, she did not principally for herself but for the bridegroom, as for her husband and [head]{g:caput}, in the way in which the Apostle speaks in 2 Corinthians 5:15: "He who lives, let him not now live to himself, but unto him who died for all and rose again"; so that the church and each member of it, whatever it has worked, does it so as to please him to whom it has engaged itself. For all others seek the things that are their own, not the things that are the Lord Jesus Christ's.[^7] And this is the reason (as I said above) why Christ did not use civil property, in himself or in his apostles. Nor let it trouble [anyone] that no one is to render good to this husband, on the ground that every man is an unprofitable servant to him when he has done all that this ruler commanded, as is said in Luke 17:10; and in Psalm 15:2 it is said: "I have said to the Lord, thou art my God, for thou hast no need of my goods." It is true that Christ in his own person has no need now, either humanly or divinely; but we have need to do all our works first for him, that is, to his honour and to the profit of his body, in which he has need.[^8]

## ¶4 · 477.26–478.18

> [p. 477] Habita solicitudine generali huius matrisfamilias, specificat secundo opus suum, cum subditur: Quesivit lanam et linum et operata est consilio manuum suarum. Secundum Augustinum lana est habitus collectus ex campo scripture ad regulandum vitam laudabilem corporalem et linum est habitus ad conversacionem spiritualem ducendam. Sic enim in ordine vestimentorum nostrorum corporalium interiora sunt linea, sed vestimenta exteriora sunt lanea. Correspondenter in vestibus anime que secundum fidem scripture sunt virtutes: [p. 478] quidquid carne operamur, est a dextra sensibile: quidquid spiritu, in secreto. Oportet ergo hanc feminam cum sua familia indui hoc geminum vestimentum. Operari autem carne et non spiritu quamvis bonum videatur, utile tamen non est. Operari vero spiritu et non carne pigrorum est; nam dans elemosinam pro laude hominum nudam vestem videtur indui, sicut dicens sibi sufficere in consciencia sua Deum colere, nec quod debet ad ecclesiam ire vel ceteris christianis actibus sensibilibus immisceri. Talis, inquit Augustinus, nudam vestem lineam vult habere. Non sic docet hec mulier, sed instituit simul secundum praxim et theoricam operari, nam ad hoc colligere debemus hos habitus, ut consilio manuum operemur. Virtus enim secundum philosophos est propter opera, et hinc felicitas consistit in actu vel opere, et melior est virtute. Omnem autem virtutem moralem oportet prudenciam regulari, et ideo signanter dicitur quod hec mater operata est consilio manuum suarum.

The general care of this mother of the household having been treated, [the song] secondly specifies her work, when it is added: "She hath sought wool and flax, and hath wrought by the counsel of her hands." According to Augustine, the wool is the habit gathered from the field of scripture for ruling a praiseworthy bodily life, and the flax is the habit for leading a spiritual [manner of life]{g:conversacio}. For so, in the order of our bodily garments, the inner ones are linen, but the outer garments are woollen. Correspondingly in the garments of the soul, which according to the faith of scripture are the [virtues]{g:virtus}: [478] "whatever we work in the flesh is on the right hand, [perceptible]{g:sensibilis}; whatever in the spirit, in secret."[^9] This woman, then, with her [household]{g:familia}, must be clothed with this twofold garment. "And to work in the flesh and not in the spirit, though it seems good, yet is not profitable. But to work in the spirit and not in the flesh is [the part] of the slothful; for one who gives [alms]{g:elemosina} for the praise of men seems to be clothed with a bare garment, as [is] one who says that it suffices him to worship God in his conscience, and not that he ought to go to church or to take part in the other perceptible christian acts." "Such a one," says Augustine, "wants to have a bare linen garment."[^10] Not so does this woman teach; but she sets [us] to work at once according to practice and theory; for we ought to gather these habits to this end, that we may work by the counsel of [our] hands. For virtue, according to the philosophers, is for the sake of works, and hence happiness consists in act or work, and is better than virtue. Now every moral virtue must be ruled [by] prudence; and therefore it is said expressly that this mother "hath wrought by the counsel of her hands",[^11]

## ¶5 · 478.19–480.15

> [p. 478] Unde ad denotandum varietatem operacionum huius matrone subiungit tercio: Facta est quasi navis institoris de longe portans panem suum. Notum est ex tractantibus de archa Noe et navicula Petri quomodo ecclesia similatur navi in qua divicie et diversa mercimonia congeruntur habetque intra se mercantes tam venditores quam eciam institores, et cum per totam habitabilem nostram saltem potestative diffunditur, ymmo per purgatorium atque celos, patet quod de longe portat panem quo pascitur. Navis autem nedum in figura tendit ad similitudinem matrone sed ligna levigata ad imitacionem ligni crucis composita evehunt navigantem in seculo, ne procellis undarum [p. 479] et aliis maris periculis submergatur. Mercimonia et divicie huius navis sunt omnia genera virtutum et merita quibus bona patrie comparantur; sic pascitur anima viatrix per effluxum longinquum a triumphante ecclesia, sed anima purganda recipit panem ab ecclesia militante. Divicie, inquit Augustinus, in quibus pascitur et mercatur ecclesia sunt largiflue laudes sponsi, de quibus Psalmo CXII° dicitur: A solis ortu usque ad occasum laudabile nomen domini. Non enim pascitur nec solicitatur membrum ecclesie nisi in laude domini. Sed quia nec navis dicta nec sui incole debent ab operibus ociari, ideo quarto subiungitur huius matrone provida prudencia operando. De nocte, inquit, surrexit deditque predam domesticis suis et cibaria ancillis suis. Nox secundum Augustinum est tempus quo tortores dyaboli persequuntur christicolas, in qua involuti peccatorum tenebris videntur nocere fidelibus, sed eis surgentibus ad vitam plus prosperam nocent realiter sibi ipsis. Unde quia in talibus tribulacionibus claruit et profecit ecclesia, sicut granum trituracione separatum a palea, ideo signanter dicit sapiens quod de nocte surrexit. Aporiamur, inquit apostolus, et non defecimus, IIª Corinth. IV°, 8 et Ysaie XXVI°, 9: Anima mea desideravit te in nocte, et Job XXXVI°, 20: Ne protrahas noctem ut ascendant populi. Dedit autem predam domesticis suis, quando viris evangelicis domesticis fidei dedit ex vi communionis sanctorum populum conversum ad Christum, quem dyabolus ante captivavit, et dedit cibaria ancillis suis, quando christianis inferioribus esurientibus et sicientibus iusticiam dedit pastum legis domini et potum in complemento iusticie delectandi. Et sic de matre nostra verificata est doctrina matrone Anne, Tobie X°, [p. 480] ubi docta est a parentibus honorare soceros, diligere maritum, regere familiam, gubernare domum et se ipsam irreprehensibilem exhibere. Nec moveat quod filii ecclesie sunt ancille, cum vir tocius ecclesie sit eius dominus; quod figuratum est in Sara Genesis XVIII°, 12, vocante Abraham dominum suum (ut notat beatus Petrus Iª Petri III°, 6). Non timeat, inquit Augustinus, condicionem, sed speret miseracionem, quia revera si Deo servire sit regnare, ancilla domini est regina. Et hinc vere ac meritorie profitetur Psalmista Psalmo CXV°, 16: Ego, inquit, servus tuus et filius ancille tue. Oportet enim membra ecclesie precipue cavere ocium et servire continue, quia aliter non datur eis spirituale cibarium. Unde IIª Thessal. III°, 10 dicit Apostolus: Qui non laborat non manducet.

Hence, to denote the variety of the operations of this matron, he adds thirdly: "She is like the merchant's ship, she bringeth her bread from afar." It is known from those who treat of the ark of Noe and the little ship of Peter how the church is likened to a ship, in which riches and diverse merchandise are gathered, and it has within itself traders, sellers as well as merchants; and since it is spread through the whole of our habitable [world], at least potentially, indeed through purgatory and the heavens, it is clear that it carries from afar the bread by which it is fed.[^12] And the ship not only in figure tends to the likeness of the matron, but its smoothed timbers, put together in imitation of the wood of the cross, bear up the voyager in the world, lest he be sunk by the storms of the waves [479] and the other perils of the sea. The merchandise and riches of this ship are all kinds of virtues, and the merits by which the goods of the [homeland]{g:patria} are purchased; so the [wayfaring]{g:viator} soul is fed by a far-off outflow from the [church triumphant]{g:ecclesia-triumphans}, but the soul to be purged receives bread from the [church militant]{g:ecclesia-militans}.[^13] "The riches," says Augustine, "in which the church is fed and trades are the overflowing praises of the bridegroom, of which it is said in Psalm 112: 'From the rising of the sun unto the going down of the same, the name of the Lord is worthy of praise.'" For a member of the church is not fed, nor busied, except in the praise of the Lord.[^14] But since neither the said ship nor its inhabitants ought to be idle from works, therefore fourthly the provident prudence of this matron in working is added: "She hath risen," he says, "in the night, and given a prey to her household, and victuals to her maidens." The night, according to Augustine, is the time in which the devil's torturers persecute the [worshippers of Christ]{g:christicole}, in which, wrapped in the darkness of sins, they seem to harm the faithful; but as these rise to a more prosperous life, they really harm themselves.[^15] Hence, since in such tribulations the church has shone and profited, like grain separated from the chaff by threshing, the wise man therefore says expressly that "she hath risen in the night". "We are straitened," says the apostle, "and have not failed" (2 Corinthians 4:8); and Isaiah 26:9: "My soul hath desired thee in the night"; and Job 36:20: "Prolong not the night that people may come up."[^16] And she gave a prey to her household when to evangelical men, those of the household of the faith, she gave, by force of the communion of saints, the people converted to Christ, whom the devil had before taken captive; and she gave victuals to her maidens when to lower christians, hungering and thirsting after justice, she gave the food of the Lord's law and the drink of delight in the fulfilment of justice.[^17] And so the [doctrine]{g:doctrina} of the matron Anna, Tobias 10, is verified of [our mother]{g:mater-nostra}, [480] where [the daughter] was taught by her parents "to honour her father and mother in law, to love her husband, to take care of the family, to govern the house, and to behave herself irreprehensibly."[^18] Nor let it trouble [anyone] that the sons of the church are handmaids, since the husband of the whole church is its lord; which is figured in Sara, Genesis 18:12, calling Abraham "her lord" (as blessed Peter notes, 1 Peter 3:6). "Let her not fear," says Augustine, "her condition", but let her hope for mercy; for truly, if to serve God is to reign, the handmaid of the Lord is a queen. And hence the Psalmist truly and meritoriously professes in Psalm 115:16: "I," he says, "am thy servant, and the son of thy handmaid." For the members of the church must especially beware of idleness and serve continually, since otherwise spiritual victuals are not given them. Hence the Apostle says in 2 Thessalonians 3:10: "He who does not labour, let him not eat."[^19]

## Notes

[^2]: **S.** Augustine, *Sermo* 37 on Proverbs 31 (LEDGER E20b-1). The codices read *quia querit*; Loserth corrected to *quia non querit* from Augustine, whose text runs *Non quia non querit spolia, ideo non indigebit, sed quia multis abundabit* (476.5). Wisdom 10:19 ("Therefore the just took the spoils of the wicked"); Psalm 118:162. **C.** *Eius trophea*: her husband's trophies. *Quadruplex proposicio*: the four points drawn from verses 10–11 (the finding, the price, the trust, the spoils).

[^3]: **C.** The editor's paragraph ends inside the sentence; it continues in the next paragraph. *Tam fertiliter dotata a viro*: so richly dowered by her husband. **R.** A in the margin at 476.12: *Secunda pars*. Side-notes 476.12, 476.14.

[^4]: **S.** Proverbs 31:12. Augustine, the same sermon, whose text has *in omni tempore* for *continue* (476.17) (LEDGER E20b-1). **C.** *Sine dampnacionis sue demerito*: without any demerit that would bring their damnation. **T.** AA₁ lack *a* (476.15).

[^5]: **C.** *Per accidens*: incidentally, not of its own nature. *De peccato Petri*: Peter's denial, which turned to his and the church's good. *Sibi nocet*: *sibi* is the foreknown sinner. **T.** B reads *Peccatum per accidens* (476.22) and lacks *et secundum … tamen* (476.23). **R.** A in the margin at 476.20: *Differencie inter peccatum presciti et predestinati*.

[^6]: **C.** *Equivocatur in nomine dampnabile*: the word is used equivocally. *Culpandum*: the blameworthy act. *Ad quod est Dei ordinacio, ut dampnetur*: what God has ordained to damnation. *Fecit malum dampnabile sponso suo* answers to the verse, "will render him good, and not evil". **T.** AA₁ lack *equivocatur … dampnabile* (476.29).

[^7]: **S.** Augustine on the verse (LEDGER E20b-1). 2 Corinthians 5:15 ("that they also who live may not now live to themselves, but unto him who died for them and rose again"): Wyclif's wording differs. *Ut ei placeat cui se probavit*: 2 Timothy 2:4 ("that he may please him to whom he hath engaged himself"). Philippians 2:21 ("For all seek the things that are their own not the things that are Jesus Christ's"): Wyclif adds *alii* and *domini*.

[^8]: **C.** *Ut dixi superius*: cf. 303 (LEDGER E20b-2). *Proprietate civili*: civil property, the ownership of human law. *Nemo debet bonum reddere*: the objection is that no one can owe or render good to Christ. *Modo*: now. **S.** Luke 17:10 ("We are unprofitable servants"); Psalm 15:2.

[^9]: **S.** Proverbs 31:13: Wyclif's *consilio* for the Vulgate's *consilia*. Augustine, the same sermon, worked over with some words changed (Loserth, 478.4; his text *Quidquid carne operamur, in promptu est: quidquid spiritu, in secreto*) (LEDGER E20b-3). **C.** *A dextra sensibile*: "on the right hand, perceptible", the open work set against the secret. **T.** B lacks *carne* and reads *ad extra* for *a dextra* (478.1), "is outward, perceptible", closer to Augustine's *in promptu*; translated as printed.

[^10]: **C.** The words from *Operari autem* to *immisceri* are printed as Augustine's, as is *Talis … habere*. *Nudam vestem*: one garment without the other; the almsgiver for praise has the outer without the inner, the one content with conscience the inner without the outer. *Pigrorum est*: the mark of the slothful. **R.** Side-note 478.4.

[^11]: **S.** Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*: virtue for the sake of activity, happiness an activity and better than virtue, and the moral virtues ruled by prudence (I.8, X.6–7, VI.13; LEDGER E20b-4). **C.** *Secundum praxim et theoricam*: in practice and theory together. *Prudenciam regulari*: read as *prudencia*, "be ruled by prudence"; the accusative as printed would make prudence the subject of a passive. **T.** B reads *moralem et potenciam oportet enim et ideo* (478.16–17).

[^12]: **S.** Proverbs 31:14. **C.** *Tractantibus de archa Noe et navicula Petri*: the expositors of the ark and of Peter's boat as figures of the church. *Institores* is "merchants", as in the verse; *mercantes* "traders". *Potestative*: potentially, in reach of its power. **R.** Side-note 478.19.

[^13]: **C.** *Ligna levigata*: the planed timbers of the ship. *Evehunt*: bear up, carry over. *Per effluxum longinquum a triumphante ecclesia*: the wayfarer is fed from the far-off church triumphant; the soul in purgatory receives from the church militant (its prayers and suffrages). *Comparantur*: are bought.

[^14]: **S.** Augustine, with the words changed (Loserth, 479.6) (LEDGER E20b-5). Psalm 112:3; the codices read *CXXII*, corrected by Loserth (479.8). **C.** *Solicitatur*: is busied.

[^15]: **S.** Proverbs 31:15. Augustine, Sermo 37, where the nights are tribulations; the devil's torturers are Wyclif's (LEDGER E20b-6). **C.** *Tortores dyaboli*: the devil's torturers, the persecutors. *Eis surgentibus*: as the faithful rise. **R.** Side-note 479.11.

[^16]: **S.** 2 Corinthians 4:8: the Vulgate has *aporiamur, sed non destituimur* ("We are straitened: but are not destitute") (Loserth, 479.22–23); Wyclif's *et non defecimus* is translated. Isaiah 26:9. Job 36:20 (Wyclif without *pro eis*, "for them"). **C.** *Sicut granum trituracione separatum a palea*: cf. Matthew 3:12.

[^17]: **S.** *Domesticis fidei*: Galatians 6:10 ("those who are of the household of the faith"). *Esurientibus et sicientibus iusticiam*: Matthew 5:6 ("they that hunger and thirst after justice"). **C.** *Predam*: the prey is the people taken back from the devil. *Christianis inferioribus*: christians of lower rank, the maidens of the verse. *Potum in complemento iusticie delectandi*: the drink of delighting in justice fulfilled.

[^18]: **S.** Tobias 10:13 ("Admonishing her to honour her father and mother in law, to love her husband, to take care of the family, to govern the house, and to behave herself irreprehensibly").
