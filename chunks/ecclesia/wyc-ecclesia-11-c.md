# *De ecclesia*, chapter 11, part 3: correction as medicine; what immunity is; the first objection answered

## ¶1 · 238.1–238.27

> [p. 238] Confirmatur primo ex hoc quod Christus medicus sapientissimus et clementissimus ita facit cum suis discipulis quos plus amat (ut declarat beatus Gregorius XXI° Moralium, et recitatum est proximo capitulo). Unde Apoc. III°, 19, dicit is medicus: Ego quos amo arguo et castigo, et Hebr. XII°, 16, 19, dicitur: Flagellat autem Deus omnem filium quem recipit. Secundo confirmatur ex testimonio Augustini posito in decretis XXIIIª q. IV. Ipsa pietas, ubi sic scribitur: Molestus est medicus furenti frenetico et pater indisciplinato filio: ille ligando iste cedendo, sed ambo diligendo. Si autem illos negligant et perire permittant, ista falsa mansuetudo foret crudelitas, Vª, q. IVª. Non vos, et Non omnis. Et ponit post exemplum quomodo existentibus pluribus amicis in domo certissime ruitura, si nollent credere narracioni periculi, flagellaremus vel invite extraheremus aliquem, licet credamus quod aliquis se stulte precipitet. Multo magis in maiori periculo spirituali. Unde XXIII°, q. IVª. Quid faciet, dicitur quod ad hoc datur ecclesie medicina, ut inter freneticos atque litargicos eciam contempnentes non debet desistere sic quod perseveret diligencia caritatis, ligando freneticum et litargicum stimulando. Quotlibet tales sunt leges que idem sentenciant. Si ergo tantum sit peccatum in omittentibus habentibus potestatem, manifestius et inexcusabilius est peccatum in speculatore qui ommittendo proderit veritatem.

This is confirmed, first, from the fact that Christ, the wisest and most merciful physician, does so with his disciples, whom he loves more (as blessed Gregory declares in the twenty-first [book] of the *Moralia*, and as was recited in the preceding chapter). Hence in Apocalypse 3:19 this physician says: "Such as I love, I rebuke and chastise"; and in Hebrews 12:6 it is said: "And God scourgeth every son whom he receiveth."[^1] Second, it is confirmed from the testimony of Augustine set down in the Decretum, C. 23 q. 4, *Ipsa pietas*, where it is written thus: "The physician is troublesome to the raving frenzied man, and the father to the undisciplined son: the one by binding, the other by beating, but both by loving." But if they should neglect them and let them perish, this false gentleness would be cruelty: C. 5 q. 5, *Non vos* and *Non omnis*.[^2] And afterwards he gives an example: how, if several friends were in a house most certainly about to fall, and they would not believe the report of the danger, we would beat someone or drag him out against his will, even though we believe that someone may foolishly throw himself down. Much more in a greater, spiritual danger.[^3] Hence in C. 23 q. 4, *Quid faciet*, it is said that medicine is given to the [church]{g:ecclesia} for this: that among the frenzied and the lethargic, even those who scorn [it], it ought not to desist, so that the diligence of [charity]{g:caritas} may persevere, binding the frenzied and goading the lethargic. Any number of such laws [pronounce]{g:sentenciare} the same. If, then, the sin is so great in those having power who omit [this], more manifest and more inexcusable is the sin in the [watchman]{g:speculator} who by omitting betrays the truth.[^4]

## ¶2 · 238.28–239.31

> [p. 238] Cum ergo (ut tactum est superius) non stat duo contraria esse simul privilegia eidem, et secundum idem patet cum casu limitato, foret privilegium fugitivo [p. 239] ligari et pungi, non foret tunc idem privilegium in facultatibus et pena relaxari, quia revera omnis taliter cupidus fastus et questus est furens freneticus et soporatus litargicus ex fumo temporalium inficiencium cerebrum racionis. Et patet per modum epilogi quomodo oportet privilegium sic interpretari quod fugitivi ad locum sic privilegiatum pro quocunque commisso habeant libertatem vel immunitatem omnimodam secundum regulas caritatis et misericordie a rege et regno cum paribus eis dandam, sic quod omne dandum sonet tam eis quam dantibus bonum utile. De diversitate vero inter crimen, facinus atque flagicium dictum est XXV° cap., libri VIⁱ quomodo facinus sonat in proximi iniuriam; de quiditate autem privilegii dictum est superius, VIII° cap. huius libri; et cum oportet ut intelligatur libertas a peccato (ut loquitur Apostolus Rom. VI°, 22); patet quod immunitas pertinenter est indignitas criminis ad tormentum (ut loquitur scriptura Jeremie XXV° et Ecclesiasticus VII° cap.). Communiter autem intelligitur de mundicia a facinore quod in Anglia dicitur felonia, et est quedam species libertatis quam quidam vocant franchisiam et sumitur nunc extensius et nunc striccius. Unde immunis dicitur ab in et munium tributum, quasi liber a reddicione tributi, ut est expers vel alienus a reatu et vere loquendo solum insons, dum non est dignus sic puniri. Dicitur inmunis sic quod immunitas opponatur reatui, ad quem sensum ex nullo humano privilegio sequitur in criminoso immunitas aput Deum, sed ex privilegiacione sequitur irreatus pene quoad principem libertantem.

Since, then (as was touched on above), it cannot be that two contraries are at once privileges to the same [person] and in the same respect, it is clear that, since in the case specified it would be a [privilege]{g:privilegium} to the fugitive [239] to be bound and goaded, it would not then likewise be a privilege [to him] to be released in his goods and his penalty; because truly everyone so greedy of pomp and gain is a raving frenzied man and a drowsy lethargic, from the fumes of [temporal goods]{g:temporalia} infecting the brain of reason.[^5] And it is clear, by way of epilogue, how the privilege must be so interpreted that fugitives to the place thus privileged, for whatever offence, have liberty or immunity of every kind according to the rules of charity and mercy, to be given them by the king and kingdom together with the peers, so that everything to be given tends to a useful good both for them and for the givers.[^6] As for the difference between crime, misdeed and outrage, it has been said in chapter 25 of book 6 how a misdeed tends to the injury of one's neighbour; and of the [quiddity]{g:quiditas} of privilege it was said above, in chapter 8 of this book; and since [by liberty] freedom from sin must be understood (as the Apostle speaks, Romans 6:22), it is clear that immunity, properly speaking, is the crime's not deserving torment (as scripture speaks in Jeremiah 25 and Ecclesiasticus 7).[^7] But commonly it is understood of cleanness from a misdeed, which in England is called felony; and it is a certain species of liberty which some call franchise, and it is taken now more widely and now more narrowly. Hence *immunis* is said from *in* and *munium*, "tribute", as though free from the rendering of tribute; so [one is immune] as he is without part in, or a stranger to, guilt, and, truly speaking, only the guiltless, so long as he does not deserve to be so punished. One is called immune in such a way that immunity is opposed to guilt; in which sense no human privilege yields immunity before God in a criminal; but from [privileging]{g:privilegiacio} there follows freedom from liability to penalty as regards the prince who grants the liberty.[^8]

## ¶3 · 239.32–240.22

> [p. 239] Contra dicta obicitur primo per hoc quod, si quilibet criminosus sit indignus habere suffragium ecclesie, tunc [p. 240] lex illud exprimeret, sed facit oppositum cum servum eciam existentem in gracia docet extrahere et alios criminosos docet libertate gaudere. Hic dicitur quod plus peccans est de racione plus elongatus a suffragio ecclesie, licet ecclesia ex ignorancia gravedinis peccati vel ex percepcione planiori iniurie vel alia causa culpabili hoc dispariter prosequatur. Nam si secundum iura humana et ecclesiastica licitum racionabile atque legitimum est extrahere servum atque libertum de ecclesia pro debito operis impendendo domino temporali (ut patet capitulo De hiis qui ad ecclesiam fugiunt, le. fi.), si omnis indebitatus proximo in pecunia vel quocunque debet servicium satisfaccionis sub pena dampnacionis domino dominorum, ergo multo magis licet eum extrahere de ecclesia, si exhinc pauset inobediens ad reddendum servicium regi regum. Minor patet ex hoc quod omnis homo est magis servus Deo, plus debitor et prius quam aliquis servus est debitor domino temporali. Unde Apostolus Ephes. VI°, 5°, docet servos obedire dominis carnalibus cum timore et tremore, sicut Christo, servientes sicut domino non hominibus.

Against what has been said [it is objected, by appeal to]{g:obicitur per} this, first: that if every criminal is unworthy to have the [aid]{g:suffragium} of the church, then [240] the law would express it; but it does the opposite, since it teaches that even a servant who is in grace be dragged out, and teaches that other criminals enjoy liberty.[^9] To this [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that one who sins more is, in principle, further removed from the aid of the church, although the church, from ignorance of the gravity of the sin, or from a plainer perception of the injury, or from some other culpable cause, pursues this unequally.[^10] For if, according to human and ecclesiastical laws, it is lawful, reasonable and legitimate to drag a servant or a freedman out of a church for the debt of work to be rendered to a temporal lord (as is clear from the chapter *De hiis qui ad ecclesiam fugiunt*, the last law), [and] if everyone indebted to his neighbour in money or anything else owes the [service]{g:servicium} of satisfaction, under pain of damnation, to the lord of lords, then much more is it lawful to drag him out of a church if from there he rests disobedient to rendering service to the king of kings.[^11] The minor is clear from this, that every man is more a servant to God, more a debtor and earlier [a debtor], than any servant is a debtor to a temporal lord. Hence the Apostle, Ephesians 6:5[–7], teaches servants to obey their carnal lords with fear and trembling, as to Christ, serving as to the Lord and not to men.[^12]

## ¶4 · 240.23–242.3

> [p. 240] Confirmatur primo per hoc quod, sicut homo tenetur plus Deum diligere quam se ipsum, sic tenetur plus odire Dei iniuriam, unde XXIIIª, q. IV sic scribitur: Si ea de quibus vehementer Deus offenditur, insequi vel ulcisci deferimus, ad irascendum utique divinitatis pacienciam provocamus. Secundo confirmatur ex hoc quod secundum leges humanas lesis et non [p. 241] ledentibus debent privilegia ecclesie suffragari (ut patet collacione tercia) sed omnis profugus, iniuste detinens alienum debitum pretextu profugii ad quamcunque ecclesiam iniuriatur Deo et omnibus sanctis leditque se ipsum et quemlibet proximum mortalis peccati comparticipem. Ergo non est iustum ut sic ledens gaudeat taliter immunitate ecclesie. Et ad idem vadunt quotlibet iuris principia, ut III° Decretalium, De rerum permutacione, cap°. Cum universorum: Deceptis et non decipientibus iura subveniunt; aliter enim ordinarentur leges ad fovendum iniurias. Sed Isaie X°, 1, dicitur: Ve qui condunt leges iniquas; non enim est lex humana vel divina nisi ad continuandum iusticias et expurgandum iniurias. Tercio confirmatur ex hoc quod homo est obligacior ad vindicandum iniuriam Dei quam propriam, cum suam debet remittere, nisi de quanto sapit divinam iniuriam, sicut patet de Christo, de Moyse et ceteris Christi membris, que omnia debent exponere se pro capite; sed omne ad cuius complecionem est obligacior debet magis attendere: ergo debet magis attendere vindicacionem divine iniurie; unde omnes facientes oppositum sunt culpabiliter se ipsos amantes (ut loquitur Apostolus, II°, Thymothei Et istam sentenciam docet Augustinus, libro primo ad Cresconium grammaticum ut recitatur in decretis, XXXIIIª, q. IVᵗᵃ): Quisquis christianus iniquum non persequitur, Christi est inimicus. Verum dicis, si non hoc in illo persequitur quod Christo est inimicum. Neque enim dominus in servo, pater in filio, maritus [p. 242] in coniuge, cum sint utrique christiani, non debent persequi nisi vicia christiane contraria veritati; que si non persequuntur, rei negligencie merito tenebuntur.

This is confirmed, first, by this: that as a man is bound to love God more than himself, so he is bound to hate more an injury to God. Hence in C. 23 q. 4 it is written thus: "If we put off pursuing or avenging the things by which God is vehemently offended, we surely provoke the patience of the [divinity]{g:divinitas} to anger."[^13] Second, it is confirmed from this: that according to human laws the privileges of the church ought [to aid]{g:suffragari} the injured and not [241] those who injure (as is clear from the third collation); now every refugee who, on the pretext of refuge at any church, unjustly withholds a debt owed to another, injures God and all the saints, and harms himself and every neighbour who shares in [his] mortal sin. Therefore it is not just that one who so injures should thus enjoy the immunity of the church.[^14] And any number of principles of [law]{g:ius} go to the same, as in the third [book] of the Decretals, *De rerum permutacione*, chapter *Cum universorum*: "The laws come to the aid of the deceived and not of the deceivers"; for otherwise laws would be ordained to foster injuries. But in Isaiah 10:1 it is said: "Woe to them that make wicked laws"; for there is no law, human or divine, except to maintain justice and purge away injuries.[^15] Third, it is confirmed from this: that a man is more obliged to avenge an injury to God than his own, since he ought to remit his own except in so far as it savours of an injury to God, as is clear in Christ, in Moses and in the other [members]{g:membrum} of Christ, all of whom ought to expose themselves for the [head]{g:caput}; now he ought to attend more to everything to whose fulfilment he is more obliged; therefore he ought to attend more to the avenging of an injury to God. Hence all who do the opposite are culpably lovers of themselves (as the Apostle speaks, 2 Timothy [3:2]).[^16] And Augustine teaches this [teaching]{g:sentencia} in the first book *To Cresconius the Grammarian*, as it is recited in the Decretum, C. 23 q. 4: "Whoever, being a Christian, does not pursue the wicked is an enemy of Christ. You say truly, if he does not pursue in him what is hostile to Christ. For neither the master in the servant, nor the father in the son, nor the husband in [242] the wife, since both are Christians, ought to pursue anything but the vices contrary to Christian truth; and if they do not pursue these, they will rightly be held guilty of negligence."[^17]

## ¶5 · 242.5–242.18

> [p. 242] Et ista sentencia rectificaret multum ecclesiam, quia desidia et deformitas correpcionis perturbant ecclesiam, cum multi non correptores, sed corruptores zelo vindicte amant peccatum et odiunt personam, querunt pecuniam, non correpti iusticiam, et ideo correpciones propter ordinem preposterum non habent efficaciam. Conceditur ergo assumptum in obiecto, cum oportet Deum dignificare fugitivum per graciam, et post redditur dignior ad habendum matris sue suffragium; nec possunt aliqua iura exprimere huius oppositum. Racionabile autem est servum fugientem a servicio debito domino suo extrahere, cum ut sic delinquit in Deum; quod autem homines illud maioribus peccatis preponderant, accusant proprietarie se amantes.

And this [teaching]{g:sentencia} would set the [church]{g:ecclesia} greatly to rights; because sloth in correction, and its deformity, disturb the church, since many, not correctors but corrupters, out of zeal for vengeance love the sin and hate the person, seek money, not the justice of the one corrected; and therefore corrections, because of their inverted order, have no efficacy.[^18] The [assumption]{g:assumptum} in the objection is therefore granted, since God must make the fugitive worthy through grace, and afterwards he is rendered more worthy to have the [aid]{g:suffragium} of his mother; nor can any laws express the opposite of this. And it is reasonable to drag out a servant fleeing from the [service]{g:servicium} owed to his lord, since as such he offends against God; but in that men weigh this more heavily than greater sins, they accuse [themselves] as men who love themselves as owners.[^19]

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 238.1: Christ chastises those whom he loves.
- 239.6: To sum up: The fugitives should have all liberty that is good tor themselves and for others.
- 240.1: Objections: 1. It is said that the law would express these limitations, if they were intended.
- 240.12: Answer: It does in a way express them.
- 241.1: The Church should help the injured, not those who do wrong.
- 241.15: A man is more bound to revenge an injury to God than to himself.
- 241.25: Augustine teaches the duty of punishing the wicked.
- 242.5: The performance of this duty would do much to set the Church right.

## Notes

[^1]: **S.** A in the margin *Confirmacio triplex* (238.1); side-note 238.1. Gregory, *Moralia* XXI: the place is to be checked; Loserth's "Cap. IV" (238.4) does not say whether it refers to the *Moralia* or to this treatise (LEDGER E11c-1). Apocalypse 3:19, as the Vulgate; DR. Hebrews 12:6: Wyclif adds *Deus* to the Vulgate's *flagellat autem omnem filium*; DR "and he scourgeth" with "God" added.

[^2]: **S/T.** C. 23 q. 4 c. 24, *Ipsa pietas* (Loserth, 238.9; Augustine to Boniface; Friedberg I, 909–10; LEDGER E11c-2). A in the margin *Augustinus* (238.8). A and A₁ read *nec ligant* (238.12). *Vª, q. IVª*: so the print, following the manuscripts (*V q. vel IV*, 238.13); the canons are C. 5 q. 5 cc. 1–2 (Friedberg I, 549), as Loserth's own reference has it, and the English gives that (LEDGER E11c-3). *Furenti frenetico*: the raving madman; *freneticus* and *litargicus* are the two diseases of Augustine's figure (the frenzied, the lethargic).

[^3]: **C.** *Ponit*: Augustine, in the same text (Loserth, 238.14). *Licet credamus quod aliquis se stulte precipitet*: "even though we believe someone may foolishly throw himself down", i.e. even at that risk; Augustine has no *flagellaremus*: the beating is Wyclif's addition (c. 24 §§4–5) (LEDGER E11c-2). B in the margin, second hand, *Correccio est medicina* (238.23).

[^4]: **S/T.** C. 23 q. 4 c. 25, *Quid faciet* (Loserth, 238.19; LEDGER E11c-4). *Ut … non debet*: *ut* with the indicative; *debet* has the church as subject. *Proderit*: the sense requires "betrays" (*prodit*), which we translate; the print's *proderit* ("will profit") does not take *veritatem*. *Speculator*: the watchman of Ezek. 3:17.

[^5]: **C.** *Secundum idem*: the clause of the principle of contradiction, "in the same respect". *Patet [quod] cum … foret … non foret*: taken as "it is clear that, since … would be …, it would not …"; *cum casu limitato* may instead be ablative absolute, "the case being limited". *In facultatibus et pena relaxari*: to be spared in his property and his punishment. *Pungi*: goaded, as the lethargic is *stimulandus*.

[^6]: **C/T.** Side-note 239.6. A in the margin *Epilogus. Nec omnia valent pro correccione peccancium* (239.6). B reads *omnino dandum* (239.11). *Cum paribus*: taken as the peers of the realm, joined to king and realm; *eis* goes with *dandam*. B reads *et se portatus* (239.4); the print is translated.

[^7]: **S/C.** *Libri VIⁱ*: which work Wyclif counts as book 6, and its cap. 25, are to be checked (LEDGER E11c-5). Romans 6:22, alluded to (*liberati a peccato*). B in the margin *Immunitas quid sit, nota* (239.18).

[^8]: **C/T.** B reads *quedam franchisia* (239.22–23) and *et buium* (239.24). *Munium*: Wyclif's form of *munia*, duties, glossed *tributum*; the derivation is the grammarians' (cf. Isidore, *Etym.* X.141, *immunis, qui non facit munia*, PL 82:382A, and XV.2.10, *munia, id est tributa debita*, 82:537C; the form *ab in et munium* is not Isidore's, LEDGER E11c-6). *Ut est expers*: the clause is loose; taken as describing who is properly immune. *Irreatus pene*: a non-classical abstract, "non-liability to penalty". *Libertantem*: who grants the liberty (franchise).

[^9]: **C.** Loserth (239.32): *Contra. Prima obieccio*; side-note 240.1. *Suffragium ecclesie*: the aid of the church, i.e. sanctuary. *Servum … extrahere*: the law that a fugitive servant be returned (see n. 11). *Sed*: the minor of the objection.

[^10]: **T/C.** A in the margin *Responsio* (240.3); side-note 240.12. B reads *in iure*, apparently for *de racione*; A *culpali* for *culpabili* (240.6); the print is translated. *De racione*: in principle, by the nature of the case. *Hoc … prosequatur*: the church applies this exclusion unequally.

[^11]: **S.** *De hiis qui ad ecclesiam fugiunt, le. fi.*: *le. fi.* is the civilians' *lex finalis*, the last law of a title; the title matches the Code's *De his qui ad ecclesias confugiunt* (Cod. 1.12), to be checked (LEDGER E11c-7). [^12]: **S.** Ephesians 6:5–7, paraphrased: Wyclif joins verse 5 (*cum timore et tremore … sicut Christo*) to verse 7 (*sicut Domino et non hominibus*); DR. *Servus*: "servant" throughout, as DR, though the law in question is of slaves. *Prius*: earlier, i.e. by a prior debt.

[^13]: **S/T.** C. 23 q. 4 c. 50 (Loserth's *causa XXIII, cap. L*, without the *quaestio*, 240.25; LEDGER E11c-8). A and A₁ omit *Unde* (240.25). *Deferimus*: for *differimus*, "put off".

[^14]: **S/G/T.** *Collacione tercia*: the third collation of the *Authenticum*, as at 226.5; the novel is to be checked (LEDGER E10f-7). *Suffragari*: the GLOSSARY's "to aid by intercession" is shortened to "to aid", since here privileges aid, not prayers. B reads *ledit se* (241.4). *Comparticipem*: agrees with *proximum*: the neighbour who shares the sin; side-note 241.1.

[^15]: **S/T.** X 3.19.8, *Cum universorum* (Loserth, 241.8; our modern form; LEDGER E11c-9). Isaiah 10:1, as the Vulgate; DR. B reads *qui comedunt* (241.12). *Continuandum iusticias*: to maintain just dealing.
