# *De potestate pape*, chapter 6, part 8: states: whether a man's state is changed by sin

## ¶1 · 129.32–131.34

> [p. 129] Et istam sentenciam approbat tota Trinitas cum tota ecclesia triumphante, tota natura bestiarum [p. 130] cum condicione status innocencie et saniores monachi, qui propter perfeccionem decreverunt statum illum eligere. Et patet probabilitas cronice, quam narrat Cestrensis libro IV, cap. LXXXVI quomodo tempore dotacionis ecclesie audita est angelica vox in aere, dicens quod hodie effusum est venenum in ecclesia sancta Dei. Nam quomodocumque hoc fuerit, hoc est verum, cum clerus a statu perfecciori decidit sine equivalente recompensa, ut eventus patulus rei probat. Unde manifestum signum foret Pseudochristi dampnantis ut hereticum quod genus illorum qui sic maculaverunt matrem nostram possunt eam purgare de macula quam ex eorum stulticia sic contraxit. Unde capitalis defendens pertinaciter istam sentenciam contra perfeccionem membrorum Christi et sancte matris ecclesie, heresiarcha racionaliter potest dici. Sed dubitatur si homo sit simul in multis statibus. Et videtur planum quod sic de statibus tam corporis quam anime compossibilibus, sicut homo habet simul denominaciones multorum actuum, ut puta qualitatum. Similiter differendum est utrique preposito peccanti propter dignitatem status, ut patet LXXXII distinccione cap. Presbiter et sepe alibi; et cum hoc valet efficaciter testimonium Augustini in multis locis, ut ubi docet quod in Pharaone, in Nabuchodonosor propter statum et dignitatem regalem ad regimen populi subiecti Deus inspiraverat propheciam, et propter sacerdotalem [p. 131] dignitatem Deus dedit Cayphe propheciam, ut dicit Augustinus in de Questionibus veteris et nove legis XXXV, et hinc David rite pepercit Sauli, ut patet I Reg. XXIV. Similiter, aliter nullius status celsitudo gravaret peccatum, quod est contra illud Sapiencie VI, 6 Durissimum iudicium hiis qui presunt, fiet, ymo aliter cuiuscumque prepositi status extingueretur, antequam quicquam boni vel mali faceret, que contradicunt scripture, cum ecclesie legibus, communi modo loquendi et sanctis doctoribus. Supposita igitur constancia statuum et facta limitacione ad statum quem preter nudos ritus humanos Deus ordinat, est dubium quomodo status variatur propter actus statui dissonos vel peccatum. Videtur enim quod status cum sit accidens tam parve constancie quod sit inalterabilis et per idem immobilis, cum manet eius dignitas cum peccato; aliter enim status sacerdotalis posset per alteracionem subiecti equiparari statui laycali et quilibet status cuilibet. Similiter, cum dignitas status hominis sit impertinens suis moribus, videtur quod stat eundem esse simul quantumcumque dignum et indignum et propter multiplicitatem statuum foret impertinens discernere per statum de hominis dignitate, nec sufficit homo cognoscere statum totalem aggregatum, tum quia infinitus est statuum numerus, tum quia aliquem statum proximi oportet viatorem quomodolibet ignorare. Similiter, cum status denigrari et debilitari poterit et alias serenari, videtur quod omne mortale statum quem invenit inficit, cum secundum Augustinum facit hominem esse nichil.

And this [teaching]{g:sentencia} the whole Trinity approves, with the whole [church]{g:ecclesia} triumphant, the whole nature of beasts [130] together with the condition of the state of innocence, and the sounder monks, who for the sake of perfection decided to choose that state.[^1] And the probability of the chronicle is clear which Cestrensis tells in book 4, chapter 86: how at the time of the [endowment]{g:dotacio} of the church an angelic voice was heard in the air, saying that "today poison has been poured out into the holy church of God".[^2] For however this may have been, this is true, since the clergy fell from a more perfect state without equivalent recompense, as the open outcome of the matter proves. Hence it would be a manifest sign of a [false christ]{g:pseudochristus}, condemning as heretical [the teaching] that the kind of those who so stained [our mother]{g:mater-nostra} can cleanse her of the stain which she thus contracted from their folly. Hence the chief man who defends this teaching pertinaciously against the perfection of the [members]{g:membrum} of Christ and of holy mother church can reasonably be called a heresiarch.[^3] But it is asked whether a man may be in many states at once. And it seems plain that he may, as to states of body as well as of soul that are [compossible]{g:compossibilis}, just as a man has at once the [namings]{g:denominacio} of many acts, for instance of qualities. Likewise, deference is to be shown to each [superior]{g:prepositi} who sins, on account of the dignity of his state, as is clear in distinction 82, chapter *Presbiter*, and often elsewhere;[^4] and with this the testimony of Augustine holds good effectively in many places, as where he teaches that in Pharaoh and in Nebuchadnezzar God had inspired prophecy on account of the royal state and dignity, for the rule of the subject people, and on account of the priestly [131] dignity God gave Caiaphas prophecy, as Augustine says in *On Questions of the Old and New Law* 35; and hence David rightly spared Saul, as is clear in 1 Kings 24.[^5] Likewise, otherwise no loftiness of state would aggravate sin, which is against that word of Wisdom 6:6, "a most severe judgment shall be for them that bear rule"; indeed, otherwise the state of any superior whatever would be extinguished before he did anything good or bad; and these things contradict scripture, together with the laws of the church, the common way of speaking, and the holy doctors.[^6] Supposing, therefore, the constancy of states, and the [limitation]{g:limitacio} being made to the state which God ordains beyond bare human [rites]{g:ritus}, there is a question how a state is varied on account of acts discordant with the state, or of sin. For it seems that a state, although it is an accident of such slight constancy, is unalterable and by the same token immovable, since its dignity remains along with sin; for otherwise the priestly state could, through alteration of the subject, be made equal to the lay state, and any state to any other.[^7] Likewise, since the dignity of a man's state is irrelevant to his morals, it seems that it is consistent for the same man to be at once however worthy and unworthy; and on account of the multiplicity of states it would be irrelevant to judge of a man's dignity by his state; nor is a man able to know the total aggregate state, both because the number of states is infinite, and because a [wayfarer]{g:viator} must in some way be ignorant of some state of his neighbour. Likewise, since a state can be blackened and weakened and at another time made bright, it seems that every mortal [sin] infects the state it finds, since according to Augustine it makes a man to be nothing.[^8]

## ¶2 · 132.1–133.25

> [p. 132] Ex quo videtur quod sicut homo dubitat de proximo si sit in mortali vel prescitus, sic dubitaret de excellencia cuiuscumque status viantis. Sic enim fuit status exproprietarie vivendi Scariothis vilissimus, ymo cum status sit successivus et dubitatur de fine qui est status beatitudinis secundum Boecium III de Consolatione philosophie videtur, quod quilibet foret de totali statu fratris sui ambiguus. Hic oportet supponere descripcionem et distinccionem supradictas de statibus. Et secundo oportet notare quomodo dupliciter aliquid dicitur transmutari, vel subiective vel gracia concomitancie. Subiective ut sola substancia transmutatur; gracia concomitante omnis status vel res alia inexistens homini melioratur vel peioratur propter concomitanciam virtutis aut vicii. Et sic res in natura sua continue nunc est melior et nunc peior, et sic fides et spes possunt nunc formari caritate et nunc remanere informes, cum ipsa non permittit secum crimen ut suprema virtus et simplex. Et patet, quod triplex primum argumentum roborat veritatem. Quantum ad primum trium argumentorum sequencium patet ex distinccione iam posita, quomodo status non alteratur subiective sed con- comitative. Et quandoque per peccatum extinguitur, quod pertinet ad eum cognoscere, qui nobis incomprehensibiliter ordinat cunctos status; quamvis autem materia de statibus sit valde ampli ambitus, ut medicus tractat de statu corporis, ut precedit ad felicitatem vie sive miseriam, politicus [p. 133] autem considerat de statu partis populi, secundum quem respicit pacem reipublice, tamen theologus et curatus, qui debet esse animarum medicus, considerat de statu hominis secundum virtutes vel vicia, ut respicit statum beatitudinis; et de tali statu est nobis sermo. Ipse autem infinitum excedit status alios, eciam status vivendi sive officii, qui dicuntur racione concomitancie virtutis vel vicii alterari; et patet ad primum obiectum. Ad secundum dicitur quod status temporalis est impertinens moribus quantum ad naturam status pertinet, vel in se simpliciter quod solum considerantes naturas universalium et reduplicativas concipiunt, verumtamen quantum ad denominacionem respectivam concomitancie status, tales valde pertinenter respiciunt virtutes et vicia; et hinc voco eos status disposicionis et alios virtutis; et sic concedo quod stat eundem esse dignum et indignum equivoce quoad dignitates morum et status, ideo pertinet specificare de qua hominis dignitate loquimur, quando ex statu dignitatem hominis arbitramur, et ultra conceditur quod non sufficimus hic cognoscere nisi imperfecte de statu totali hominis, quia ignoramus de exitu.

From which it seems that, just as a man is in doubt about his neighbour whether he is in mortal [sin] or [foreknown]{g:prescitus}, so he would be in doubt about the excellence of any state of [one on the road]{g:viantes}. For so the state of living [without property]{g:exproprietarie} was most vile in Iscariot;[^9] indeed, since a state is [successive]{g:successivum} and there is doubt about the end, which is the state of blessedness according to Boethius, *On the Consolation of Philosophy* III, it seems that each would be uncertain of the total state of his brother.[^10] Here one must suppose the [definition]{g:descripcio} and distinction of states given above. And secondly one must note how something is said to be changed in two ways: either subjectively or by reason of concomitance. Subjectively, as the substance alone is changed; by reason of concomitance, every state or other thing existing in a man is bettered or worsened on account of the concomitance of virtue or vice.[^11] And so a thing in its nature is continually at one time better and at another worse; and so faith and hope can at one time be formed by [charity]{g:caritas} and at another remain unformed, since charity, as the supreme and simple [virtue]{g:virtus}, does not allow crime alongside it.[^12] And it is clear that the threefold first argument strengthens the truth. As to the first of the three following arguments, it is clear from the distinction now set down how a state is altered not subjectively but concomitatively. And sometimes it is extinguished through sin, which it belongs to him to know who, incomprehensibly to us, ordains all states. And although the matter of states is of very wide compass (as the physician treats of the state of the body as it leads on to the felicity or misery of [the way]{g:via}, and the politician [133] considers the state of a part of the people according as it regards the peace of the commonwealth), yet the theologian and the [curate]{g:curatus}, who ought to be the physician of souls, considers the state of a man according to virtues or vices, as it regards the state of blessedness; and of such a state is our discourse.[^13] And this state infinitely exceeds the other states, even the states of living or of office, which are said to be altered by reason of the concomitance of virtue or vice; and the answer to the first [objection]{g:objectum} is clear. To the second the answer is that a temporal state is irrelevant to morals so far as concerns the nature of the state, or in itself [without qualification]{g:simpliciter}, which only those conceive who consider the natures of universals and reduplicative [natures]; nevertheless, as to the [relational]{g:respectivus} naming of the concomitance of the state, such states bear very relevantly on virtues and vices; and hence I call these states of disposition, and the others states of virtue. And so I grant that it is consistent for the same man to be worthy and unworthy [equivocally]{g:equivoce}, as regards the dignities of morals and of state; therefore it is pertinent to specify of which dignity of a man we speak when we judge a man's dignity from his state. And further it is granted that here we are not able to know the total state of a man except imperfectly, because we are ignorant of the outcome.[^14]

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 130.19: A man may be in several states at once.
- 130.24: The higher a man's state, the worse his sin.
- 131.17: A man's state is not changed by his sin.
- 132.1: We cannot judge of the spiritual state of others.
- 132.11: Change may be in substance or in concomitant good or evil.
- 132.24: It is in the latter way that state is altered,
- 133.1: As doctors treat of the state of the body, so priests should of the soul.

## Notes

[^1]: **T/C/R.** *Monachi* is Loserth's correction; the manuscripts read *monses* (130.2). "This teaching" is the one restated at 129.4 ff., that the state of living without property is more perfect than any civil state; AB in marg.: *Status exproprietarius approbatur a tota Trinitate* (129.32). Beasts and man in the state of innocence are cited, it seems, as holding nothing as property.

[^2]: **S/R.** Cestrensis, "the man of Chester", is Ranulf Higden, *Polychronicon*; Loserth corrects the chapter: not 86 but 26, "tom. V, 130" (130.4; LEDGER P06h-1, editor). The voice is the legend attached to Constantine's endowment of the church. AB in marg.: *Vox hodie effusum est venenum* (130.7).

[^3]: **C.** *Dampnantis ut hereticum quod …*: the participle agrees with *Pseudochristi*, and the *quod*-clause is what he condemns: that the descendants (*genus*) of the endowers can cleanse the church of the stain, i.e. take the endowment back. *Possunt* is plural with the collective *genus*. *Capitalis defendens*: "the chief man who defends", the leader of that party; *istam sentenciam* here is the condemning teaching just named, not the teaching of 129.32.

[^4]: **C/S.** *Differendum … utrique*: *differendum* read as *deferendum*, "deference is to be shown"; *utrique*, strictly "to each of two", is rendered "to each" (a doubt: the two may be the man and his state). Decretum D.82 c.5 *Presbiter* (Loserth 130.26; the modern form is ours; LEDGER P06h-2, editor). AB in marg.: *Status multos si tenet homo* (130.19).

[^5]: **S.** Pharaoh's dreams, Gen. 41; Nebuchadnezzar's, Dan. 2; Caiaphas, John 11:51 (DR "being the high priest of that year, he prophesied"). The *Quaestiones veteris et novi testamenti* (Wyclif: *legis*), q. 35, now given to Ambrosiaster; Loserth refers to Augustine, *Opera* III, Appendix, p. 53 (131.3; LEDGER P06h-3, editor); the content of q. 35 is to be checked (LEDGER P06h-4). 1 Kings 24 (1 Sam. 24): David spares Saul as "the Lord's anointed" (24:7).

[^6]: **S.** Wis. 6:6 (DR; Wyclif puts *Durissimum* first). *Hiis* and *fiet* were read on the plate.

[^7]: **C.** *Cum sit accidens tam parve constancie quod sit inalterabilis*: *cum* is taken as concessive and the *quod*-clause as the content of *videtur* ("it seems that a state, although …, is unalterable"). Taken as *tam … quod* ("of so slight constancy that it is unalterable") the clause contradicts itself. This is the first of the three arguments answered at 132.21 ff.

[^8]: **S.** Augustine: sin is nothing, and men become nothing when they sin (from memory, *In Iohannis evangelium* tr. 1.13; LEDGER P06h-5, to-verify). Loserth gives no reference. This is the third argument, answered at 133.26.

[^9]: **C/T/R.** *Sic enim*: Judas lived without property among the apostles, yet his state was the vilest, so the excellence of a state cannot be read from its kind. B reads *vilissimis* (132.5). A in marg.: *Nota optime* (132.4).

[^10]: **T/S.** *De Consolatione* is Loserth's correction; the manuscripts read *de questione* (132.7). Boethius, *Consolatio* III pr. 2, beatitude as "a state made perfect by the gathering of all goods" (*status bonorum omnium congregatione perfectus*, from memory; Loserth: PL 63, 720; LEDGER P06h-6, editor; the wording P06h-7, to-verify).

[^11]: **T.** The print has *gracia concomitante* (132.14, so the plate) where the line before has *gracia concomitancie*; we read *concomitancie* (our emendation) and render it as the same phrase, "by reason of concomitance" (*gracia* with a genitive, "for the sake of"). As an ablative absolute it would be "concomitant grace" (a glossary term, *gracia concomitans*), which cannot be "bettered or worsened … on account of vice". AB in marg.: *Mutari dicitur aliquid dupliciter* (132.11).

[^12]: **T/C.** *Caritate* is Loserth's correction; the manuscripts read *caritatem* (132.19). *Ipsa* is taken as charity. "Formed … unformed": formed faith and unformed faith, faith formed by charity.

[^13]: **C/T/R.** *Triplex primum argumentum*: the three-part argument first stated (130.19–131.12: many states at once, deference to the sinning superior, the gravity of a lofty sinner's sin), set against the "three following arguments" (131.12 ff.) now answered in turn. *Quamvis autem … tamen*: the *autem* only continues the period. B reads *policitus* (132.31). B in marg.: *Ad primum* (132.23).

[^14]: **C/R.** *Naturas universalium et reduplicativas*: *reduplicativas* agrees with *naturas*: natures taken reduplicatively, the state "as a state". *Status disposicionis* and *status virtutis* are Wyclif's names. *Exitus*: the end of life. AB in marg.: *Ad 2* (133.10); and at 132.30, *Diversitas statuum. Dignum et indignum stat eundem esse hominem*.
