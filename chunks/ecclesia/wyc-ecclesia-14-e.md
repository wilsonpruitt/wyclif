# *De ecclesia*, chapter 14, part 5: the two swords, and the clergy's civil lordship not founded in scripture

## ¶1 · 314.28–316.10

> [p. 314] Intelligo autem per utrumque gladium ad sensum misticum potenciam castigandi, priorem potenciam [p. 315] castigandi spiritualiter tam filios ecclesie quam eciam inimicos, et secundum gladium potestatem corporaliter cohercendi. Et cum iste due partes ecclesie sicut omnia eius membra in omnibus que habent communicant, patet quod utraque istarum parcium habet modo suo utrumque gladium, modo quo loquitur Apostolus II Corinthiorum II°, 3: Meum gaudium omnium vestrum est. Sacerdos itaque habet gladium spiritualem in se formaliter et sue iurisdiccioni subiectum, ut regulet eius usum. Sed ad hoc requiritur noticia utriusque testamenti, ut sciat docere quomodo et qualiter est ille gladius exercendus; aliter enim non daret Deus gladium illum in manus ydiote, frenetici sive litargici, qui non sciat uti ipso, ad defensionem ecclesie secundum regulas pugnandi quas Christus instituit, sed omnino ad dampnum vel perturbacionem ecclesie. Habet ergo secularis gladium temporalem in se subiective ad illud officium exequendum; sacerdos autem habet, si sciverit, illum gladium obiective ad eius officium regulandum, et sic utraque pars ecclesie comparticipat fructu gladii utriusque. Christus autem fuit simul rex et sacerdos, habens in se subiective utrumque gladium, modo quo impossibile fuit ut Petro vel eius vicario communicetur, eo quod principium generis et exemplar oportet esse perfectius, habens simplicius omnes raciones tocius generis christiani. Unde oportet quod post illud caput sive principium tocius christiani generis sint duo rami disparis vicarie, scilicet laicorum et clericorum; et in illis ramis fundatis in eadem vite sunt quoad subiectacionem impermixte due maneries potestatum. Nec sufficit aliquis Christi vicarius sine peccato plene exercere unam illarum potestatum; multo magis ipsis confuse commixtis. Unde ad figurandum [p. 316] illas potestates precipit Christus gladios eius virtutibus et diffinivit sufficienciam in duobus. Et (ut notat beatus Bernardus ad papam Eugenium) per hoc quod precipit Petro ponere gladium in vaginam, prohibuit clero in persona vel causa sua execucionem gladii corporalis. Christus autem servans inseparabiliter summum gradum innocencie, scivit sine defectu vel confusione utrumque gladium regulare et utriusque status honores accipere (ut alias explanavi), nec potuit cum istis statibus civiliter dominari.

And by each sword, in the mystical sense, I understand a power of chastising: by the first, the power of chastising [315] spiritually both the sons of the [church]{g:ecclesia} and its enemies too, and by the second sword the power of [coercing]{g:cohercere} bodily. And since these two parts of the church, like all its [members]{g:membrum}, share in everything they have, it is clear that each of these parts has both swords in its own manner, in the manner in which the Apostle speaks, 2 Corinthians 2:3: "My joy is the joy of you all." The [priest]{g:sacerdos}, then, has the spiritual sword in himself formally, and subject to his jurisdiction, so that he may regulate its use.[^1] But for this a knowledge of both testaments is required, so that he may know how to teach how and in what manner that sword is to be exercised; for otherwise God would not be giving that sword into the hands of an unlettered man, a frenzied or a lethargic man, who does not know how to use it, for the defence of the church according to the rules of fighting which Christ instituted, but wholly to the harm or disturbance of the church.[^2] [A secular]{g:secularis}, therefore, has the temporal sword in himself subjectively, for carrying out that office; but the priest has that sword, if he has the knowledge, objectively, for regulating its office; and so each part of the church shares in the fruit of both swords.[^3] But Christ was at once king and priest, having both swords in himself subjectively, in a manner in which it was impossible that [it] be communicated to Peter or to his vicar, because the principle and exemplar of a kind must be more perfect, holding more simply all the aspects of the whole Christian kind. Hence after that [head]{g:caput} or principle of the whole Christian kind there must be two branches of disparate vicarship, namely of the laity and of the clergy; and in those branches, founded in the same vine, there are two sorts of powers, unmixed as to the subject [that bears them].[^4] Nor is any [vicar of Christ]{g:vicarius Christi} sufficient to exercise one of those powers fully without sin; much more so with them mixed together in confusion. Hence, to figure [316] those powers, Christ commanded swords for its powers, and defined their sufficiency as two.[^5] And (as blessed Bernard notes to Pope Eugenius), by commanding Peter to put up the sword into the scabbard, he forbade the [clergy]{g:clerus} the execution of the bodily sword in their own person or cause.[^6] But Christ, keeping inseparably the highest degree of innocence, knew how to regulate both swords without defect or confusion and to receive the honours of both estates (as I have explained elsewhere); and he could not, with these estates, [exercise lordship]{g:dominari} civilly.[^7]

## ¶2 · 316.11–316.29

> [p. 316] Et si queratur ubi scriptura prohibet clerum civi- liter dominari, interrogo sic querentem unam aliam questionem: Cum omne privilegium ecclesie sit fundabile in scriptura, ubi in scriptura fundatur clero potestas vel utilitas taliter dominandi? Nam secundum Augustinum, II° De Doctrina Christiana, in fine: Si aliquid utile est, ibi invenitur; sed nulla talis cleri dominacio ibi invenitur, ergo nulla talis cleri dominacio est utilis. Maior istius racionis est assercio archidoctoris Augustini qui multum horrebat mendacium; que maior vel est necessaria vel summe heretica, quod non dicetur ob reverenciam tanti sancti. Et pro minori huius argumenti est plana induccio per singulas partes alterius testamenti; vel si non sit plana induccio, dicat falsigraphus ubi fundatur clero Christi taliter dominativa possessio. Nam tam necessarium et tam notabile privilegium foret ab auctore scripture et capite ecclesie alicubi expressatum. Sed ego non reperi, nisi dicatur yronice vel per antifrasim quod

And if it is asked where scripture forbids the clergy to exercise lordship civilly, I ask the one so asking another question: since every [privilege]{g:privilegium} of the church can be founded in scripture, where in scripture is the power or the usefulness of exercising lordship in this way founded for the clergy? For according to Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* 2, at the end: "If anything is useful, it is found there";[^8] now no such [exercise of lordship]{g:dominacio} by the clergy is found there; therefore no such exercise of lordship by the clergy is useful. The major of this argument is the assertion of the arch-doctor Augustine, who greatly abhorred a lie; and this major is either necessary or supremely heretical, which will not be said out of reverence for so great a saint. And for the minor of this argument there is a plain induction through the several parts of either testament; or, if the induction is not plain, let the [false writer]{g:falsigraphus} say where possession that lords it in this way is founded for the clergy of Christ.[^9] For so necessary and so notable a privilege would have been expressed somewhere by the author of scripture and head of the church. But I have not found it, unless it be said ironically or by antiphrasis that[^10]

## ¶3 · 317.1–318.3

> [p. 317] in hiis tribus locis fundatur dominacio civilis clericorum. Primo in illo Luce XXII°, 25, 26: Reges gencium dominantur eorum, vos autem non sic; ubi notum est quod Christus approbavit dominacionem secularem regum gentilium, ut patet de Job, de Nabuchodonosor et de aliis regibus tam preteritis quam futuris. Sed iuxta beatum Bernardum interdixit eam suis apostolis. Secundus locus scripture in quo taliter fundaretur seculare dominium clericorum foret preceptum Christi Matthei X°, 9: Nolite possidere aurum neque argentum; iuxta quod locutus est Petrus ceco, ut legitur Actorum III, 6°, dicens: Argentum et aurum non est mihi, quod autem habeo hoc tibi do. Et hinc tradit pro regula, V° capitulo sue prime epistole, quod non simus dominantes in clero sed forma facti gregis ad deserendum mundum et eius concupiscencias, non coacte et invite, sed sponte et ex animo. Tercius locus scripture est dictum Apostoli, Iª Tim. VI°, 8, dicentis: Habentes ergo alimenta et quibus tegamur, hiis contenti simus. Ille autem non est contentus alimento et tegumento qui ultra hec exigit nedum temporalium superaffluenciam ad ministrandum sed ad dominandum, nec solum clericaliter sed seculariter. Unde contendimus instancius circa iurisdiccionem et secularem dominacionem tamquam nobis privilegium requisitum, quod tangit Doctor Solempnis, VI° quolibeto q. XXIIIª, sed cum formidine; quod, inquit, ecclesiastici temporalium habeant jurisdiccionem forte non multum expedit et erat forte venenum [p. 318] quod legitur cecidisse in ecclesia Dei tempore Constantini qui in iurisdiccione sua dotavit ecclesiam, sed non assero.

the civil exercise of lordship of the clergy is founded in these three places. First, in that of Luke 22:25–26: "The kings of the Gentiles lord it over them ... but you not so"; where it is known that Christ approved the secular exercise of lordship of the gentile kings, as is clear of Job, of Nebuchadnezzar, and of other kings both past and future. But according to blessed Bernard he forbade it to his apostles.[^11] The second place of scripture in which the secular [lordship]{g:dominium} of the clergy would be founded in this way would be Christ's command, Matthew 10:9: "Do not possess gold, nor silver"; in keeping with which Peter spoke to the blind man, as we read in Acts 3:6, saying: "Silver and gold I have none; but what I have, I give thee."[^12] And hence he gives as a rule, in the fifth chapter of his first epistle, that we should not be lording it over the clergy, but being made a pattern of the flock for forsaking the world and its desires, not by constraint and unwillingly, but willingly and from the heart.[^13] The third place of scripture is the saying of the Apostle, 1 Timothy 6:8: "Having therefore food and wherewith to be covered, with these we are content."[^14] Now he is not content with food and covering who beyond these demands a superabundance of [temporal goods]{g:temporalia} not only for ministering but for lording it, and not only in clerical fashion but in secular fashion. Hence we contend more insistently about jurisdiction and the secular exercise of lordship as a privilege required for us, a point the Solemn Doctor touches on, *Quodlibet* 6, q. 23, but with [misgiving]{g:formido}: "That the [clergy]{g:ecclesiastici}," he says, "should have jurisdiction over temporal goods is perhaps not very expedient, and it was perhaps the poison [318] which we read fell into the church of God in the time of Constantine, who endowed the church in his jurisdiction; but I do not assert it."[^15][^16][^17]

## ¶4 · 318.4–318.26

> [p. 318] Iste autem doctor reprobatus fuit acute, quia tractavit de potestate pape, ideo innuit cum formidine quod debuit constanter asserere, ut docet Hugo de Sancto Victore, libro II° De Sacramentis, parte II°, capitulis III° et VII°. Nam quod amplius est quam liber usus fructus in clero, vergens ad ritum mundanum, malum est, quia sacerdotalis officii tardativum. Cum ergo in istis locis scripture non fundatur clero civilitas sed plane contrarium, nec est scriptura sibi ipsi contraria, utinam illud dominium esset suspensum a clero, quousque in scriptura sacra eius invenerit fundamentum, et vellem si Deo placeret quod nullus sacerdos haberet primatum vel privilegium ecclesie quousque cartam illius invenerit in scriptura. Cum enim nemo debet privilegium appetere nisi sibi utile, et secundum Augustinum si utile est, ibi invenitur, videtur quod ad clericum privilegiandum pertinet quod cartam tam autenticam inveniat in scriptura; tunc enim laboraremus circa eius noticiam, niteremurve in deposicione sic nobis adversaria ex deformitate vite nostre ad cartam illam. Et tercio non acceptaremus tantum onus nisi quasi nolentes ad magnam instanciam populi pro spirituali suffragio nos urgentis.

And this doctor had been sharply rebuked because he treated of the [pope's]{g:papa} power; therefore he hinted with misgiving what he ought to have asserted steadfastly, as Hugh of St Victor teaches, *On the Sacraments*, book 2, part 2, chapters 3 and 7.[^18] For whatever in the clergy is more than the free use of the fruits, tending to a worldly rite, is evil, because it is a hindrance to the priestly office.[^19] Since therefore in these places of scripture civil standing is not founded for the clergy, but plainly the contrary, and scripture is not contrary to itself, would that that lordship were [suspended]{g:suspendere} from the clergy until it has found its foundation in holy scripture; and I would wish, if it pleased God, that no priest should have a primacy or privilege of the church until he has found its charter in scripture.[^20] For since no one ought to desire a privilege unless it is useful to him, and according to Augustine, "if it is useful, it is found there", it seems that it belongs to the cleric [who is to be privileged]{g:privilegiandus} that he find so [authentic]{g:autenticus} a charter in scripture; for then we would labour over the knowledge of it, or we would strive at a laying aside, so adverse to us from the deformity of our life, [answering] to that charter.[^21] And thirdly, we would not accept so great a burden except as though unwilling, at the great [urging]{g:instancia} of the people pressing us for spiritual [aid]{g:suffragium}.[^22]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** As printed, both members describe the spiritual sword: the priest has it "in himself formally" and "subject to his jurisdiction". What follows (the priest has "that sword", the temporal one, objectively, to regulate the secular's office) and the side-note at 315.18 suggest that the second member meant the temporal sword; the plate shows no word lost at 315.7–8. Translated as printed.
[^2]: **C.** *Ydiota* is the unlettered man, not the fool. The *enim* clause argues from the contrary: given to one without knowledge of the testaments, the sword would serve not the defence of the church but its harm.
[^3]: **C.** *Subiective*: as the subject that bears the power; *obiective*: as the object of the priest's knowledge and direction. *Secularis* is here the layman, the lay part of the church against the clergy.
[^4]: **C.** *Genus* is both the logical genus (a principle "more perfect" than the kind it heads) and the Christian people; "kind" keeps both. *Quoad subiectacionem*: as to the subject in which each power inheres. *Confuse commixtis* just after is "mixed together in confusion", not the cognitive *confusus* of the glossary.
[^5]: **T.** 316.1: the print *precipit Christus gladios eius virtutibus* hardly construes. A reads *gladios eius*; A₁ *gladios virtutibus*; B *gladios enis* (sic) *virtutibus*. Luke 22:36–38 (the command to buy a sword; "Lord, behold, here are two swords. ... It is enough") is plainly in view, and *eius* may stand for *emi* ("commanded swords to be bought"), but no witness has it and the emendation is not adopted.
[^6]: **S.** Bernard, *De consideratione* IV, the editor's reference (316.3 foot: Opp. p. 1036); in the modern form *De consid.* 4.3.7, ours (LEDGER E14e-1). The command is John 18:11, "Put up thy sword into the scabbard."
[^7]: **C/R.** *Nec potuit*: even holding both estates without confusion, Christ could not exercise civil lordship. B's margin at 314.14 asks the question this answers: if Christ had power to coerce bodily by reason of his regality, why is civil lordship denied to him?
[^8]: **S.** Augustine, *De doctrina christiana* 2.42.63, the editor's reference (316.15 foot, read as "cap. XLII ... 63"); modern form ours (LEDGER E14e-2).
[^9]: **C.** *Alterius testamenti* may be "of either testament" (each in turn) or "of the other testament" (the New, against the Old); the induction is the same either way.
[^10]: **T.** The editor's paragraph ends here mid-sentence; the clause runs on at 317.1.
[^11]: **S.** DR Luke 22:25–26, the intervening words left out by Wyclif. Bernard's ruling that lordship is forbidden to the apostles is *De consideratione* II.6.10–11 (PL 182; *Planum est: apostolis interdicitur dominatus*) (LEDGER E14e-3).
[^12]: **S.** In Acts 3:2 the man is lame (*claudus*), not blind; the print has *ceco*, and the apparatus records no variant.
[^13]: **S.** 1 Pet. 5:2–3, paraphrased; the Vulgate has *neque ut dominantes in cleris, sed forma facti gregis ex animo* and *non coacte, sed spontanee*. Wyclif adds "for forsaking the world and its desires". A reads *sub forma*.
[^14]: **S.** The Vulgate has *Habentes autem*; Wyclif's *ergo* is translated.
[^15]: **C.** *Ecclesiastici* (CONTEXTUAL): here the clergy, who are to have jurisdiction over temporal goods. *In iurisdiccione sua* may mean "within his jurisdiction" or "with his jurisdiction", i.e. by granting it.
[^16]: **S/R.** Henry of Ghent, *Quodlibet* 6 q. 23 (on papal grants of tithes from lay goods), quoted nearly word for word: *quod ecclesiastici temporalium habent iurisdictionem, forte non multum expedit; deerat forte venenum, quod legitur cecidisse in ecclesia Dei tempore Constantini* (ed. 1613, I, 170); the editor identifies the Solemn Doctor only (317.27 foot) (LEDGER E14e-4). The "poison" is the story of the voice heard at Constantine's donation, *hodie venenum infusum est in ecclesia*, a medieval legend not found in the Patrologia (LEDGER E14e-5). A and B note *Henricus de Gandano* in the margin; B adds *hereticus*, which the editor refers not to Henry but to John of Jandun.
[^17]: **T.** *Sed non assero* (318.3), lost from the OCR text and restored from the plate: Henry hedges the poison story.
[^18]: **C/S.** *Ut docet Hugo* hangs on what Henry "ought to have asserted steadfastly". Hugh of St Victor, *De sacramentis* II.2.3 and II.2.7, Wyclif's reference: chapter 3 sets clergy and laity as the church's two walls, chapter 7 how the church holds earthly things; the two powers themselves are chapter 4 (PL 176) (LEDGER E14e-6).
[^19]: **C.** *Ritus* is any usage or observance, not only a liturgical rite; "rite" is kept for it throughout. *Liber usus fructus*: the free use of the fruits, against ownership.
[^20]: **C.** *Civilitas*: civil standing, the condition of holding civil lordship. The subject of the first *invenerit* is the clergy.
[^21]: **C.** 318.22–24 is hard to construe. Taken here: *in deposicione* (a laying aside, of our deformed life or of what the charter forbids), with *adversaria* agreeing with it ("so adverse to us"), and *ad cartam illam* "answering to that charter". *Adversaria* may instead be a neuter plural ("what is so adverse to us"); the plate shows the print as we have it.
[^22]: **C.** *Instancia* here is urging, not an objection (*ad instanciam*, at someone's urging). *Suffragium* is "aid", not the church's suffrages.
