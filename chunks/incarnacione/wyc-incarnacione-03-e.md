# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 3, part 5: three objections: the living and dead body, the parts of Christ, mortal and immortal

## ¶1 · 44.27–45.13

> [p. 44] Sed contra illud obicitur. Videtur primo quod corpus mortuum¹² sit corpus vivum; quia Christus, qui est corpus mortuum¹² in dicto triduo, est corpus vivum post et ante: et sic, cum corpus animatum et corpus inanimatum distinguuntur in specie, videtur quod illa non sunt idem corpus in numero, cum differencia specifica presupponit differenciam [p. 45] numeralem.¹ Hic dicitur quod corpus vivum et animatum est idem corpus in numero mortuum et inanimatum, licet non simul et semel; nec differt corpus in quantum² animatum specifice ab inanimato² corpore. Pro quo notandum quod dupliciter³ dicitur corpus animatum, scilicet, ab extrinseco participative, vel ab intrinseco qualitative⁴; ut corpus compositum ex corpore et anima est animatum anima, que est eius forma et quiditas, et illud non potest esse inanimatum: sed corpus, quod est altera pars qualitativa hominis, est animatum participacione anime; que, cum non sit forma eius intrinseca, potest abesse a corpore manente eodem in numero; sicut homines, qui participative dicuntur dii possunt manere iidem⁵ Deo absente ab eis per graciam.

But against this [it is objected]{g:obicitur}. It seems, first, that a dead body is a living body; because Christ, who is a dead body in the said [three days]{g:triduum}, is a living body after and before: and so, since an animate body and an inanimate body are distinguished in species, it seems that they are not the same body in number, since a specific [difference]{g:differencia} presupposes a numerical [45] difference. Here [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that the living and animate body is the same body in number as the dead and inanimate, though not at one and the same time; nor does the body [insofar as]{g:in-quantum} animate differ in species from the inanimate body. For which it is to be noted that a body is called animate in two ways, namely from outside, [participatively]{g:participative}, or from within, qualitatively; as the body composed of body and soul is animated by the soul, which is its form and [quiddity]{g:quiditas}, and that cannot be inanimate: but the body which is the other [qualitative part]{g:pars-qualitativa} of man is animated by participation of the soul; and the soul, since it is not its intrinsic form, can be absent from the body while the body remains the same in number;[^1] as men, who are participatively called gods, can remain the same while God is absent from them by grace.[^2]

## ¶2 · 45.14–45.18

> [p. 45] 2°. obicitur sic. Si Christus in dicto triduo habet corpus et animam,⁶ tunc Christus est animatus; et per consequens tam corpus quam anima⁶ est pars⁷ Christi; et per consequens Christus in illo triduo componitur ex corpore et anima; et per consequens non iacet mortuus in sepulcro.

Second, it is objected thus. If Christ in the said three days has body and soul, then Christ is animate; and consequently both body and soul is a part of Christ; and consequently Christ in that three days is composed of body and soul; and consequently he does not lie dead in the sepulchre.

## ¶3 · 45.19–46.5

> [p. 45] Hic dupliciter⁸ dicitur. Primo quod tam⁹ corpus quam anima⁹ eius¹⁰ est pars Christi, sed non in dicto triduo; quia non tunc qualitative componunt Christum, sed alias pro suo tempore. Unde posito quod essemus in dicto triduo, vere sciremus quod hec¹¹ anima que nunc est anima Christi, sed non nunc pars Christi, est pars Christi optima post et ante; et correspondenter de corpore. Ex quo patet quod hec non est synecdochica¹² locucio: ‘hoc corpus’ vel ‘hec anima est nunc Christus:’ sed, sicut homo est essencia substancia vel quiditas sua intransitive intelligendo, sic Christus est tunc hoc corpus et hec anima, que sunt sua ad¹³ sensum intransitivum, ut supra. Unde hec anima, licet non nunc animat corpus illud quod iacet mortuum, animat tamen Filium Dei; [p. 46] sicut caput¹ denominat hominem capitatum: et patet quod² animare est equivocum ad corpus subiectum vivificare et ad denominare personam cui copulatur ypostatice habentem³ animam, quam⁴ per se animat, cum ad invicem⁵ consequuntur⁶ vires inferiores sicut passio ad subiectum.

Here the answer is twofold. First, that both his body and his soul is a part of Christ, but not in the said three days; because they do not then qualitatively compose Christ, but at other times, for their own time. Hence, supposing that we were in the said three days, we would truly know that this soul, which now is the soul of Christ but not now a part of Christ, is the best part of Christ after and before; and correspondingly of the body. From which it is clear that this is not a [synecdochic locution]{g:synecdochica-locucio}: "this body", or "this soul is now Christ":[^3] but, just as man is his [essence]{g:essencia}, substance or quiddity, understanding it [intransitively]{g:intransitive}, so Christ is then this body and this soul, which are his in the intransitive sense, as above.[^4] Hence this soul, although it does not now animate that body which lies dead, yet animates the Son of God; [46] as a head [names]{g:denominare} a man ["headed"]{g:capitatus}:[^5] and it is clear that "to animate" is equivocal between vivifying a body that is its [subject]{g:subiectum} and naming the [person]{g:persona} to whom it is joined [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} as having a soul, which it animates through itself, since the lower powers follow upon one another as an [attribute]{g:passio} upon a subject.[^6]

## ¶4 · 46.6–46.16

> [p. 46] Secunda responsio dicit quod tam corpus quam anima Christi est in triduo vere pars Christi, sed nec qualitativa nec quantitativa, cum non tunc⁷ componuntur ad idem,⁷ sed est pars discretiva. Et sic intelligit Damascenus, quod Christus descendit totus ad inferos, sicut totus iacuit in sepulcro, sed non totum; quia persona fuit homo in utroque loco, sed non secundum ambas naturas; et sic in triduo habuit omnes partes⁸ quas habuit⁸ post resurreccionem, sed⁹ tunc defecit unio vel¹⁰ composicio. Sed nullum absolutum¹¹: nec pono vim in illa variacione, quia utraque sentencia est satis¹² catholica ad sensus equivocos.¹³

The second response says that both the body and the soul of Christ is truly in the three days a part of Christ, but neither qualitative nor quantitative, since they are not then composed into the same thing, but it is a [discrete part]{g:pars-discretiva}. And so Damascene understands that Christ descended whole into hell, as he lay whole in the sepulchre, but not the whole; because the person was man in each place, but not as to both [natures]{g:natura}; and so in the three days he had all the parts he had after the resurrection, but then the [union]{g:unio} or composition was lacking.[^7] But nothing absolute [was lacking]: nor do I lay weight on that variation, because each [teaching]{g:sentencia} is catholic enough in the equivocal senses.[^8]

## ¶5 · 46.17–46.25

> [p. 46] Sed 3°. obicitur ex hoc, quod Christus in illo triduo nec est homo immortalis nec homo¹⁴ mortalis; et omnis homo est mortalis vel immortalis: ergo Christus pro illo triduo non est homo. Maior patet eo quod Christus tunc passus est mortem: ergo iuxta Apostolum ‘mors illi ultra¹⁵ non dominabitur.’ Et quod non sit homo immortalis videtur ex hoc quod iacet mortuus in sepulcro et nondum consecutus est dotem¹⁶ immortalitatis, sed primo in resurreccione sequente iuxta communem doctorum sentenciam.¹⁷

But third, it is objected from this, that Christ in that three days is neither an immortal man nor a mortal man; and every man is mortal or immortal: therefore Christ for that three days is not a man. The major is clear from this, that Christ then suffered death: therefore, according to the Apostle, "death shall no more have dominion over him."[^9] And that he is not an immortal man appears from this, that he lies dead in the sepulchre and has not yet obtained the [dower of immortality]{g:dos-immortalitatis}, but first in the resurrection that follows, according to the common teaching of the doctors.[^10]

## ¶6 · 46.26–47.13

> [p. 46] Hic dicitur quod Christus pro hoc¹⁸ triduo est tam homo mortalis quam eciam¹⁹ homo immortalis. Est homo mortalis quia corpus, quod moritur et²⁰ iacet mortuum in sepulcro. Pro quo notandum quod mori, cum sit privari vita, vel potest intelligi ut dicit subitum actum privacionis vite a corpore, vel ut dicit absolute²¹ carenciam vite a corpore prius²² vivificato. [p. 47] Primo modo¹ est mors Christi subita non possibilis iterari licet Christus eternaliter potest mori. 2°. modo est² mors triduana, sicut corpus incorruptibile per triduum caret vita, sicut aer habet tenebram triduanam, et sicut pater semper gignit³ filium, sol semper producit lumen et medium per tempus privatur lumine, licet quodlibet istorum complete continue sit⁴ productum. Sic Christus ad sensum expositum⁵ continue moritur per istud triduum et habet simplam mortem triduanam, sicut dampnati habent mortem perpetuam. Est ergo Christus mortalis pro illo triduo cum moritur in effectu: non potest tamen nisi solummodo semel mori. Est eciam immortalis pro illo triduo⁶ quia est spiritus Iesus,⁷ qui nec potest mori corporaliter nec spiritualiter.

Here the answer is that Christ for this three days is both a mortal man and also an immortal man. He is a mortal man because [he is] the body, which dies and lies dead in the sepulchre. For which it is to be noted that "to die", since it is to be deprived of life, can be understood either as it [signifies]{g:dicere} the sudden act of the privation of life from the body, or as it signifies, absolutely, the lack of life in a body previously vivified. [47] In the first way the death of Christ is sudden, not possible to be repeated, although Christ eternally can die.[^11] In the second way there is a three days' death, as the incorruptible body lacks life through the three days, as the air has a three days' darkness, and as the father always begets the son, the sun always produces light, and the medium is deprived of light through a time, although each of these is completely, continuously produced.[^12] So Christ, in the sense set out, continuously dies through that three days and has a single three days' death, as the damned have perpetual death. Christ is therefore mortal for that three days, since he dies [in actual fact]{g:in-effectu}: yet he cannot die except only once. He is also immortal for that three days because he is the spirit Jesus, which can die neither bodily nor spiritually.[^13]

## ¶7 · 47.14–48.2

> [p. 47] Et si obicitur quod hic predicatur oppositum de opposito; homo mortalis pro hoc instanti⁸ est homo immortalis; dicitur quod verum est. Ideo conclusio est impossibilis. Nec est color in isto paralogismo :—ergo homo mortalis est homo immortalis— iste homo Dominus Iesus est nunc homo mortalis, et iste idem homo est nunc homo immortalis;— Non enim sequitur, si persona que est commune ad ambas naturas illas est utraque illarum, quod ex hoc una sit reliqua; sicut non oportet, licet tantum sit unus Deus et ille sit copulative⁹ tam Deus genitus quam Deus ingenitus, quia tam Pater quam Filius, quod exinde Pater sit Filius vel Deus genitus sit Deus ingenitus, quod est idem. Nec sequitur, si Petrus sapiens et salvatus sit natura humana specifica, et Paulus insipiens et dampnatus sit eadem natura specifica, quod Petrus sapiens et salvatus sit Paulus insipiens¹⁰ et dampnatus. Et patet¹¹ ex simili modo¹¹ nutritis in rectis principiis. Sicut enim natura divina est trium personarum quelibet, et species humana personarum hominum quelibet; et tamen tantum est una natura divina sive [p. 48] species¹ humana:¹ sic persona Verbi Dei est trium naturarum quelibet et tamen una persona Verbi.

And if it is objected that here an opposite is predicated of an opposite, "a mortal man at this instant is an immortal man", the answer is that it is true. Therefore the [conclusion]{g:conclusio} is impossible. Nor is there [colour]{g:color} in this [paralogism]{g:paralogismus}: "therefore a mortal man is an immortal man", [from] "this man, the Lord Jesus, is now a mortal man, and this same man is now an immortal man".[^14] For [it does not follow]{g:sequitur}, if the person who is common to both those natures is each of them, that from this the one is the other; just as it is not necessary, although there is only one God and he is [conjunctively]{g:copulative} both God begotten and God unbegotten, because both Father and Son, that thence the Father is the Son, or God begotten is God unbegotten, which is the same. Nor does it follow, if Peter wise and saved is [the human nature of the species]{g:natura-specifica}, and Paul foolish and damned is the same nature of the species, that Peter wise and saved is Paul foolish and damned. And it is clear in a like way to those nourished in right principles.[^15] For just as the divine nature is each of the three persons, and the human species each of the persons of men; and yet there is only one divine nature, or [48] human species: so the person of [the Word]{g:verbum} of God is each of three natures, and yet one person of the Word.

## ¶8 · 48.3–48.28

> [p. 48] Quod autem Magister Distinctione 22 aliter loquitur cum modernis est propter equivocacionem, cum non admittit aliquid pro quacunque mensura esse hominem, nisi tunc componatur ex corpore et anima. Nos autem sequentes² scripturam non ficte sed philosophice³ dicimus quod eadem persona Petri vel cuiuscunque sancti prioris, que conversata est hic corporaliter in terris, manet iam beata in celis; et ubicunque est spiritus ille creatus sive in corpore sive extra corpus est idem homo in numero, et solum, ubi est corpus vel anima, est persona humanitus. Ut patet Ep. 40 ad Dardanum. Et cum certum sit quod Christus pro illo triduo non componitur qualitative⁴ ex corpore et anima, eo quod unio illorum dissolvitur; patet quod tunc nec est sic homo mortalis nec homo immortalis, quia non ad illum⁵ sensum tunc est homo. Si autem vocatur⁶ vere corpus et anima separata utrumque homo, sicut vocat Hugo de sancto Victore sequendo Augustinum, scripturas, et philosophicas raciones; tunc respondendum est sicut superius ad obiectus. Nam anima est per se homo et corpus nuncupative; et sic utrumque non duo homines, sed unus tantum. Si autem Magister intelligit hominem collective pro⁷ duabus naturis ad⁸ invicem et Verbo ypostatice copulatis, non est color quod tunc sit homo nisi utraque natura sit homo: et sic esset homo immortalis sicut anima fuit a sua⁹ origine immortalis; et per consequens partim mortalis, quia mortuus, et partim immortalis, quia anima. Hoc autem concedendum est de quolibet alio homine.

And that [the Master]{g:magister}, in Distinction 22, speaks otherwise, along with the moderns, is because of equivocation, since he does not admit that anything is a man for any measure [of time] unless it is then composed of body and soul.[^16] But we, following scripture not feignedly but philosophically, say that the same person of Peter or of any earlier saint, who lived here bodily on earth, remains now blessed in heaven; and wherever that [created]{g:creatus} spirit is, whether in the body or out of the body, it is the same man in number, and only where the body or the soul is, is the person there [humanly]{g:humanitus}.[^17] As is clear from Letter 40, to Dardanus.[^18] And since it is certain that Christ for that three days is not composed qualitatively of body and soul, in that their union is dissolved, it is clear that then he is neither a mortal man nor an immortal man in this way, because he is not then man in that sense. But if the body and the separated soul are truly each called man, as Hugh of Saint Victor calls them, following Augustine, the scriptures, and philosophical reasons, then the reply must be as above to the objections.[^19] For the soul is man through itself and the body [nuncupatively]{g:nuncupative}; and so the two are not two men, but one only. But if the Master understands "man" collectively, for the two natures joined to one another and to the Word hypostatically, there is no colour [for saying] that he is then man unless each nature is man: and so he would be an immortal man, as the soul was immortal from its origin; and consequently partly mortal, because dead, and partly immortal, because soul. And this is to be granted of any other man.

## ¶9 · 48.29–48.33

> [p. 48] Mansit ergo Christus pro triduo minor patre quidquid fuit ante et sic suppositum humane speciei licet non tunc componebatur qualitative ex anima et corpore. Unde tunc complevit obedienciam Trinitati¹⁰ ministrando secundum naturam assumptam.

Christ therefore remained for the three days less than the Father, whatever he was before, and so a [supposit]{g:suppositum} of the human species, although he was not then composed qualitatively of soul and body.[^20] Hence he then fulfilled obedience to the Trinity, ministering as to the nature [assumed]{g:assumere}.

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** 45.6 *qualitative*: O reads *quiditative*, "quidditatively", from within as its quiddity. A "qualitative part" (45.9) is Wyclif's term for body or soul as the parts that compose a man by kind, against the quantitative parts of the body.
[^2]: **S.** 45.12–13 *qui participative dicuntur dii*: Ps. 81:6, "I have said: You are gods" (John 10:34). *Deo absente ab eis per graciam*: God absent from them in respect of grace, that is, his grace withdrawn; the men remain the same men.
[^3]: **T.** 45.26 *synecdochica*: A B C *sinodica*, O *synodogica*, as at 39.14 (where Harris notes his correction). Harris's emendation is kept against every manuscript because their readings do not construe. The proposition is not a figure naming the whole for the part: the body is Christ in the proper sense.
[^4]: **C.** 45.28–30 *intransitive intelligendo ... ad sensum intransitivum*: "intransitively": the predication does not pass from the subject to another thing, as "man is his essence" says nothing other than the man. *Ut supra*: the earlier treatment in this chapter.
[^5]: **C.** 46.1 *sicut caput denominat hominem capitatum*: the head gives the man the name "headed" (denominative naming, as "white" from whiteness); so the soul makes the Son of God "animate" without animating the dead body.
[^6]: **C.** 46.3–5 *habentem animam, quam per se animat, cum ad invicem consequuntur vires inferiores*: the construal is doubtful. *Habentem* is taken with *personam* ("naming the person as having a soul") and the subject of *animat* as the soul; O reads *quomodo* for *quam* and *mentem* for *invicem*, A B C *consequitur*.
[^7]: **S.** John Damascene, *De fide orthodoxa* III.7: Christ whole (*totus*, the person) in the tomb and in hell, but not the whole (*totum*, not every nature). III.7 has the distinction: Christ is wholly (ὅλος) God and wholly man, but not the whole (ὅλον), the masculine going with the hypostasis, the neuter with the nature (*verified* in the Greek division; Burgundio's Latin not seen). III.27 says only that the one hypostasis stayed with body and soul in death. The tomb and hell are the Lombard's, *Sent.* III d. 22 c. 3, PL 192, 804: *totus erat in sepulcro, totus in inferno, totus ubique … sed non totum* (LEDGER I03e-1).
[^8]: **C.** 46.14 *Sed nullum absolutum*: the verb is understood from *defecit*: only the union, a relation, was lacking, no absolute thing. A B C write *abtū*. *Sentencia* is "teaching" here: the first and second responses.
[^9]: **S.** Rom. 6:9, said in the Vulgate of Christ "rising again from the dead". A B omit *ultra*.
[^10]: **C.** 46.24 *dotem immortalitatis*: the dower (*dos*) of immortality, one of the endowments of the glorified body, given at the resurrection.
[^11]: **C.** 47.2 *licet Christus eternaliter potest mori*: taken literally ("eternally can die"); in the first sense it is the death, not the power of dying, that cannot be repeated.
[^12]: **C.** 47.4–7 *sicut pater semper gignit filium ...*: comparisons of something produced continuously, though complete at every point: begetting, the sun's light, the medium's darkness. *Pater* and *filium* are lower case in the print; the eternal generation of the Son may be meant.
[^13]: **T.** 47.12 *spiritus Iesus*: A B C *Iesu*, "the spirit of Jesus". The print makes the soul Jesus, as Wyclif's position requires; C O omit *pro illo triduo*.
[^14]: **C.** 47.16–19 *Nec est color in isto paralogismo*: "colour", a plausible ground. The conclusion is printed first, then the premisses; "[from]" is supplied. 47.15: A B C read *per hoc instans*.
[^15]: **T.** 47.29 *ex simili modo*: A B C *similitudo*, O *ex silido*. "Paul foolish and damned" is a schoolroom case, not the Apostle.
[^16]: **S.** Peter Lombard, *Sententiae* III d. 22 (whether Christ was a man in the three days). Chapter 1, PL 192, 802–803 (*verified*, LEDGER I03e-2): the Master holds that Christ was truly man in death, *nec mortalis quidem, nec immortalis*, but *non … sic … ut ex anima et carne simul junctis subsisteret*, the sense in which Wyclif says he denies it. **C.** Wyclif narrows the Master: Lombard, *Sent.* III d. 22 c. 1, grants that Christ *vere erat homo* in death and denies only that he subsisted as man from soul and flesh joined, not, as Wyclif puts it, that he was man at all (Bonaventure, Quaracchi III, 452, reads the Master the same way).
[^17]: **T.** 48.7 *philosophice*: B reads *sophistice*, "sophistically". 48.10–11 *sive in corpore sive extra corpus* echoes 2 Cor. 12:2, "whether in the body ... or out of the body".
[^18]: **S.** Augustine's letter to Dardanus, Ep. 187 in the Maurist numbering, on Christ whole everywhere, in the tomb and in hell; "40" is Wyclif's numbering. *Ep.* 187.5, PL 33, 833, *in inferno secundum animum, in sepulcro autem secundum carnem*, with 187.13, PL 33, 836, *in seipso ubique totus* (*verified*, LEDGER I03e-3).
[^19]: **S.** Hugh of Saint Victor, *De sacramentis* II.1.11 (whether Christ was a man in the three days), place not given: PL 176, 407D, *Quid enim magis est homo quam anima? … Ergo, inquis, homo est anima? Quidni?*, with Cicero's *Mens cujusque, is est quisque* (*verified*, LEDGER I03e-4).
[^20]: **C.** 48.29 *minor patre quidquid fuit ante*: Christ remained, for the three days, all that he was before, and so less than the Father as to the humanity. *Patre* is lower case in the print.
