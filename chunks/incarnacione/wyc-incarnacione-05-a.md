# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 5, part 1: that God could not lay aside the assumed nature: the argument from damage

## ¶1 · 65.0–65.0

<!-- the editor's bracketed argument at the chapter head: a Latin summary, then his own English
     summary in the same bracket (see the volume CARD for what is translated) -->

> [p. 65] [Supponendo quod Verbum non dimisit humanitatem pro triduo, probat quod nec potuit dimittere naturam quam assumpsit. Assuming as already proved that the Word did not as a matter of fact in the three days lay aside the human nature which he had taken up; it is here proved to be impossible that he should so lay it aside.]

[Supposing that [the Word]{g:verbum} did not [lay aside]{g:dimittere} [the humanity]{g:humanitas} for [the three days]{g:triduum}, it proves that he could not lay aside the [nature]{g:natura} that he [assumed]{g:assumere} either.]

## ¶2 · 65.18–65.20

> [p. 65] Viso¹¹ quod Verbum non dimisit de facto in sancto triduo corpus vel animam: restat ostendere quod Deus non potuit dimittere naturam hominis quam assumpsit.

Since it has been seen that the Word did not in fact, in the holy three days, lay aside body or soul, it remains to [show]{g:ostendere} that God could not lay aside the nature of man that he assumed.

## ¶3 · 65.21–68.4

> [p. 65] Probatur tripliciter. Primo sic. Deus non potest dampnificare naturam aliquam sine suo demerito¹² precedente: sed [p. 66] si dimitteret naturam assumptam, eam sic dampnificaret: ergo et cetera.¹ Maior patet ex hoc, quod dampnificacio est creature racionali² mali illacio inutilis,² que non potest sibi inexistere nisi oriatur principaliter a se ipsa, iuxta sentenciam beati Iohannis Chrisostomi et aliorum doctorum communiter. Confirmatur. Si Deus aufert creature racionali bonum aliquod, iustum et pulcrum est illud auferri et per consequens non sonat³ in malum inutile: et sic⁴ servata immunitate peccati non est dampnum. Nam si Deus dampnificat hominem, quia facit eum iuste carere bono possibili; tunc infinitum multum dampnificaret quamlibet beatificabilem creaturam; et, ut communiter deducitur, infinitum impediret quodlibet opus suum. 2°. Confirmatur⁵ ex hoc, quod infliccio⁶ pene perpetue pro peccato non dampnificat sed meliorat dampnatum in tantum quod, si esset in eleccione⁷ sua sic dampnari post peccatum vel vacare⁸ impunem,⁹ eligeret racionabiliter sic dampnari: ergo a maiori vel pari, si Deus aufert ab aliquo¹⁰ sine suo demerito¹⁰ quodvis bonum, cum sit pulcrum et bonum quod ita faciat nedum toti universitati¹¹ sed persone, que aliter frustratorie¹² occuparet; sequitur quod talis ablacio non sit dampnificacio; cum nec pro illa ablacione nec pro eius causa racionabiliter¹³ sit dolendum. Ex quo¹⁴ videtur 3°. quod innocens hoc videns eligeret¹⁵ racionabiliter sic privari: et cum nemo¹⁶ potest racionabiliter eligere¹⁶ dampnificari, sequitur quod Deus non dampnificat creaturam, sed solum ipsamet¹⁷ seipsam¹⁸ vel aliud alliciens ad peccandum. Ymo si bene consideretur, omnis dampnificacio sapit iniuriacionem, que non potest per se bono et iusto competere, quin pocius bonificare et corrigere. Minor autem argumenti sic [p. 67] probatur. Humanitas assumpta a Verbo non potest demereri previe suam dimissionem: ergo, illa dimissa, hoc sibi contingeret sine suo demerito. Antecedens patet per hoc, quod non potest demereri stante unione, quia tunc Verbum peccaret: et si pro instanti dissolucionis vel post peccaret, patet quod demeritum [dimissionem] sequetur;¹ et per consequens non dampnificaretur ex suo demerito precedente. Quod autem dimissio² foret dampnosa humanitati Christi ex hoc evidet, quod deperdicio gracie unionis foret deperdicio maximi boni possibilis intrinseci creature:³ et per consequens tum⁴ loco illius non succederet tantum bonum: sequitur quod humanitas ipsam perdens foret notabiliter peiorata, et per consequens dampnificata. Valorem autem ypostatice unionis suppono⁵ ex dictis et dicendis: per illam enim fuit homo assumptus idem personaliter Verbo Dei. Magna ergo peioracio sua foret quod dimitteretur sine suo demerito tanquam extranea. Non enim pertinet ad dictum Cesarem semper Augustum⁶ sic peiorare sine suo demerito carissimam⁷ creaturam: quia indubie creatura dimissa pro instanti dimissionis posset racionabiliter conqueri de ingratitudine Dei sui, cum continue ante fecit quidquid debuit, et finaliter reciperet pro ‘retribucione⁸ mercedis’ tantum dampnum.⁸ Non ergo foret spes⁹ in tali Deo. Et hinc nimirum¹⁰ dicit Augustinus Ome. 47: ‘Absit¹¹ quod Christus pro triduo animam suam dimiserit:¹² ipsam enim inseparabiliter habuit:’ unde indubie, si post peccaret, dimissio gracie unionis preservantis ne peccet foret causa principalis peccati: quia si affirmacio sit causa affirmacionis, negacio est causa negacionis, ut absencia naute est causa periclitacionis navis ex 2° Physicorum. Illud ergo peccatum, ex dimissione originatum, esset Deo principaliter imputandum, cum non ex parte creature, sed Dei, causatur talis absencia: talis¹³ ergo [p. 68] Deus excederet in malicia deum Manicheorum,¹ qui sine² demerito servi sui absentaret se pro temptacionis tempore non iuvando. Unde et illam racionem tangit sanctus Thomas De Christo,³ articulo 70.⁴

It is proved in three ways. First thus. God cannot [damage]{g:dampnificare} any nature without its own preceding demerit: now, [66] if he were to lay aside the assumed nature, he would thus damage it: therefore etc.[^1] The major is clear from this, that [damaging]{g:dampnificacio} is, for a rational [creature]{g:creatura}, the useless [bringing-on]{g:illacio} of evil, which cannot be present in it unless it arises principally from itself, according to the [teaching]{g:sentencia} of blessed John Chrysostom and of the other doctors commonly.[^2] This is confirmed. If God takes away some good from a rational creature, it is just and beautiful that it be taken away, and consequently it does not amount to useless evil: and so, immunity from sin being preserved, it is no loss. For if God damages a man because he makes him justly lack a possible good, then he would damage every beatifiable creature infinitely much; and, as is commonly deduced, he would infinitely hinder each of his works. Second, this is confirmed from this, that the infliction of perpetual punishment for sin does not damage but betters the damned man, to such a degree that, if it were in his choice to be thus damned after sin or to remain unpunished, he would reasonably choose to be thus damned: therefore, from the greater or from the equal, if God takes away any good whatever from anyone without his demerit, since it is beautiful and good that he so act, [not only]{g:nedum} for the whole universe but for the person, who otherwise would hold [it] to no purpose, [it follows]{g:sequitur} that such a [taking-away]{g:ablacio} is not damaging, since neither for that taking-away nor for its cause is one reasonably to grieve.[^3] From which, third, it seems that an innocent seeing this would reasonably choose to be thus deprived: and since no one can reasonably choose to be damaged, it follows that God does not damage a creature, but only [the creature] itself [damages] itself, or another enticing [it] to sin.[^4] Indeed, if it is well considered, all damaging savours of wronging, which cannot [of itself]{g:per-se} belong to one who is good and just, but rather to [make good]{g:bonificare} and to correct. Now the minor of the argument is [67] proved thus. The humanity assumed by the Word cannot [demerit]{g:demereri} its [laying-aside]{g:dimissio} beforehand: therefore, were it laid aside, this would befall it without its demerit. The [antecedent]{g:antecedens} is clear by this, that it cannot demerit while the [union]{g:unio} stands, for then the Word would sin: and if it sinned at the instant of the dissolution or after, it is clear that the demerit will follow [the laying-aside]; and consequently it would not be damaged from its own preceding demerit.[^5] And that the laying-aside would be damaging to the humanity of Christ is evident from this, that the loss of [the grace of union]{g:gracia-unionis} would be the loss of the greatest possible good intrinsic to a creature: and consequently no so great a good would then succeed in its place: it follows that the humanity losing it would be notably made worse, and consequently damaged.[^6] And the worth of the hypostatic union I suppose from what has been said and is to be said: for through it [the assumed man]{g:homo-assumptus} was personally the same as the Word of God. Great, therefore, would be its [worsening]{g:peioracio}, that it should be laid aside without its demerit as a stranger. For it does not belong to the said Caesar ever August thus to make worse, without its demerit, his dearest creature: for undoubtedly the creature laid aside could, at the instant of the laying-aside, reasonably complain of the [ingratitude]{g:ingratitudo} of its God, since continually before it did whatever it ought, and at the last would receive, for "recompense of reward", so great a loss.[^7] There would therefore be no beauty in such a God.[^8] And hence, no wonder, Augustine says, Homily 47: "Far be it that Christ for the three days laid aside his soul: for he had it inseparably":[^9] hence undoubtedly, if it sinned afterward, the laying-aside of the grace of union, which preserves [it] from sinning, would be the principal cause of the sin: for if an affirmation is the cause of an affirmation, a negation is the cause of a negation, as the absence of the pilot is the cause of the ship's peril, from the second [book] of the *Physics*.[^10] That sin, therefore, originating from the laying-aside, would be principally imputable to God, since such an absence is caused not on the creature's side but on God's: such a [68] God, therefore, would exceed in malice the god of the Manichees, who without the demerit of his servant would absent himself for the time of temptation, not helping. Hence St Thomas also touches that argument, *On Christ*, article 50.[^11]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** 65.21–22 *dampnificare*: "damage", to do harm to; kept apart from *dampnum* "loss" (66.9), *dampnosa* "damaging" (67.8) and *dampnari* "be damned" (66.16). The argument: God harms no nature without its prior demerit; laying aside the assumed nature would harm it; so he cannot lay it aside. B *merito* for *demerito* (65.22).
[^2]: **S.** Chrysostom: the teaching that no one is harmed except by himself, the treatise *Quod nemo laeditur nisi a seipso*, whose thesis (§1, restated in §17) is that no one who is wronged is wronged by another, only by himself (*verified* in sense, in the NPNF translation; the work circulated in Latin; LEDGER I05a-1). At 66.3 O reads *inutilis illacio* and omits *mali*.
[^3]: **C.** 66.17–23: *a maiori vel pari*, the topic from the greater or the equal: if punishment betters even the damned man, who deserved it, the taking-away of a good from one who did not deserve it is still less a damaging. *Frustratorie occuparet*: the person would hold the good to no purpose; "[it]" is supplied. *Sine suo demerito* (66.18–19) is wanting in A B C; O *universo* for *universitati* (66.20).
[^4]: **C.** 66.27 *ipsamet seipsam*: the creature itself is the only one that harms itself, or another (the devil, or a fellow) who entices it to sin. A B C *ipsam* for *ipsamet*; O omits *seipsam*. At 66.25 A B C read *sic (sit B) racionabiliter eligeret* for *potest racionabiliter eligere*.
[^5]: **T.** 67.6 "[the laying-aside]": Harris's own bracket *[dimissionem]*, from A's margin (*suple dimissione*); O *sequitur* for *sequetur*. A sin at or after the dissolution would follow the laying-aside, and so could not be the demerit that preceded it.
[^6]: **C.** 67.9 *evidet*, as printed, "is evident". 67.11 *tum*, wanting in O, is taken as "then"; it may stand for *cum*, which would make the clause a reason ("since no so great a good would succeed"); the sense is the same. O *a creatura* for *creature* (67.10).
[^7]: **S.** 67.22 "recompense of reward": Heb. 2:2 (DR "received a just recompense of reward"), Harris's margin. A B C read *mercedis tantum premium* ("so great a prize") and O *mercedem*; the foot does not make plain whether O keeps *tantum dampnum*, and the print is kept. 67.17–18 *Cesarem semper Augustum*, the imperial style "Caesar ever August", is given to God; O *augustutum*. *Carissimam*: B and O abbreviate, O's read by Harris as *beatissimam* (67.18).
[^8]: **T.** 67.23 *species* "beauty", with B O, against the print's *spes* "hope": "there would be no hope in such a God". B and O agree across the families, and their reading construes: such a God would lack the beauty claimed for his acts (*pulcrum*, 66.7, 66.19). The two words share an abbreviation, so the print's word may be Harris's expansion.
[^9]: **S.** Augustine, "Homily 47": *Tract. in Ioh.* 47 (on John 10:17–18), the place Wyclif cites by homily throughout; 47.10, PL 35, 1738: *Fidelem latronis animam non deserebat, et deserebat suam? Absit: sed illius ut Dominus custodivit, suam vero inseparabiliter habuit* (*verified*, LEDGER I05a-2). Here Wyclif's number is the received one; *pro triduo* is his. A *mimirum*; O omits *absit* and reads *dimiseret*.
[^10]: **S.** Aristotle, *Physics* II.3 (195a11–14): the same thing is cause of contraries, the pilot's presence of the ship's safety and his absence of its wreck (*verified*, LEDGER I05a-3; Harris quotes the Greek).
[^11]: **S/T.** 68.3–4 Thomas, "*De Christo*, article 50", with O and the source, against the print's *70*. The question whether the Godhead was parted from Christ's flesh and soul in death is *ST* III q. 50 aa. 2–3, which bears out O's number: a. 2 co., *id quod per gratiam Dei conceditur, nunquam absque culpa revocatur … Cum igitur in Christo nullum fuerit peccatum, impossibile fuit quod solveretur unio divinitatis a carne ipsius* (*verified*, LEDGER I05a-4). Harris quotes the same sentence in his note but prints *70*. C *Aquino* for *Christo*.
