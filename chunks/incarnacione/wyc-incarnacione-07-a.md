# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 7, part 1: that Christ is movable: wisdom more active than all active things

## ¶1 · 105.0–105.0

<!-- the editor's bracketed argument at the chapter head: a Latin summary, then his own English
     summary in the same bracket (see the volume CARD for what is translated) -->

> [p. 105] [Declarando Christi mobilitatem explanat sensum scripture multis ambiguum, quomodo sapiencia summe mobilis sit habitu inventa ut homo. The sense in which Christ is subject to change (mobilis) is defined by discussing Wisdom vii. 24, ‘Wisdom is more moving (mobilior) than any motion,’ and Philipp. ii. 8, ‘found in fashion (habitu) as a man.’]

[In [making clear]{g:declarare} Christ's [movability]{g:mobilitas} it explains a sense of scripture that is ambiguous to many: how Wisdom, supremely movable, was "in habit found as a man".]

## ¶2 · 105.11–107.7

> [p. 105] Secundo principaliter arguitur non esse concedendum quod Christus sit mobilis. Nam si Christus sit mobilis, tunc Deus est mobilis: sed nullus Deus est mobilis:³ ergo nec Christus. Minor sic arguitur.⁴ Aliqua res est immobilis: sed⁵ nulla, si non Deus: ergo Deus est immobilis:⁵ et cum unus Deus sit omnis Deus, sequitur quod omnis Deus et per consequens omnis⁶ persona divina sit simpliciter immobilis, et per consequens nulla est mobilis.⁷ Si ergo Deus sit mobilis, et omne aliud a Deo sit⁸ mobile, sequitur quod omne ens⁹ sit mobile, et sic nullum immobile. Ex quo sequitur quod Christus non sit passus, mortuus, aut cum hominibus conversatus, cum tunc esset destructibilis¹⁰ et adnichilabilis. [p. 106] Hic dicitur quod necesse est aliquam rem esse omnino immobilem; quod patet tripliciter. Primo ex hoc quod omne motum oportet inniti alicui fixo immoto: universitas¹ creata est mota: ergo oportet illam inniti alicui fixo immoto:¹ sicut² ergo mixta terrestria innituntur orbi terre, qui non commovebitur secundum centrum fluctuans huc aut illuc, sic omne motum cum sit ordinatum sic moveri ab alio oportet stare in suis limitibus secundum³ ordinacionem principaliter⁴ ordinantis: quia cum moveri sit imperfeccionis et posse movere perfeccionis simpliciter, patet quod prius est pura perfeccio quam imperfeccio exemplata. 2°. patet idem ex hoc quod veritates multe eterne, tam affirmaciones quam negaciones, sunt omnino immobiles; ut ‘quod nichil simul est et non est’; ‘quod multa encia possunt esse,’ etc.⁵ Cum ergo omnem rem racionis oportet fundari in absoluta essencia, sequitur quod oportet dare essenciam vel naturam immobilem, cui innixe predicte⁶ veritates immobilitatem suscipiunt. 3°. patet idem ex hoc quod illa prima natura est in fine perfeccionis possibilis, quia aliter non esset summe perfecta; sed si posset moveri, posset perfici: ergo moveri non poterit. Si autem⁷ moveri posset,⁸ hoc foret potissime obiective: sed eternaliter ordinat omnia, cum non capit suam speculacionem aut⁹ praxim a rebus extra: ergo non movetur¹⁰ a suis effectibus obiective; et multo minus localiter,¹¹ augmentative, vel alterative: aliter enim esset in eo¹² passiva potencia ante actum mixta cum potencia activa, cum aliud sit posse movere et aliud posse moveri: et sic varie posset¹³ perfici et imperfici, quod non competit summe bono. Supposito ergo, quod sit aliqua res et aliquid omnino immobile; consequenter dicendum est quod necesse est quidquid, quod poterit¹⁴ esse,¹⁴ esse mobile. Patet sic. Non est possibile aliquid esse nisi illud sit¹⁵ essencia creata [p. 107] vel essencia¹ increata; omnis essencia creata est mobilis; omnis essencia increata est mobile: ergo conclusio. Minor patet ex hoc quod essencia increata est suppositum mobile:² ergo illa est mobile: consequencia patet, quia ipsa est res mobilis³ eo quod est persona Verbi, que moveri poterit, et nedum hoc, sed habet naturam sibi⁴ unitam secundum quam moveri sufficit.

Secondly, [as a main head]{g:principaliter}, it is argued that "Christ is movable" is not to be granted. For if Christ is movable, then God is movable: now no God is movable: therefore neither is Christ. The minor is argued thus. Some thing is [immovable]{g:immobilis}: now none is, if not God: therefore God is immovable: and since one God is every God, it follows that every God, and consequently every divine [person]{g:persona}, is immovable [without qualification]{g:simpliciter}, and consequently none is movable. If, then, God is movable, and everything other than God is movable, it follows that every [being]{g:ens} is movable, and so none immovable. From which it follows that Christ did not suffer, die, or [live his life]{g:conversari} among men, since he would then be destructible and annihilable. [106] Here the answer is that it is necessary that some thing be altogether immovable; which is clear in three ways. First from this, that everything moved must rest on something fixed and unmoved: [the created totality]{g:universitas-creata} is moved: therefore it must rest on something fixed and unmoved:[^1] as, then, the mixed bodies of the earth rest on the globe of the earth, which "shall not be moved",[^2] fluctuating about its centre this way or that, so everything moved, since it is ordered to be so moved by another, must stand within its [limits]{g:limites} according to the [ordinance]{g:ordinacio} of him who orders principally: for since to be moved belongs to imperfection, and to be able to move to perfection without qualification, it is clear that pure perfection is prior to exemplified imperfection. Secondly, the same is clear from this, that many eternal truths, affirmations as well as negations, are altogether immovable; as "that nothing at once is and is not"; "that many beings can be", etc. Since, then, every [thing of reason]{g:res-racionis} must be grounded in absolute [essence]{g:essencia}, it follows that one must posit an immovable essence or [nature]{g:natura}, resting on which the aforesaid truths receive their [immovability]{g:immobilitas}. Thirdly, the same is clear from this, that that first nature is at the end of possible perfection, for otherwise it would not be supremely perfect; now if it could be moved, it could be perfected: therefore it cannot be moved. And if it could be moved, this would be above all objectively: now it orders all things eternally, since it does not take its speculation or its practice from things outside: therefore it is not moved objectively by its effects; and much less locally, by augmentation, or by alteration: for otherwise there would be in it a passive [potency]{g:potencia}[^3] before the act, mixed with active potency, since to be able to move is one thing and to be able to be moved another: and so it could in various ways be perfected and made imperfect, which does not befit the supreme good. Supposing, then, that there is some thing, and something altogether immovable, it must next be said that it is necessary that whatever can be is movable. This is clear thus. It is not possible that anything be unless it is created essence [107] or uncreated essence; every created essence is movable; every uncreated essence is [a movable]{g:mobile}:[^4] therefore the conclusion. The minor is clear from this, that uncreated essence is a movable [supposit]{g:suppositum}: therefore it is a movable: the [inference]{g:consequencia} is clear, for it is a movable thing because it is the person of the [Word]{g:verbum}, which can be moved; and [not only]{g:nedum} this, but he has a nature united to him, as to which he is able to be moved.[^5]

## ¶3 · 107.8–108.21

> [p. 107] Et sic intelligo illud⁵ Sapiencie 7°, ‘omnibus mobilibus est mobilior sapiencia’: quod dictum, ut michi videtur, potest catholice intelligi et vere ad litteram de Sapiencia eterna⁶ suo tempore incarnata; que, cum attrita⁷ sit secundum humanitatem assumptam propter scelera nostra, bonificans et vivificans omnia ultra hoc quod sufficeret vel⁸ poterit⁸ alia creatura, habuit nimirum mocionem fontalem, qua principiat quasi vita omnem mocionem mundi sensibilis et cuiuslibet sue partis. Sicut ergo Aristoteles 8° Physicorum: ‘Ymaginatur motum primi mobilis esse quasi vitam viventibus cum mediante⁹ illo influitur perfeccio mundo supposito’; sic verissime sine ficticia illa Sapiencia est primum mobile efficacia et dignitate secundum assumptum hominem, mediante cuius motu primo omnium¹⁰ totus mundus ante et post perficitur; cum quelibet alia creatura per Christi passionem ad perfeccionem primariam,¹¹ qua Deo serviret placato¹² et¹² homini, instauratur. Non quod propter devocionem finguntur falsa; sed quod verissime de¹³ vi sermonis secundum seriem verborum primum¹³ mobile sit sapiencia increata; et quod eius motus vel passio perficit *[hominem] plus quam motus celi¹⁴ quamlibet creaturam¹⁵ [aliam] post vel ante. Nam¹⁶ quelibet creatura [p. 108] citra hominem debet ex institucione primaria servire homini; et sic peccato primi hominis, a quo hodie¹ redundanter² haberet perfeccionem ipso stante in originali iusticia, quodammodo peioratur. Cum ergo, per passionem et per consequens per mocionem huius Sapiencie, facta plena satisfaccione,³ sit natura humana gloriosius restituta; patet quod⁴ ista passio rectificat omnem operacionem nature, que humano generi est subiecta: et cum omnis⁵ servitus post et ante facta humano generi, non interveniente ista redempcione, foret quodammodo cassata, patet quod compendiosius motu celi vivificat⁶ preterita⁶ et futura: et cum exinde dolus et peccatum demonum mitigatur, lectum⁷ angelorum consorcium augmentatur, patet quod ultra possibilitatem motus celi spiritualem bonificat creaturam. Nec mirum; quia motor⁸ intrinsecus huius⁹ motus fuit non intelligencia, motrix orbis, sed spiritus¹⁰ preciosissimus¹⁰ Filii hominis et Dei Filii naturalis, qui est ‘tanto melior angelis effectus, quanto differencius pre illis nomen hereditavit;’ ut dicit Apostolus ad Hebre. primo ca°, quando dicit quod ‘Deus proposuit instaurare in Christo omnia, que in celis et que in terra sunt’; ut exponit Gregorius 31° Moralium ca° 38.

And so I understand that [text] of Wisdom 7, "wisdom is more active than all active things":[^6] which saying, as it seems to me, can be understood in a catholic way and truly to the letter of the eternal Wisdom incarnate in his time; who, since he was "bruised" as to the [assumed]{g:assumere} [humanity]{g:humanitas} "for our sins",[^7] [making good]{g:bonificare} and quickening all things beyond what would suffice, or another [creature]{g:creatura} could, had, no wonder, a fontal motion, by which he originates, as a kind of life, every motion of the [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} world and of each of its parts. As, then, Aristotle in Physics 8: "He imagines the motion of [the first movable]{g:primum-mobile} to be as it were a life to living things, since by means of it perfection [flows into]{g:influere} the world set beneath";[^8] so most truly, without [fiction]{g:ficticia}, that Wisdom is the first movable in efficacy and dignity as to [the assumed man]{g:homo-assumptus}, by means of whose motion, the first of all,[^9] the whole world before and after is perfected; since every other creature is restored through Christ's passion to the primary perfection, by which it would serve God, once appeased, and man.[^10] Not that false things are feigned for devotion's sake; but that most truly, by the force of the words,[^11] according to the order of the words, uncreated wisdom is the first movable; and that his motion or passion perfects any [other] creature, after or before, more than the motion of the heaven [does].[^12] For every creature [108] short of man ought by its primary institution to serve man; and so by the sin of the first man, from whom it would have had perfection overflowingly had he stood in original justice, it is in a manner [made worse]{g:peiorare}.[^13] Since, then, through the passion, and consequently through the motion, of this Wisdom, full [satisfaction]{g:satisfaccio} having been made, human nature has been more gloriously restored, it is clear that this passion sets right every operation of nature that is subject to the human race: and since all [servitude]{g:servitus} rendered to the human race after and before would, had this redemption not intervened, have been in a manner made void, it is clear that he quickens things past and future more compendiously than the motion of the heaven: and since thereby the guile and sin of the demons is mitigated, [and] the chosen fellowship of the angels is increased, it is clear that beyond the possibility of the motion of the heaven he makes good the spiritual creature. Nor is it any wonder; for the intrinsic mover of this motion was not an intelligence, [mover]{g:motrix} of an orb, but the most precious spirit of the Son of man and natural Son of God, who is "made so much better than the angels as he hath inherited a more excellent name than they", as the Apostle says in Hebrews, chapter 1, when he says that "God purposed to re-establish in Christ all things that are in heaven and on earth", as Gregory expounds in Moralia 31, chapter 38.[^14]

## ¶4 · 108.22–109.20

> [p. 108] Sic ergo intelligendo per Sapienciam personaliter Verbum Dei, ut docent Augustinus et Ieronimus,¹¹ concedendum est quod ipsa sit summe mobilis; ymo plus attendendo ad litteram concedi potest quod dicta Sapiencia nedum fuit plus mobilis, sed plus effectualiter movebatur quam alia creatura quoad velocitatem, quoad compendiositatem, et quoad generalitatem. Quoad velocitatem, quia pro instanti sue generacionis plena carismatum; post verisimiliter in sua ascensione movebatur recte tam velociter ut primum mobile circulariter,¹² vel ut est possibile quod aliquid moveatur, quia causa tardacionis post¹³ discipulorum intuitum non est [p. 109] faciliter fingenda: Christus ergo ex se ascendendo a limbo ad celum ultimum pertransiit velocissime longissimum spacium transmeabile. Quoad compendiositatem patet quod eius brevis passio reduxit totum mundum post et ante ad maius temperamentum quam motus perpetuus primi mobilis suffecisset: prima enim secundum Aristotelem sunt quantitate minima. Quoad generalitatem patet, cum Christus sit creatura corporea et creatura incorporea, quarum utraque¹ multipliciter movebatur, quod sit ceteris plus mobilis: movebatur quidem² generacione et morte, augmentacione et alteracione, et demum motu locali multiplici super terram, aquam, et aerem³ pulsione, traccione, veccione et vertigine, ut actu illo imperfecto reduceret suos ad quietem perpetuam. Unde sicut naturalis philosophus commendat motum tanquam signum efficax nedum ad philosophandum sed ad intentum nature perficiendum, sic nimirum commendat theologus voluntariam passionem. Et constat⁴ ex 3° Physicorum quod passio vel est motus vel accidens multum cognatum motui. Sic ergo verissime de virtute sermonis dicitur ‘Sapiencia’ Dei Patris ‘omnibus aliis creaturis mobilior.’

So, then, understanding by Wisdom, personally, the Word of God, as Augustine and Jerome teach,[^15] it is to be granted that he is supremely movable; indeed, attending more to the letter, it can be granted that the said Wisdom not only was more movable, but was more effectually moved than another creature, as to speed, as to compendiousness, and as to generality. As to speed, because at the instant of his generation [he was] full of [charisms]{g:carismata}; afterwards, in all likelihood, in his ascension he was moved in a straight line as swiftly as the first movable [is moved] circularly, or as swiftly as it is possible that anything be moved, since a cause of delay after [he passed from] the disciples' sight is not [109] easily to be feigned: Christ, then, ascending of himself from limbo to the last heaven, passed most swiftly through the longest space that can be traversed. As to compendiousness, it is clear that his brief passion brought back the whole world, after and before, to a greater temperament than the perpetual motion of the first movable would have sufficed [to do]: for first things, according to Aristotle, are least in quantity.[^16] As to generality, it is clear, since Christ is a corporeal creature and an incorporeal creature, each of which was moved in many ways, that he is more movable than the rest: he was moved indeed by generation and death, by augmentation and alteration, and lastly by manifold local motion over land, water, and air, by pushing, pulling, carrying and whirling,[^17] so that by that imperfect act he might bring his own back to perpetual rest. Hence, just as the natural philosopher commends motion as an efficacious [sign]{g:signum}, not only for philosophising but for accomplishing the intent of nature, so, no wonder, the theologian commends the voluntary passion. And it is evident from Physics 3 that passion either is motion or is an accident closely akin to motion.[^18] So, then, most truly, [in the proper sense of the words]{g:de-virtute-sermonis}, the "Wisdom" of God the Father is said [to be] "more movable than all other creatures".

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** *Universitas creata ... fixo immoto* (p. 106), the minor premiss and conclusion of this first proof, is O's alone; A B C omit it.
[^2]: **S.** Ps. 92:1, *orbem terre, qui non commovebitur* (Douay "the world which shall not be moved"), applied to the globe of the earth.
[^3]: **C.** *Potencia* inside Aristotle's analysis of active and passive potency, so "potency", the first sense of the contextual row.
[^4]: **C.** *Mobile* (neuter) with a feminine subject, *essencia increata*, is a substantive: "a movable", a movable thing, against the adjective *mobilis* agreeing with *essencia creata*. The chapter returns to the difference (pp. 116–117: "taking 'movable' essentially, as a substantive").
[^5]: **T.** A B C omit *essencia* before *increata*, *mobile* after *suppositum*, *mobilis* after *res*, and *sibi* (p. 107); the print follows O, without which the proof of the minor reads "uncreated essence is a supposit ... it is a thing".
[^6]: **G.** Douay's "active" renders *mobilis, mobilior*, which the rest of the chapter gives as "movable"; Wyclif omits the Vulgate's *enim* (Wis. 7:24). *Sapiencia* is here the Word in person (108.22), so the pronoun is "he", as for *Verbum*, though the Latin is feminine.
[^7]: **S.** Isa. 53:5, *attritus est propter scelera nostra* (Douay "he was bruised for our sins").
[^8]: **S.** Aristotle, *Physics* VIII.1 (250b14), motion "as a kind of life" to all things constituted by nature; Wyclif gives a paraphrase, not the text (LEDGER I07a-1).
[^9]: **C.** *Motu primo omnium*: taken as "whose motion, the first of all [motions]"; it can also be read adverbially, "by whose motion, first of all". A B C read *omni* for *omnium*.
[^10]: **T.** The print's *placato et* (107.24) stands in no manuscript as printed: A B C read *et placito*, O *placato* without *et*. The witnesses do not agree against it, and it is kept.
[^11]: **G.** *De vi sermonis* is neither *de virtute sermonis* "in the proper sense of the words" nor *de vi vocis*; rendered "by the force of the words". A has *aut* before *secundum seriem* (in an erasure), B *sermonis sed causacionem verborum*, and O omits *vi* (p. 107).
[^12]: **T.** The print reads *perficit [hominem] plus quam motus celi quamlibet creaturam [aliam]* (107.28–29). *Hominem* is the editor's conjecture, in no manuscript; without it the sentence construes, and it is set aside. *Aliam* is O's, bracketed by the editor, and is kept. For *celi* O has an abbreviation the editor takes for *ceteri*; *celi* recurs at 108.11–14.
[^13]: **T.** *Hodie* (108.2) is omitted by C and O, against A B; with C O, across the families, "today" is not rendered.
[^14]: **S.** Heb. 1:4; the second quotation is Eph. 1:9–10 (Douay "to re-establish all things in Christ, that are in heaven and on earth"), given in Wyclif's word order. Gregory, *Moralia* XXXI.38 (LEDGER I07a-2).
[^15]: **S.** Augustine and Jerome on Wisdom as the Word in person; no work is named (LEDGER I07a-3).
[^16]: **S.** The maxim *principium est minimum quantitate, maximum virtute*: Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations* 34 (183b22) (LEDGER I07a-4).
[^17]: **S.** The four kinds of motion by another, pushing, pulling, carrying and whirling: Aristotle, *Physics* VII.2 (243a16 ff.) (LEDGER I07a-5).
[^18]: **C.** *Passio* is both Christ's passion and Aristotle's being-acted-on, which *Physics* III.3 makes one motion with action (LEDGER I07a-6); the sentence needs both, so "passion" carries both.
