# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 7, part 4: the Word suffers, and the six senses of "habit"

## ¶1 · 117.28–118.8

> [p. 117] Hic dicitur quod non esset concedendum Verbum pati propter passionem sue humanitatis, nisi Verbum esset personaliter illa humanitas. Sed quia Verbum ex integro est illa humanitas tam secundum corpus quam secundum animam, et acciones ac passiones sunt primo suppositorum; patet quod illa humanitas non pateretur vel ageret,⁹ nisi [p. 118] quia Verbum, quod est eadem ypostasis vel persona, sic agit vel patitur. Firmiter itaque est tenendum quod Verbum Dei et per consequens Deus ipse pendebat dolens et passus in cruce, et sic de ceteris humanis actibus quos evangelium de Iesu prosequitur. Et sic, licet humanitas Christi sit essencia, substancia, vel natura separata non conservative sed essencialiter a deitate, que Christus est; non tamen sic separatur quin sit persona Christi.

Here [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that it would not be granted that [the Word]{g:verbum} suffers because of the suffering of his [humanity]{g:humanitas}, unless the Word were personally that humanity. Now since the Word is wholly that humanity, as much as to the body as as to the soul, and actions and [being-acted-on]{g:passio} belong first to [supposits]{g:suppositum};[^1] it is clear that that humanity would not suffer or act, unless [118] because the Word, who is the same [hypostasis]{g:ypostasis} or [person]{g:persona}, so acts or suffers. So it must be firmly held that the Word of God, and in consequence God himself, hung grieving and suffering on the cross; and so of the other human acts which the gospel recounts of Jesus. And so, although the humanity of Christ is an [essence]{g:essencia}, substance, or [nature]{g:natura} separate from [the Godhead]{g:deitas}, which Christ is, not as to conservation but essentially, yet it is not so separated that it is not the person of Christ.[^2]

## ¶2 · 118.9–118.30

> [p. 118] Sed pro nomine ‘habitus’ est notandum quod ‘habitus’ quadrupliciter sumitur, ut exponit Augustinus 83 Questionum Questione 73ᵃ. exponendo illud Apostoli ad Philipp. 2°. ‘habitu inventus est ut homo.’ Primo pro sapiencia, que est veritas, quam addiscimus, que manens¹ non mota movet animam, quam informat. 2°. pro alimentis habitis mutantibus corpus, quod nutriunt, et digestione mutatis. Omne quidem substanciale nobis adiacens, quod mutat et mutatur adiacendo subiecto, dicitur ‘habitus’ huiusmodi²: ‘habitus’² quidem, quia denominat subiectum habere quod sibi accidentaliter adiacet. 3ᵐ. genus est quando habituata formantur a subiectis habituatis³ †perfeccius quam quando membris aptata induuntur.†³ 4ᵐ. genus habitus, ut anulus in digito. Omnia autem hec quatuor, cum⁴ denominant substanciam habituari vel habere, dicuntur in predicacione secundum essenciam esse⁵ ‘habitus;’ ut ‘motus’ dicitur materialiter esse res, que est motu dicto formaliter acquisita, ut patet 3°. Physicorum 9°· 4°. Sumitur autem sufficiencia illorum quatuor penes hoc, quod primus habitus mutat substanciam cui advenit et⁶ non mutatur; secundus⁷ mutat et mutatur⁷; tercius mutatur et non mutat; quartus nec mutat necessario nec mutatur.

But concerning the name "[habit]{g:habitus}" it is to be noted that "habit" is taken in four ways, as Augustine expounds in the *Eighty-Three Questions*, question 73, expounding that word of the Apostle to the Philippians 2, "in habit he was found as a man" (Phil 2:7).[^3] First, for wisdom, which is the truth that we learn, which, remaining unmoved, moves the soul that it informs. Second, for the foods we have, which change the body they nourish and are changed by digestion. Indeed every substantial thing lying beside us which changes and is changed by lying beside its [subject]{g:subiectum} is called a "habit" of this kind: a "habit", that is, because it [names]{g:denominare} the subject as having what lies beside it accidentally. The third genus is when the things worn are formed by the subjects that wear them, †more perfectly than when, fitted to the limbs, they are put on.†[^4] The fourth genus of habit, as a ring on the finger. And all these four, since they name a substance as being habited or as having, are said in [predication by essence]{g:predicacio-secundum-essenciam} to be "habits"; as "motion" is said, taken materially, to be the thing which is acquired by motion taken formally, as is clear from *Physics* III, 9, 4.[^5] And the sufficiency of those four is taken according to this: that the first habit changes the substance to which it comes and is not changed; the second changes and is changed; the third is changed and does not change; the fourth neither necessarily changes nor is changed.

## ¶3 · 118.31–119.10

> [p. 118] Sed preter hec quatuor, que⁸ in se sunt⁸ substancie, sumitur habitus apud philosophos formaliter ad duos⁹ sensus [p. 119] equivocos. Primo pro qualitate preternaturali¹ de prima specie qualitatis; sive² sit² corporis habitus, ut sanitas; sive habitus anime, ut virtus intellectualis aut moralis³; habitus autem spiritualis ex habitu primo modo dicto³ materialiter generatur, sicut⁴ habitus corporalis efficitur ex secundo. Sed secundo modo sumitur habitus pro forma respectiva de 10°. genere, quod est habere, possessio, vel habicio, et talis habitus causatur ab habitu tercio et quarto modis materialiter intellectis: ‘divicie’ enim denominant homines divites,⁵ possessionatos, vel habentes formaliter.

But besides these four, which in themselves are substances, "habit" is taken among the philosophers formally in two [119] equivocal senses. First, for a preternatural quality of the first species of quality;[^6] whether it is a habit of the body, as health, or a habit of the soul, as an intellectual or moral [virtue]{g:virtus}; and the spiritual habit is generated materially from habit said in the first way, as the bodily habit is produced from the second. But in the second way "habit" is taken for a [relational]{g:respectivus} form of the tenth genus, which is having, possession, or [holding]{g:habicio};[^7] and such a habit is caused by habit in the third and fourth ways, understood materially: for "riches" name men rich, [possessioned]{g:possessionatus}, or having, formally.

## ¶4 · 119.11–119.27

> [p. 119] Et patet quod ille sex maneries habitus sunt satis equivoce. Dicitur ergo quod humanitas assumpta a Verbo est habitus tercii modi, cum accidit enti in actu, non mutans vel faciens ipsam aliam personam quam prefuit. Ideo dicunt sancti quod humanitas est quasi vestis detegens deitatem;⁶ et religiosi, qui Christum induunt, habent habitus⁷ corporis; hoc notantes, quod⁸ accidit Deo humanitas, sed non⁹ inseparabiliter. Ideo dicit Augustinus in Dialogo ad Felicianum quod humanitas est accidens Verbo, non quod sit res inherens ut accidencia novem generum, cum sit precipua creata substancia; nec quod sit coëva Verbo vel sicut passio naturaliter consequens¹⁰ ad subiectum; sed contingenter ex tempore nobis ineffabiliter¹¹ inest Verbo non mutando naturam, cui advenit, sed formata¹² mirabiliter, quia Verbo Dei ydemptificata vel ypostatice copulata: cum secundum Augustinum primo De Trinitate, ‘talis fuit unio¹³ incarnacionis, que Deum faceret¹⁴ hominem et hominem Deum.’

And it is clear that those six [kinds]{g:maneries} of habit are quite [equivocally]{g:equivoce} so called. So the answer is that the humanity [assumed]{g:assumere} by the Word is a habit of the third mode, since it befalls [a being]{g:ens} in act, not changing it or making it another person than it was before.[^8] So [the saints]{g:sancti} say that the humanity is as it were a garment exposing the Godhead; and [the religious]{g:religiosi}, who put on Christ, have habits of the body;[^9] noting this: the humanity befalls God, but inseparably.[^10] So Augustine says in the *Dialogue to Felicianus* that the humanity is an accident to the Word: not that it is a thing inhering like the accidents of the nine genera, since it is the foremost created substance; nor that it is coeval with the Word, or like an attribute naturally following on its subject;[^11] but contingently, from time, in a way ineffable to us, it is in the Word, not by changing the nature to which it comes, but formed wonderfully, because identified with the Word of God or [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} joined to him: since, according to Augustine, *On the Trinity* book 1, "such was the [union]{g:unio} of the incarnation, that it would make God man and man God."[^12]

## ¶5 · 119.28–120.18

> [p. 119] Et patet quod dicta humanitas est forma in tribus conveniens cum formis per se in genere accidentis. Primo in hoc quod presupponit naturam, cui contingenter advenit, longe priorem ipsa naturaliter, quam accidens presupponit creatam substanciam, quam informat. 2°. in hoc quod non facit [p. 120] naturam, cui advenit, esse¹ quid vel aliud² quam absolute necessario semper erat.³ Et 3°. quod non potest esse sine supposito Verbi Dei, cui inseparabiliter sed contingenter adheret. Et constat quod proprietates proporcionales analogice conveniunt generi accidentis: assimilatur autem passionibus, que dicuntur per se egredi de propriis principiis⁴ subiectorum; cum non potest abesse, postquam infuit, cum inseparabiliter consequitur⁵ ad Verbum, sicut passio ad subiectum; sed, sicut accidentia separabilia, deesse poterit a subiecto. Convenit 2°. cum accidentibus absolutis per se in genere, cum sit per se substancia motiva et mobilis denominans subiectum proprie et per se moveri formaliter. 3°. convenit⁶ cum accidentibus respectivis. Nam, sicut relaciones, habitaciones, et respectus consimiles non exinde movent naturam, cui inherenter adveniunt; sic indubie ‘forma servi,’ que est humanitas, non potest movere naturam divinam, cum deitas solum denominacione respectiva denominatur ex eius adiacencia summe mirabili.⁷

And it is clear that the said humanity is a form [agreeing]{g:convenire} in three things with forms that are [of themselves]{g:per-se} in the genus of accident. First, in this, that it presupposes the nature to which it contingently comes, by nature far prior to it, [more] than an accident presupposes the created substance which it informs.[^13] Second, in this, that it does not make [120] the nature to which it comes be something, or other than it absolutely necessarily always was. And third, that it cannot be without the supposit of the Word of God, to which it adheres inseparably but contingently. And it is evident that proportional properties agree analogically with the genus of accident: and it is likened to the attributes, which are said to issue of themselves from the proper principles of their subjects; since it cannot be absent after it has been present, since it follows inseparably on the Word, as an attribute on its subject; but, like separable accidents, it could be lacking from the subject.[^14] It agrees, second, with absolute accidents that are of themselves in the genus, since it is of itself a substance motive and [movable]{g:mobilis}, naming the subject properly and of itself as moved formally. Third, it agrees with relational accidents. For, as relations, [habitations]{g:habitacio}, and like respects do not thereby move the nature to which they come by inhering; so without doubt "the form of a servant" (Phil 2:7), which is the humanity, cannot move the divine nature, since the Godhead is named only by a [relational naming]{g:denominacio-respectiva} from its most wonderful lying-beside.

## ¶6 · 120.19–121.7

> [p. 120] Convenit autem cum ceteris formis substancialibus⁸ in his tribus. Primo quod facit suppositum, cui advenit, esse aliud quam prefuit; quia, postquam Verbum fuit pure deitas, nunc est homo. 2°. in hoc quod est idem ypostatice cum subiecto, cui advenit. Sicut generaliter omnis forma substancialis est idem essencialiter vel personaliter⁹ cum subiecto forme et composito ex eisdem; sic, inquam, ista humanitas; cum sit eiusdem racionis cum aliis et idem personaliter Verbo Dei, licet Verbum commune sit natura divina, que non potest esse illa humanitas; sed cum sit individuum humane nature homo Iesus Christus, suscipit saltem in predicacione et secundum essenciam predicata temporalia, que historia evangelica de Christo contexuit, ut post declarabitur. Et patet mira subtilitas in verbis Apostoli quando dicit ‘habitu inventus ut homo’: non enim video alium terminum, quo [p. 121] posset apcius quoad auditorii intelleccionem, copiosius quoad philosophicam consideracionem, aut expressius quoad fidei explanacionem, humanitatem Christi exprimere. Ideo respectu¹ sentencie Apostoli, qui fuit prudentissimus predicator, et Augustini sui discipuli, qui fuit scripture sacre subtilissimus explanator, oportet nos ignaros colla mentis submittere.

And it agrees with the other [substantial forms]{g:forma-substancialis} in these three things. First, that it makes the supposit to which it comes be something other than it was before; since, after the Word was purely Godhead, now he is man. Second, in this, that it is the same hypostatically with the subject to which it comes. As in general every substantial form is the same essentially or personally with the subject of the form and with the composite of the two; so, I say, is this humanity; since it is [of the same kind]{g:eiusdem-racionis} as the others and the same personally as the Word of God, although the Word [taken as] common is the divine nature, which cannot be that humanity;[^15] but since the man Jesus Christ is an [individual]{g:individuum} of human nature, he [takes up]{g:suscipere}, at least in predication and by essence, the temporal predicates which the gospel history has woven together about Christ, as will be [made clear]{g:declarare} later. And a wonderful subtlety is clear in the words of the Apostle when he says "in habit found as a man" (Phil 2:7): for I do not see another term by which [121] he could express the humanity of Christ more aptly as to the understanding of his hearers, more copiously as to philosophical consideration, or more expressly as to the explanation of the faith. So, with respect to the [teaching]{g:sentencia} of the Apostle, who was the most prudent preacher, and of Augustine his disciple, who was the subtlest explainer of holy scripture, we ignorant ones must bow the necks of the mind.

## Notes

[^1]: **G.** *Acciones ac passiones sunt primo suppositorum*: the schools' maxim that acting and being acted on belong to the supposit, not to its nature (LEDGER I07d-6). *Passio* here is the category set against *accio*; the "suffering" of 117.29 is the same word, of the passion.
[^2]: **C.** *Non conservative sed essencialiter* (118.6–7): the humanity is distinct from the Godhead in essence, not in being kept in being apart from it. *Que Christus est* is taken with *deitate*, the nearer noun; with *humanitas* it would say the humanity is Christ, which Wyclif also holds (120.25–26).
[^3]: **S.** Augustine, *De diversis quaestionibus LXXXIII*, q. 73, on Phil 2:7 (LEDGER I07d-1). Wyclif adds *est* to the Vulgate's *habitu inventus ut homo* (DR "in habit found as a man"), which he quotes exactly at 120.32–33.
[^4]: **T.** The words between daggers are Harris's conjecture, made "not without doubt": A B C read *habituatis quam quando membrum aptata induitur*, O *habituatis proficitur quam quando membris aptata induitur*. Neither construes (*quam* has no comparative), so the conjecture is translated. Augustine's third habit is the garment, which takes its form from the body it is put on (LEDGER I07d-1).
[^5]: **S.** *3°. Physicorum 9°· 4°*: Aristotle on motion, *Physics* III; the figures *9* and *4* are not identified (perhaps chapters or texts of a medieval division) (LEDGER I07d-2).
[^6]: **T.** *Preternaturali* (119.1) is the print's, from O's abbreviation (*pr̈ n̈li*); A B C read *passionali*, which would put habit in the third species of quality (passions and passible qualities) instead of the first (habit and disposition). At 119.3–4 A B C read *moralis aut habitus specialis. Et ille ex habitu primo dicto*; the print follows O.
[^7]: **C.** *De 10°. genere, quod est habere*: the tenth of Aristotle's categories, "having" (LEDGER I07d-3). *Habicio* is rendered "holding".
[^8]: **C.** *Ipsam* is feminine with no feminine antecedent but *humanitas*; taken as the person of the Word implied in *aliam personam* ("making it another person").
[^9]: **C.** *Detegens* is "exposing" (GLOSSARY §D): the garment that shows the Godhead, not one that hides it. *Qui Christum induunt*: Gal 3:27, "have put on Christ"; the religious habit is read as the figure.
[^10]: **T.** *Hoc notantes, accidit*: *quod* is omitted, with C O, against the print's *hoc notantes, quod accidit* (A B); the English is the same. *Sed inseparabiliter*, with A B C, against the print's *sed non inseparabiliter* (O): the argument requires it, since at 120.3 and 120.8 the humanity adheres to the Word *inseparabiliter*, and at 120.8 A B C have *non inseparabiliter* where the print and O have none: the two recensions carry the negative in opposite places.
[^11]: **S/C.** The *Dialogue to Felicianus* is Vigilius of Thapsus, *Contra Felicianum Arianum*, transmitted among Augustine's works (LEDGER I07d-4). *Passio* here is the proper attribute that follows on a subject (*subiectum / passio*), not the category of 117.32.
[^12]: **S.** Augustine, *De Trinitate* I.13.28, where the word is *susceptio*, not *unio* (LEDGER I07d-5).
[^13]: **C.** *Longe priorem ... quam*: *quam* taken as the comparative after *priorem* (the divine nature is prior to the humanity by far more than substance is to accident); read as "as", it states a likeness instead.
[^14]: **C.** *Abesse* / *deesse*: once present, the humanity cannot be absent from the Word, as an attribute from its subject; it is like a separable accident in that it could have been lacking, since it comes contingently.
[^15]: **C.** *Verbum commune*: *commune* is taken with *Verbum*, the Word as what he has in common with the Father and the Spirit, the divine nature; it could be read "although what is common in the Word is the divine nature".
