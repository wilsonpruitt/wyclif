# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 2, part 2: the second Arian error, Christ without a soul; Augustine against Pascentius

## ¶1 · 17.20–18.5

> [p. 17] Secundo stetit¹¹ error Arrianorum in hoc quod dicunt Christum sine anima solam carnem suscepisse¹²; ut meminit Augustinus libro suo De Heresibus Heresi 49. 'Arriani, inquit, dicti ab Arrio in eo sunt notissimi¹³ errore, quo Patrem et Filium et Spiritum Sanctum nolunt esse eiusdem substancie vel nature; sed Filium dicunt creaturam, Spiritum vero creaturam creature; hoc est, ab ipso Filio creatum¹⁴ volunt. In eo autem quod dicunt Christum sine anima solam carnem sumpsisse minus noti sunt. Sed hoc verum esse¹⁵ Epifanius¹⁶ non tacuit, et ego ex eorum scriptis et collocucionibus certissime comperi.' Et in hoc conveniunt cum Appollinaristis¹⁷; de quibus scribit Augustinus Heresi 55 [p. 18] quod de anima Christi ab ecclesia catholica¹ differunt² dicentes, sicut Arriani, Dominum³ Jesum Christum sine anima carnem suscepisse,³ et Verbum fuisse pro anima: et sic glosant scripturas loquentes de anima Christi et Verbum in carnem⁴ fuisse conversum, non carnem sumpsisse de virgine.

Second, the error of the Arians stood in this, that they say "Christ took up flesh alone, without a soul"; as Augustine recalls in his book *On Heresies*, heresy 49: "The Arians," he says, "named from Arius, are best known for that error by which they will not have the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit be of the same substance or [nature]{g:natura}; but they say the Son is a [creature]{g:creatura}, and the Spirit a creature of a creature; that is, they will have him created by the Son himself. But in this, that they say Christ took flesh alone without a soul, they are less known. But Epiphanius did not keep silent that this is true, and I have learned it most certainly from their writings and conversations."[^1] And in this they agree with the Apollinarists, of whom Augustine writes, heresy 55, [18] that they differ from the catholic [church]{g:ecclesia} concerning the soul of Christ, saying, like the Arians, that the Lord Jesus Christ took up flesh without a soul, and that [the Word]{g:verbum} was in place of a soul: and so they [gloss]{g:glosare} the scriptures that speak of the soul of Christ, and [say] that the Word was converted into flesh, not that he took flesh from the virgin.[^2]

## ¶2 · 18.6–18.20

> [p. 18] Et illam heresim⁵ elegantissime destruit Augustinus Ep. 127⁶ et duabus sequentibus ad Pascencium episcopum Arrianum; cuius dicta utinam hodierni atttenderent! Scribit enim capcioso heretico, non puto⁷ quidquam inprovide de quo calumpniam formidaret.⁸ 'Quia, inquit, Dei Patris unigenitus Filius Dominus et Deus noster Jesus Christus, postquam ''venit plenitudo temporis'' opportune⁹ ''formam servi suscepit'' ad diem salutis nostre¹⁰; de illo multa¹⁰ in scripturis sacris¹¹ secundum ''formam Dei'' dicuntur, multa secundum ''formam servi'': quorum exempli gracia duo commemoro,¹² ut singula ad singula referantur.¹³ Secundum formam Dei ait ''ego et Pater unum sumus'': secundum autem¹⁴ servi ipse de se ait ''Pater maior me est.''' Nescivit¹⁵ enim ille¹⁶ sanctus negare scripturas illas¹⁷ de virtute sermonis, cum personam¹⁷ Filii concedit¹⁸ esse multorum quodlibet.

And Augustine most elegantly destroys that heresy in letter 127 and the two following, to Pascentius, the Arian bishop; would that those of today attended to his sayings! For he writes to a captious heretic, [and] I do not think [he wrote] anything unguardedly over which he would fear a [challenge]{g:calumpnia}.[^3] "Since," he says, "the only-begotten Son of God the Father, our Lord and God Jesus Christ, after 'the fulness of the time was come', fittingly 'took up the form of a servant' for the day of our salvation; many things are said of him in the holy scriptures according to 'the form of God', many according to 'the form of a servant': of which, for example's sake, I recall two, that each may be referred to each. According to the form of God he says, 'I and the Father are one'; but according to [the form] of a servant he says of himself, 'The Father is greater than I.'"[^4] For that saint did not know how to deny those scriptures [in the proper sense of the words]{g:de-virtute-sermonis}, since he grants that the [person]{g:persona} of the Son is any one of many [things].[^5]

## ¶3 · 18.21–19.4

> [p. 18] Et ponit ad hoc consequenter duplicia exempla quibus Deus hucusque non¹⁹ dignatus est ostendere hominibus meliora, per que tam in noticiam Trinitatis quam eciam incarnacionis posset²⁰ hereticus manuduci. Primum sic. 'Cum corpus, inquit, et anima sunt unus homo, quamvis corpus et anima non sunt²¹ unum; cur non multo magis Pater et Filius sunt unus Deus? cum Pater et Filius sunt unum secundum illam Veritatis vocem ''ego et Pater unum sumus.'' Cum, inquit, homo exterior et interior homo non sunt unum, neque eiusdem nature est exterior cuius [p. 19] est interior¹; quia exterior cum corpore nuncupato¹ dicitur homo; interior autem sola anima racionali² intelligitur; utrumque tamen illorum simul non³ duo homines sed unus dicitur: quanto magis Pater et Filius unus Deus est?'

And to this he next lays down two examples, than which God has not hitherto deigned to show men better, by which the heretic might be led by the hand into knowledge as much of the Trinity as of the incarnation.[^6] The first is thus. "Since," he says, "body and soul are one man, although body and soul are not one, why are not the Father and the Son much more one God, since the Father and the Son are one, according to that voice of the Truth, 'I and the Father are one'?" "Since," he says, "the outer man and the inner man are not one, nor is the outer of the same nature as [19] the inner; for the outer, with the body so named, is called man, but the inner is understood as the rational soul alone; yet both of them together are called not two men but one: how much more are the Father and the Son one God?"[^7]

## ¶4 · 19.5–19.25

> [p. 19] Secundum exemplum est de natura universali. 'Homines, inquit, per consorcium et communionem⁴ unius eiusdem nature qua omnes homines unum erant, et si aliquando secundum diversitates voluntatum et sentenciarum opinionum et morum dissimilitudines⁵ non erant unum, erunt⁶ tamen perfecte pleneque unum⁶ cum perventum fuerit ad eum finem ut sit ''Deus omnia in omnibus.'' Et sic non est possibile homines esse unum in caritate vel alio accidente, nisi prius sint unum in substancia vel natura: ut declarat libro primo et⁷ 3° contra Maximinianum 22° capitulo. Quod autem multi homines sint unum in substancia vel natura⁸ probat⁹ per illud Apostoli ad Cor. 3° dictum¹⁰ de Paulo et Appollo ''qui plantat et qui rigat unum sunt.'' Unde Veritas Joh. 17.¹¹ ''Non¹² (dixit) rogo ut ipsi et nos unum simus;''¹² sed ''ut ipsi unum sint, sicut et nos unum sumus¹³:'' non solum natura quod iam erat verum, sed et perfecte¹³ caritatis atque iusticie¹⁴ pro sue¹⁵ capacitate nature, quantum¹⁶ in Dei regno esse poterit,¹⁷ ut sic ipsi summe unum sint in natura sua, sicut Pater et Filius summe unum sunt incomparabiliter¹⁸ meliori natura. Nunquam, inquit, de aliquibus rebus legimus quod unum sunt, nisi sint eiusdem substancie vel nature.'

The second example is about [universal]{g:universale} nature. "Men," he says, "through the fellowship and communion of one and the same nature, by which all men were one, even if at some time, according to diversities of wills and of [teachings]{g:sentencia}, opinions and dissimilarities of manners, they were not one, will yet be perfectly and fully one when the end is reached, that 'God may be all in all.'"[^8] And so it is not possible for men to be one in [charity]{g:caritas} or in another accident unless they are first one in substance or nature: as he [makes clear]{g:declarare} in the first and third books against Maximinus, chapter 22. And that many men are one in substance or nature he proves by that [text] of the Apostle, 1 Corinthians 3, said of Paul and Apollo: "he that planteth and he that watereth, are one." Hence the Truth, John 17: "I do not pray (he said) that they and we may be one," but "that they may be one, as we also are one": not only in nature, which was already true, but also [one] of perfect charity and justice, according to the capacity of their nature, as far as can be in the kingdom of God; so that thus they may be supremely one in their nature, as the Father and the Son are supremely one in an incomparably better nature. "Never," he says, "do we read of any things that they are one, unless they are of the same substance or nature."[^9]

## ¶5 · 19.26–20.13

> [p. 19] Et indubie¹⁹ in illo errore de universalibus cecati sunt Arrius, Sabellius, et quam plures heretici. Ideo Anselmus De Incarnacione primo vocat²⁰ negantes universalia ex parte rei extra signa²¹ dialectice hereticos. Unde Pascencius Arrianus postquam [p. 20] vocaverat¹ Augustinum succensum errore tanquam aqua cenosa se ingurgitantem et ut arborem curvam et nodosam que nichil rectitudinis in se habet, scribit quid moveat² eum ad depravandum sentenciam Augustini confitentis sibi in³ scriptis fidem suam. Nam⁴ inter alia beatus Augustinus scripsit sibi Patrem et Filium et Spiritum sanctum esse unum Deum, 'Quis, inquit, Pascencius e⁵ tribus personis est unus Deus? an forte una est persona triformis que hoc nomine nuncupetur?' Si iste hereticus cognovisset quomodo⁶ unica⁷ communis humanitas est quelibet persona hominis; vidisse potuisset⁸ faciliter quomodo unica deitas, essencia, substancia, vel natura divina, est tres persone et quelibet earundem.

And without doubt Arius, Sabellius, and very many heretics were blinded in that error about universals. Therefore Anselm, *On the Incarnation*, [chapter] 1, calls those who deny universals on the part of the thing, outside signs, heretics of dialectic.[^10] Hence Pascentius the Arian, after [20] he had called Augustine one kindled with error, as one plunging himself into muddy water, and like a crooked and knotty tree that has nothing of straightness in it, writes what moves him to distort the teaching of Augustine, who confessed his faith to him in writing. For among other things blessed Augustine wrote to him that "the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one God". "Which," says Pascentius, "of the three persons is the one God? Or is there perhaps one three-formed person who is called by this name?"[^11] If this heretic had known how a single common [humanity]{g:humanitas} is each person of man, he could easily have seen how a single [Godhead]{g:deitas}, [essence]{g:essencia}, substance, or divine nature is three persons and each one of them.

## ¶6 · 20.14–20.20

> [p. 20] Sabellius eciam si vidisset quod cum unitate nature stat distinccio personarum, sic quoque⁹ non fuisset cecatus¹⁰ in isto turpissimo paralogismo—Petrus¹¹ est natura specifica: Paulus est eadem natura specifica: ergo Petrus est Paulus— vidisset quod non oportet quod Pater sit Filius, quamvis utrumque sit eadem simplicissima substancia. Et sic de aliis heresibus pullulantibus propter defectum logice scripturarum.

Sabellius too, if he had seen that distinction of persons stands with unity of nature, so also would not have been blinded in that most foul [paralogism]{g:paralogismus} (Peter is [the nature of the species]{g:natura-specifica}; Paul is the same nature of the species; therefore Peter is Paul): he would have seen that it is not necessary that the Father be the Son, although each is the same most simple substance.[^12] And so of the other heresies sprouting for lack of the logic of the scriptures.

## ¶7 · 20.22–21.22

> [p. 20] Multi enim¹² cupiunt autorizare novam logicam extraneam a scriptura et hinc dicunt¹³ se ipsam corrigere tamquam impossibilem¹⁴ de vi vocis. Et illud meminit Augustinus contra dictum Pascencium. Nam¹⁵ contra¹⁵ negantes scripturas de Christo sic loquitur: 'Homines, inquit, minus intelligentes quid propter quid dicatur precipites volunt habere sentencias: et scripturis non diligenter scrutatis arripiunt defensionem¹⁶¹⁷ cuiuscumque¹⁸ opinionis¹⁶ et ab ea vel¹⁹ nunquam vel de difficili deflectuntur, dum docti atque sapientes magis putari quam esse cupiunt. Ea quippe, que propter formam²⁰ servi [p. 21] dicta sunt, volunt transferre ad formam Dei. 'Hoc, inquit, nomen Jesus Christus ex dispensacione misericordie¹ suscepteque² humanitatis assumptum est, quia ipse Filius Dei factus est filius hominis, non mutando quod erat, sed³ assumendo quod non erat.' Et probat diffuse ex scripturis⁴ quod tam Christus quam Spiritus Sanctus⁵ est idem Deus cum Patre et specialiter per illud Apostoli ad Cor. 8. 'Scimus quoniam nullus Deus nisi unus: nam etsi sunt qui dicantur dii⁶ multi et domini multi, nobis tamen unus Deus Pater ex quo omnia et nos in illo: et unus Dominus⁷ Jesus Christus per quem omnia et nos per illum.' Ex isto plane sequitur non protervo quod Christus sit idem Deus cum Patre. Unde dicunt periti scripture quod deus et dominus per se sumptus⁸ dicitur authonomatice de Deo et Domino dominorum. Scio tamen quod protervus potest,⁹ ut hodie, negare quamlibet partem scripture de virtute sermonis, et dicere quod Deus erat verbum nuncupative, et quod per Christum non sunt omnia facta sed omnia citra ipsum, et¹⁰ sic est Deus ac animatus,¹⁰ sed nec Deus deorum nec animatus¹¹ nobiscum univoce. Sed non sic sophisticati sunt eciam heretici, non sic negantes¹² scripturam ut hodie, quia melius fundati in logica et methaphisica quam nostrates.

For many desire to [authorize]{g:autorizare} a new logic foreign to scripture, and hence they say that they correct it as impossible [by the force of the words]{g:de-vi-vocis}.[^13] And Augustine recalls that against the said Pascentius. For against those who deny the scriptures about Christ he speaks thus: "Men," he says, "who understand too little what is said on account of what, headlong want to have their views; and, the scriptures not having been diligently searched, they seize on the defence of whatever [opinion]{g:opinio}, and from it they are turned aside either never or with difficulty, while they desire to be thought learned and wise rather than to be so. For the things that were said on account of the form of a servant [21] they want to transfer to the form of God."[^14] "This name," he says, "Jesus Christ, was [assumed]{g:assumere} from the dispensation of mercy and of the humanity taken up, because the very Son of God was made son of man, not by changing what he was, but by assuming what he was not." And he proves at length from the scriptures that both Christ and the Holy Spirit are the same God with the Father, and specially by that [text] of the Apostle, 1 Corinthians 8: "We know that there is no God but one. For although there be that are called gods many and lords many, yet to us there is but one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we in him: and one Lord Jesus Christ, by whom are all things, and we by him."[^15] From this it plainly [follows]{g:sequitur}, for one who is not [obstinate]{g:protervus}, that Christ is the same God with the Father. Hence those skilled in scripture say that "god" and "lord", taken by itself, is said [by antonomasia]{g:autonomatice} of God and of the Lord of lords.[^16] Yet I know that the obstinate man can, as today, deny any part of scripture in the proper sense of the words, and say that "the Word was God" [nuncupatively]{g:nuncupative}, and that all things were not made by Christ, but all things short of him; and so he is God and ensouled, but neither God of gods nor ensouled [univocally]{g:univoce} with us.[^17] But even the heretics did not so [play the sophist]{g:sophisticari}, not so denying scripture as today, since they were better grounded in logic and metaphysics than our own people.

## Notes

[^1]: **S.** Augustine, *De haeresibus* 49 (the Arians), citing Epiphanius: to be checked (LEDGER I02b-1). **T.** *stetit* (17.20): B M *stat*; *suscepisse*: O M *suscipere*.
[^2]: **S.** Augustine, *De haeresibus* 55 (the Apollinarists): to be checked (LEDGER I02b-2). **C.** *et Verbum in carnem fuisse conversum* still hangs on *dicentes*, across the clause *et sic glosant … Christi*; hence "[say]". **T.** *differunt* (18.2): A B C *discesserunt* "departed", O *disserunt*.
[^3]: **S.** Augustine's letters to Pascentius ("letter 127 and the two following"; O *Ep. 21 vel 5*), in the Maurist numbering *Ep.* 238–241: to be checked (LEDGER I02b-3). **C.** *non puto … formidaret* is read as Wyclif's comment on Augustine's care; A B C, with *formidarem* and a stop before *Non*, make it first person, perhaps Augustine's own words: "I do not think [I wrote] anything unguardedly over which I should fear a challenge."
[^4]: **S.** Gal. 4:4; Phil. 2:6–7 (*suscepit* for the Vulgate's *accipiens*); John 10:30; John 14:28. The opening *Quia* clause has no main clause: the period runs on into *de illo multa … dicuntur*.
[^5]: **C.** *Nescivit … negare*: "did not know how to deny", that is, would not have it in him to deny. *Personam Filii … esse multorum quodlibet*: the person of the Son is each of the many things scripture says of him, God and servant alike. A B C *persona* would make the person the subject of *concedit*, which does not construe; O *concedat*.
[^6]: **T.** *non* (18) is omitted by A B, which reverses the sense ("by which God has hitherto deigned to show men better things"); the print is kept.
[^7]: **C.** *unus homo … non sunt unum*: the masculine *unus* (one man) is set against the neuter *unum* (one thing): body and soul are one man without being one thing, and the Father and the Son are *unum* (John 10:30). *Exterior cum corpore nuncupato*: "the outer, with what is named the body"; C reads *interior comunia … exterius*. **T.** *racionali* (19) is O's reading, printed against A B C *racionabiliter* "is reasonably understood as the soul alone".
[^8]: **C.** *sentenciarum*: Augustine's plural, the differing views men hold; the row's "teaching". **T.** *erunt* (19): A B C *erant*, which loses the future the argument needs; O omits *erant … unum*. **S.** 1 Cor. 15:28.
[^9]: **S.** Augustine, *Contra Maximinum* (printed *Maximinianum*), "books 1 and 3, chapter 22" (A B C 23): to be checked (LEDGER I02b-4); 1 Cor. 3:8; John 17:11, 22. *Non rogo ut ipsi et nos unum simus* is not in the Gospel: it is the exegete's negative gloss, set against *ut ipsi unum sint*. **T.** The print's quotation marks open at *Homines* (19.5) and close only at *nature* (19.25); the English closes Augustine's words at "all in all" and quotes the last sentence, which carries its own *inquit*. *Non solum natura … meliori natura* is Augustine's argument, unmarked. A B C read *sumus. Sumus enim non solum unum natura quod verum est iam*; *perfecte caritatis* is a genitive without its noun, hence "[one]".
[^10]: **S.** Anselm, *Epistola de incarnatione Verbi* 1, on the "heretics of dialectic" for whom universal substances are only a breath of the voice: to be checked (LEDGER I02b-5). *Ex parte rei extra signa*: in reality, apart from the signs that signify them (O *signum*).
[^11]: **S.** Pascentius's abuse of Augustine and his question, from the Pascentius correspondence: to be checked (LEDGER I02b-6). **C.** *Quis, inquit, Pascencius*: Pascentius is the subject of *inquit*, and the question his; it is the "what moves him" of the sentence before.
[^12]: **T.** *quoque* (20): all the manuscripts read *quod* ("so that he would not have been blinded"); Harris prints *quoque*, and the English follows the print. **G.** *natura specifica*: the nature of the species, as in *ydemptitas specifica*; not "a particular nature". The paralogism treats the common nature, which each is, as if it were the person.
[^13]: **C.** *se ipsam corrigere*: *ipsam* is taken as scripture, which they "correct" as impossible when taken by the force of its words; it could refer to the new logic, which makes no sense of *corrigere*. *De vi vocis* is kept apart from *de virtute sermonis* (17).
[^14]: **S.** Augustine to Pascentius, on those who transfer to the form of God what is said of the form of a servant: to be checked (LEDGER I02b-7). The print opens a quotation at *Homines* and a second at *Hoc, inquit* without closing the first; the English closes it at "form of God".
[^15]: **S.** "This name Jesus Christ …": Augustine to Pascentius, to be checked (LEDGER I02b-8). 1 Cor. 8:4–6, with Wyclif's departures translated: he omits *idolum nihil est in mundo*, and *sive in caelo sive in terra siquidem sunt* (which A B C add); *nos in illo* for the Vulgate's *nos in illum* (DR "we unto him").
[^16]: **C.** *non protervo*: a dative, "for one who is not obstinate". *Authonomatice*: by antonomasia, the common noun used as the proper name of the one it fits par excellence.
[^17]: **C.** *quod Deus erat verbum nuncupative* is John 1:1 (*Deus erat Verbum*) held to be true "nuncupatively", by naming only; *per Christum non sunt omnia facta sed omnia citra ipsum* turns John 1:3. **T.** *et sic … animatus* (21) is omitted by B; A lacks *ac*, with an erasure of two letters. O reads *anima* for the second *animatus*.
