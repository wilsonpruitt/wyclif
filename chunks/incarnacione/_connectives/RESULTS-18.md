## Doubtful, left as they are

- **12-c, p. 212**: *Concesso autem consequente videtur primo …*, rendered "And, the consequent being granted". Either (c), the argument carried on, or (a), the step that brings the granted consequent to bear (it runs to *ergo … quod est intentum*). "And" is defensible.
- **13-a, p. 219**: *Sed iste homo non est iste homo*, rendered "But". Either (b), the rejoinder to "Jesus is Paul", or (a), the premiss that makes the reductio. "But" defensible.
- **13-b, p. 220**: *non esset multi homines nec unicus homo; sed et istud debet catholicus concedere*, rendered "and this too the catholic ought to grant". Intensive *sed et* ("and indeed", Wyclif endorsing Scotus) fits none of the classes cleanly; not *non … sed* (a new clause and subject). "But this too" would read as a contrast. The English carries it.
- **13-b, p. 222**: *Sed certum est, si multe persone divine sunt unus homo, sunt et una persona …; quod esse non potest*, rendered "Now". (a), the premiss that closes the reductio, or (b). "Now" defensible.
- **13-c, p. 226**: *inequalitas autem, in qua homo … ceciderat*, rendered "and the inequality". Correlative with the devil's *inequalitas* before it: (c) or (b) "but". "And" defensible.
- **13-c, p. 227**: *Sed et sanctus Thomas … dicit*, rendered "Moreover, Saint Thomas too". Intensive *sed et* adding a further authority; "Moreover" is not a card rendering. "And Saint Thomas too" (c-like) or "But Saint Thomas too" (b: he calls the teaching not heretical against Wyclif's view) are both possible; a real toss-up, so left.
- **13-d, p. 231**: *Redempcio autem humani generis … dicit infinitatem precii*, rendered "And". (c), taking up *redemit hominem* from the head of the ¶, or (a), opening the proof. "And" defensible.
