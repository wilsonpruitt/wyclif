# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 1, part 2: that Christ is a creature: Augustine, the creeds and Jerome

## ¶1 · 7.13–8.23

> [p. 7] Nec dubium quin ex principiis beati Augustini plane sequitur Christum esse creaturam. Quia iuxta suam infringibilem sentenciam Christus nedum est completa humanitas assumpta, sed tam corpus quam anima. Et quis dubitat de ista sequela? Christus est corpus creatum sicut et⁸ spiritus creatus. Ergo Christus est creatura et secundum illam creatus. Nec aliter esset univoce homo nobiscum:⁹ cum omnis alius homo sit duarum naturarum utraque. Nec credo sine [alta]¹⁰ illa philosophia De Anima nos posse recte concipere incarnacionem Verbi. Ideo Augustinus¹¹ Epistula 3ᵃ ad Volusianum sic loquitur: ‘Nunc vero inter Deum et homines mediator apparuit ut in unitate¹² persone¹³ copulans utramque naturam et solita¹³ sublimaret insolitis et insolita solitis temperaret.... Ille¹⁴ ergo sine seminibus operatus est hominem, qui in rerum natura sine seminibus operatus est semina.¹⁴ Ille in suo corpore numeros temporum mensurasque servavit etatum qui sine ulla¹⁵ mutabilitate mutando ordinem contexuit seculorum.¹⁶ Hoc¹⁷ enim crevit in¹⁸ [p. 8] tempore quod cepit ex tempore: Verbum autem in principio, per quod facta sunt omnia tempora, tempus elegit quo susciperet carnem, non tempori¹ cessit ut verteretur in carnem: homo quippe Deo accessit, non Deus a se recessit.’ Et sequitur ad propositum. ‘Sic autem quidam² reddi sibi racionem flagitant, quomodo Deus homini permixtus sit, ut una fieret persona Christi, cum hoc³ semel fieri oporteat. Sed quam racionem reddant ipsi de re quae cotidie⁴ fit, quomodo anima misceatur corpori ut una persona fiat hominis? Nam sicut in unitate⁵ persone anima unitur corpori ut homo sit, ita in unitate⁵ persone Deus unitur⁶ homini ut Christus⁷ sit. In illa ergo persona mixtura est anime et corporis, in hac persona⁸ mixtura est Dei et hominis. Si tamen recedat, auditor⁹ a consuetudine corporum qua⁹ solent duo liquores ita misceri ut neutrum servet suam integritatem.’ Et sequitur. ‘Quia ergo non oportebat ut in plenitudine temporis novum faceret¹⁰ mundum, nova¹¹ fecit in mundo. Homo enim de virgine procreatus et a mortuis in eternam vitam resuscitatus et super celos exaltatus potencius¹² fortasse opus¹³ est quam mundus.’ Quotlibet¹⁴ sunt talia dicta hujus boni hominis, ex quibus aperiri¹⁵ potest sensus scripture loquentis varie de ipso¹⁶ et controversia doctorum sequencium concordari.¹⁷

Nor is there any doubt that from the principles of blessed Augustine it plainly [follows]{g:sequitur} that Christ is a [creature]{g:creatura}. For according to his unbreakable [teaching]{g:sentencia} Christ is not only the complete [humanity]{g:humanitas} [assumed]{g:assumere}, but both body and soul. And who doubts of this, [what follows]{g:sequela}? Christ is a created body, as he is also a created spirit. Therefore Christ is a creature, and created as to it.[^1] Nor otherwise would he be [univocally]{g:univoce} man with us, since every other man is both of two [natures]{g:natura}. Nor do I believe that without that [lofty][^2] philosophy of the *De anima* we can rightly conceive the incarnation of the [Word]{g:verbum}. So Augustine, in the third letter to Volusian, speaks thus: "But now a mediator between God and men has appeared, so that, joining both natures in the unity of [person]{g:persona}, he might both raise up the accustomed by the unaccustomed and temper the unaccustomed by the accustomed. ... He therefore wrought man without seeds, who in the nature of things wrought seeds without seeds. He kept in his body the numbers of the times and the measures of the ages, who without any changeableness, by changing, wove together the order of the ages. For that grew in [8] time which began from time; but the Word in the beginning, through whom all times were made, chose the time in which to take up flesh; he did not yield to time so as to be turned into flesh: for man drew near to God, God did not withdraw from himself."[^3] And it goes on, to the purpose: "And some demand thus that an account be rendered them, how God was mingled with man so that the one person of Christ came to be, since this had to happen once. But what account do they themselves render of a thing that happens every day, how the soul is mingled with the body so that the one person of a man comes to be? For as in the unity of person the soul is united to the body that there may be a man, so in the unity of person God is united to man that there may be Christ. In that person, therefore, there is a mixture of soul and body; in this person a mixture of God and man. Provided, however, that the hearer withdraws from the habit of bodies, by which two liquids are wont to be so mixed that neither keeps its integrity."[^4] And it goes on: "Since, therefore, it was not fitting that in the fullness of time he should make a new world, he made new things in the world. For a man procreated of a virgin, and raised from the dead into eternal life, and exalted above the heavens, is perhaps a mightier work than the world." There are any number of such sayings of this good man, from which the sense of scripture, which speaks variously of him, can be opened, and the controversy of the doctors who followed can be brought into agreement.

## ¶2 · 8.24–8.30

> [p. 8] Et patet quod in simbolo Athanasii quando dicitur ‘Filius a Patre solo non est factus nec creatus sed genitus’ loquitur de eo pure secundum deitatem.¹⁸ Aliter enim contradiceret sibi ipsi, cum secundum humanitatem conceptus est a tota Trinitate ex substancia matris: ut dicit idem simbolum quando post introducit Christi incarnacionem secundum quam dicit verissime quod Christus est minor patre.

And it is clear that in the [creed]{g:symbolum} of Athanasius, when it is said "The Son, of the Father alone, is not made nor created, but begotten", it speaks of him purely [as to the Godhead]{g:secundum-deitatem}.[^5] For otherwise it would contradict itself, since [as to the humanity]{g:secundum-humanitatem} he was conceived by the whole Trinity from the substance of his mother; as the same creed says when it afterwards brings in the incarnation of Christ, according to which it says most truly that Christ is less than the Father.[^6]

## ¶3 · 8.31–9.12

> [p. 8] Et idem patet in alio simbolo ecclesie; ubi primo loquens [p. 9] de eo secundum quod Deus est vere dicit ‘genitum non factum, consubstancialem Patri per quem omnia facta sunt.’ Sed post loquendo de Christo ut homine dicit quod ‘incarnatus est de Spiritu Sancto ex Maria virgine et homo factus est.’ Ideo in talibus attendendum est ad regulam beati Augustini ‘quid et secundum quam naturam de Christo dicitur:’ et facile videtur quotquot auctoritates occurrerint¹ concordare. Cum enim sit Christus multorum singulum, sicut essencia divina, species humana, vel aliud universale; patet quia, sicut non repugnat Deum esse genitum et eundem Deum esse ingenitum, sic non repugnat eundem Christum esse creatorem et tamen ex tempore esse creatum.

And the same is clear in the other creed of the [church]{g:ecclesia}, where, speaking first [9] of him [insofar as]{g:secundum-quod} he is God, it says truly "begotten not made, consubstantial with the Father, by whom all things were made." But afterwards, speaking of Christ as man, it says that he "was incarnate of the Holy Spirit from the virgin Mary, and was made man." Therefore in such [statements] attention must be paid to the rule of blessed Augustine, "what, and according to which nature, is said of Christ"; and it is easily seen that however many authorities occur agree.[^7] For since Christ is a single [thing] of many, as the divine essence, the human species, or another [universal]{g:universale} [is],[^8] it is clear that, just as it is not [incompatible]{g:repugnare} that God is begotten and that the same God is unbegotten, so it is not incompatible that the same Christ is [creator]{g:creator} and yet is created from time.

## ¶4 · 9.13–9.23

> [p. 9] Sed oportet in istis diversas² naturas subintelligere³ cum ydemptitate persone. Unde in primitiva ecclesia quando Christi divinitas fuit minus cognita,⁴ ordinaverunt apostoli quod fideles baptizarentur in nomine domini Iesu Christi, ut patet Actuum 8. ut vel sic Christiani elevarentur in animo⁵ ad credendum Christum esse supremam naturam possibilem⁶ scilicet deitatem. Sed postquam sancti doctores ecclesie ut Augustinus, Ieronimus et ceteri cultores assidui scripturarum intellexerunt quod Christus fuit gigas gemine substancie, scilicet⁷ tam divina substancia quam humana, concesserunt copulative Christum esse tam creatorem quam⁸ creaturam.

But in these [statements] one must [understand as implied]{g:subintelligere} diverse natures together with identity of person. Hence in the primitive church, when the [divinity]{g:divinitas} of Christ was less known, the apostles ordained that the faithful be baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, as is clear from Acts 8, so that at least in this way Christians might be raised in mind to believing that Christ is the supreme possible nature, namely [the Godhead]{g:deitas}. But after the holy doctors of the church, such as Augustine, Jerome and the other assiduous cultivators of the scriptures, understood that Christ was "a giant of twin substance", namely both divine substance and human,[^9] they granted [conjunctively]{g:copulative} "Christ is both creator and creature".

## ¶5 · 9.24–10.2

> [p. 9] Unde beatus Ieronimus in fine primi libri super Epistolam ad Galat,⁹ ‘Quia semel,¹⁰ inquit, ad nomen creature venimus et sapiencia in Proverbiis Salomonis Dicit, “deus creavit me¹¹ in inicio viarum suarum antequam quidquam¹² faceret, a principio ab eterno ordinata sum,¹³ et ab antiquis antequam terra fieret”; multique timore ne Christum creaturam dicere compellantur,¹⁴ totum Christi misterium negant, ut dicant non Christum in hac sapiencia sed¹⁵ mundi sapienciam significari:¹⁵ nos libere proclamamus non esse periculum eum [p. 10] dicere¹ creaturam, quem² verum esse hominem² et crucifixum et maledictum tota spei nostre reverencia profitemur.”

Hence blessed Jerome, at the end of the first book on the Epistle to the Galatians: "Since once," he says, "we have come to the name 'creature', and Wisdom says in the Proverbs of Solomon, 'God created me in the beginning of his ways, before he made any thing; from the beginning I was set up from eternity, and of old, before the earth was made'; and many, for fear that they be compelled to call Christ a creature, deny the whole [mystery]{g:misterium} of Christ, so as to say that not Christ but the wisdom of the world is signified in this wisdom: we freely proclaim that there is no danger in [10] calling him a creature whom, with the whole reverence of our hope, we profess to be true man, and crucified, and accursed."[^10]

## ¶6 · 10.3–10.16

> [p. 10] Nec credo quod sanctus iste intellexit Christum esse creaturam sub racione qua eternaliter genitus; propter tria. Primo quia tunc concederet³ Christum eciam secundum deitatem esse creaturam; et cum creatura dicitur abstractive, sicut substancia vel essencia, tunc Christus esset alia substancia vel essencia quam Pater; quod est heresis Arriana. Et ad tantum non licet catholico laxare terminos, cum omnis creatura secundum Augustinum in Dialogo ad Felicianum et in De Questionibus⁴ Veteris et Nove Legis 122, sit de nichilo facta essencia. Sed constat quod Christus secundum deitatem non est factus ex nichilo; ergo nec secundum deitatem est creatura. Non est ergo de intencione huius sancti et profundi doctoris baptizare et⁵ tam extraneare⁶ a scriptura significacionem huius nominis ‘creatura.’

Nor do I believe that this saint understood "Christ is a creature" under the aspect by which he is eternally begotten; for three [reasons]. First, because then he would grant "Christ, even as to the Godhead, is a creature"; and since "creature" is said [abstractively]{g:abstractive}, like "substance" or "essence", then Christ would be another substance or [essence]{g:essencia} than the Father; which is the Arian heresy. And it is not permitted to a catholic to [loose]{g:laxare} the terms so far, since every creature, according to Augustine in the *Dialogue to Felician* and in the *Questions of the Old and New Law* 122, is an essence made from nothing.[^11] Now it is evident that Christ as to the Godhead is not made from nothing; therefore neither as to the Godhead is he a creature. It is not, therefore, the intention of this holy and profound doctor to baptize, and so far to estrange from scripture, the signification of this name "creature".[^12]

## ¶7 · 10.17–11.1

> [p. 10] Secundo movet me causa quam beatus Ieronimus innuit. Videtur enim loqui de fidelibus sui temporis negantibus adverbia⁷ scripturarum loquencium⁷ de Christo secundum humanitatem. Sed quis queso fidelium trepidaret concedere⁸ Christum esse genitum⁹ eternaliter de Patre? cum puri philosophi diu ante incarnacionem hoc professi sunt, ut declaravi¹⁰ in tractatu De Trinitate.¹¹ Non ergo timebat concedere Christum esse¹² genitum vel genitum¹² eternaliter adintra; sed non secundum hominem assumptum et¹³ racione cuius concedimus de Christo predicata et gesta hominum ut sanctus doctor exemplificat et clarebit posterius.¹⁴ Unde evidencia Ieronimi est quod concedendum est Christum esse creaturam eo quod concedimus ipsum verum¹⁵ esse [p. 11] hominem et cetera huiusmodi que humanitatem concernunt.

Second, the cause that blessed Jerome hints at moves me. For he seems to speak of the faithful of his time who denied the adverbs of the scriptures that speak of Christ as to the humanity.[^13] Now who of the faithful, I ask, would tremble to grant "Christ is begotten eternally of the Father"? since mere philosophers professed this long before the incarnation, as I have [made clear]{g:declarare} in the treatise *On the Trinity*. He did not, therefore, fear to grant "Christ is begotten, or begotten eternally, within";[^14] but not as to [the assumed man]{g:homo-assumptus}, by reason of whom we grant of Christ the predicates and deeds of men,[^15] as the holy doctor gives instances, and as will be clear later. Hence Jerome's [evidence]{g:evidencia} is that "Christ is a creature" is to be granted because we grant "he is true [11] man", and other things of this kind that concern the humanity.[^16]

## ¶8 · 11.3–11.20

> [p. 11] Tercio movet me quod nec iste sanctus sapiens nec scriptura alia canonica, si non fallor, alicubi¹ accipit creari² pro gignicione eterna. Supposito ergo quod scriptura tam Salomonis quam Ecclesiastica loquatur de sapiencia increata que est Verbum Dei, ut testatur Ieronimus, et beatus Augustinus Ep 31 declarat ex textu Proverbiorum 8 et 30 quod intencionis Salomonis est dicere, quod Deus habet Filium sapienciam increatam: patet quod de ipsa loquitur nunc secundum deitatem ut eternaliter adintra genita non creata, et nunc ut creata vel creanda: ut nunc dicit secundum deitatem, ‘cum eo eram cuncta componens,’ et nunc ‘ab eterno ordinata sum:’ primum ad deitatem sapiencie referens, et secundum ad eius humanitatem, ut patet Proverbiorum 8. Unde de eius humanitate dicit infra 30.³ ‘Quis ascendit in celum atque descendit etc?⁴ Quod nomen eius aut quod nomen filii eius, si nosti?’ Et isti sensui alludit Apostolus secundum exposicionem Augustini ad Eph.⁵ 4, quando⁶ dicit: ‘qui descendit ipse est qui et ascendit super omnes celos.’

Third, it moves me that neither this wise saint nor any other canonical scripture, if I am not mistaken, anywhere takes "to be created" for eternal begetting. Supposing, therefore, that the scripture both of Solomon and of Ecclesiasticus speaks of the uncreated wisdom which is the Word of God, as Jerome testifies, and as blessed Augustine, letter 31, makes clear from the text of Proverbs 8 and 30, that it is Solomon's intention to say that God has a Son, the uncreated wisdom: it is clear that it speaks of that wisdom now as to the Godhead, as eternally begotten within, not created, and now as created or to be created. So it now says, as to the Godhead, "I was with him forming all things", and now "I was set up from eternity": referring the first to the Godhead of wisdom, and the second to its humanity, as is clear from Proverbs 8. Hence of its humanity it says below, in 30: "Who hath ascended up into heaven, and descended, etc.? What is his name, or what is the name of his son, if thou knowest?" And to this sense the Apostle alludes, according to Augustine's exposition, in Ephesians 4, when he says: "He that descended is the same also that ascended above all the heavens."[^17]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Christus est creatura et secundum illam creatus*: *illam* (feminine) takes up *creatura*, "created as to [that] creature", i.e. as to the created body and spirit just named. In the next sentence *omnis alius homo sit duarum naturarum utraque* keeps Wyclif's predication: every man *is* both natures (body and soul), not "has" them; the parity of Christ with "every other man" turns on it.
[^2]: **T.** The square brackets are Harris's: *alta* stands in A's margin (A's first hand has *illa*); B reads *alta 1ᵃ*, C and O *1ᵃ* (*prima*) without *alta*, and Harris asks whether *prima* should be read, "the first philosophy", i.e. metaphysics. The English follows the print.
[^3]: **S.** Augustine, letter to Volusian, *Ep.* 137.9–11 and 14 (PL 33, 519–522; *verified*, LEDGER I01b-1). *Ulla* (7.29) is Harris's reading from Augustine "as the sense requires"; A B C read *ista*, O *illa*. The four dots are the print's, marking a cut in the quotation.
[^4]: **C.** *Si tamen recedat, auditor … integritatem*: a condition with no consequent of its own. It qualifies the sentence before (soul and body, God and man, are a "mixture" only if the hearer sets aside the mixing of liquids), and the English takes it so; Harris prints a full stop before *Si* and a comma after *recedat*. *Qua* is Augustine's reading; A and B have *quomodo*, O an abbreviation (8.15).
[^5]: **S.** The Athanasian creed (*Quicumque*): *Filius a Patre solo est: non factus, nec creatus, sed genitus*; in Wyclif's form *est* stands after *non*, and the English follows his order. The creed's *ex substantia matris* and *minor Patre secundum humanitatem* are what he cites next (PL 88, 585; *verified*, LEDGER I01b-2).
[^6]: **T.** *secundum deitatem* (8.26): A B C read *divinitatem*. The print keeps *deitas* "the Godhead", which the GLOSSARY keeps apart from *divinitas* "divinity".
[^7]: **S.** "The other creed" is the Nicene-Constantinopolitan creed of the mass (as in the Council of Friuli, PL 99, 293A; *verified*, LEDGER I01b-3). Augustine's rule *quid et secundum quam naturam de Christo dicitur*: no source given by the editor. The rule is Augustine's *quid propter quid, et quid secundum quid dicatur* (*De Trinitate* I.13.28, PL 42, 840, which the Lombard cites on whether Christ is a creature, *Sent.* III d. 11 c. 1), and *Ep.* 187.8, quoted at 5.1, has *quid secundum quid dicatur intuendum*. *Secundum quam naturam* is Wyclif's gloss (*verified*, LEDGER I01b-4).
[^8]: **C.** *multorum singulum*: one single thing that is many, as the one divine essence is three persons and the one human species is many men; the comparison with "another universal" is Wyclif's realism about common things. The alternative, "one singular among many", does not fit the examples.
[^9]: **S.** *gigas gemine substancie* echoes Ambrose's hymn *Intende qui regis Israel* (PL 16, 1411, *Geminae gigas substantiae*, after Ps. 18:6), a line Augustine himself uses of Christ in *Contra sermonem Arianorum* 8.6, PL 42, 689, *geminae gigas substantiae, secundum quid obediens, secundum quid aequalis Deo* (*verified*, LEDGER I01b-5). Acts 8:16 reads "baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus"; "Christ" is Wyclif's addition.
[^10]: **S.** Jerome, *Commentarii in epistolam ad Ephesios* I, on Eph. 2:10 (PL 26, 471A; *verified*, LEDGER I01b-6), not *In Galatas* as Wyclif (O *galath.*, A B C *Gall.*) and the print give it; Jerome has *fiducia* for *reverencia*. Jerome only paraphrases the verse (*dicit se creatam initium viarum Dei*); Wyclif's fuller Proverbs 8:22–23 is not the Vulgate's: *creavit* for *possedit* ("possessed"), *Deus* for *Dominus*, *ab antiquis* for *ex antiquis*. On *quem verum esse hominem* (10.1) see note 16.
[^11]: **S.** The *Dialogue to Felician* is the *Contra Felicianum Arianum* of Vigilius of Thapsus, current under Augustine's name; the *Quaestiones Veteris et Novi Testamenti* (here "of the Old and New Law"), question 122, are Ambrosiaster's. Both *verified*: Vigilius, *De unitate Trinitatis* 7, PL 42, 1162 (the creature is *ex eo quod non est, in id quod est*), and *Quaest.* 122, PL 35, 2365 (*Si enim coepit esse, creatura est … Quidquid enim est, aut Deus est, aut creatura est*). Neither has Wyclif's words *de nichilo facta essencia*, which sum them up (LEDGER I01b-7, I01b-8).
[^12]: **C.** *baptizare*: to give a new name, as at a christening: to re-name "creature" so that it covers eternal begetting. *Et* before *tam extraneare* is omitted in A B C, and *extraneare* is *extranee* in A B C, *extranie* O (10.15).
[^13]: **C.** *negantibus adverbia scripturarum loquencium*: so the print, "denying the adverbs" (perhaps the scriptures' qualifying expressions, such as *ab eterno*). A B C read *ad verba scripturam loquentem*, O *adverbia scripturam loquentem*: "denying [it] at the words of scripture that speaks of Christ …". The print is translated; the sense is doubtful.
[^14]: **C.** *Christum esse genitum vel genitum eternaliter adintra*: the print repeats *genitum*; A B C have *genituram vel genitum*, O *genitum vel genitura*. The English keeps the print, "begotten, or begotten eternally". The subject of *timebat* is the faithful man of the question before (or Jerome, who speaks for them).
[^15]: **T.** The print has *et* before *racione cuius* (O *esse*, M *et*, A B C omit it). The English follows A B C, since with *et* the clause does not construe: "but not as to the assumed man, and by reason of whom …".
[^16]: **T.** *ipsum verum esse hominem* (10.29): A B C read *vermem hominem*, O *ipsam esse vᵐ ē hominem*, "worm" (Ps. 21:7, "I am a worm, and no man"). At 10.1 too O has *verbum esse et hominem* for the print's *verum esse hominem*. Jerome wrote *quem vermem, et hominem, et crucifixum, et maledictionem* (PL 26, 471A), so A B C's *vermem* is his reading (LEDGER I01b-6).
[^17]: **S.** Augustine "letter 31" on Proverbs 8 and 30, with Eph. 4:10, is *Ep.* 102 (to Deogratias), q. 5, §§28–29, PL 33, 382: it reads Prov. 8:25 and 30:4 of the Son, and the *Quis ascendit … et descendit* of Prov. 30:4 by *Qui descendit, ipse est et qui ascendit super omnes coelos* (*verified*, LEDGER I01b-10, I01b-11); Wyclif's number 31 is not the received one. The place in Wyclif's own *De Trinitate* (10.23) is untraced; that work is not in the Wyclif Society series (LEDGER I01b-9). "Ecclesiastica" is the book of Ecclesiasticus. Prov. 30:4 is shortened (*etc.*) and has *aut* for the Vulgate's *et*, without *est*: "or what is the name of his son".
