# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 7, part 3: the form of a servant: the immovable nature and the movable supposit

## ¶1 · 113.30–115.21

> [p. 113] Et sic intelligendus est sensus Apostoli ad Philipp. 2°. quod Christus,¹² ‘cum in forma Dei esset, non rapinam arbitratus est esse se equalem Deo; sed semetipsum exinanivit formam servi accipiens.’ Pro cuius intellectu notandum quod sicut forma [p. 114] Dei est deitas,¹ sic forma hominis est humanitas. Utraque enim est forma substancialis; cum quelibet persona divina sit illud quo² est Deus,³ quia Deus formaliter deitate;¹ sicut quelibet persona hominis est illud quo² est homo, quia⁴ est homo⁴ formaliter humanitate. Utramque autem harum formarum habet ut subiectus eis formaliter; unam tamen habet absolute necessario; et aliam accepit⁵ ex tempore. Et istam altissimam methaphisicam de formis vellem nostros theologos, etsi non in⁶ methaphisica Aristotelis, saltem in verbis Apostoli compendiose addiscere. Ex quo patet, quod non ex propria reputacione vel arbitrio proprio sine veritate substrata vendicavit se esse equalem Patri, quasi⁷ raperet sibi honorem Patri debitum, ut facit Sathanas; cum naturaliter et communiter inest sibi cum Patre et Spiritu Sancto⁸ esse Deum. Quod co-inest⁹ sibi et Patri, ostenderat quando¹⁰ dixit,¹⁰ ‘Ego et Pater unum sumus.’ Augustinus autem format talem racionem; ‘Nichil quod est naturale¹¹ et essenciale¹¹ supposito est rapina: sed¹² Christum esse equalem Deo est naturale et per se inest Verbo: ergo hoc non est rapina.¹² Sed faciendo se esse naturam visibilem exinanivit misericorditer semetipsum: semetipsum dicit¹³ propter ydemptitatem persone que est filius hominis¹⁴ et Dei Filius:¹⁴ exinanivit dicit,¹⁵ quia fecit se ad¹⁶ extra esse naturam corpoream, que,¹⁷ [cum] in mundi principio prius originem¹⁷ quam formam habeat, est inanis.’ Ad quem sensum, ut exponit Augustinus 12°. Confessionum, scriptura in principio Gen. dicit, ‘terra erat inanis et vacua.’ Nec video quomodo exinanicio¹⁸ pocius¹⁹ possit intelligi quam quod sit²⁰ res extra facta inanis²¹ essencialiter sic ut sit²¹ exinanita. Et hoc est verum de Christo ad litteram, cum sit trium naturarum [p. 115] quelibet. Et hinc vere dicit Veritas, Ioh. 14°. ‘Pater maior me est:’ quia, cum¹ Christus sit tam equivoce divinitas et humanitas, potest respectu predicati absoluti intelligi secundum racionem divinitatis vel secundum racionem humanitatis, sicut exigit predicatum. Et patet ex dictis superius quod non est repugnancia—Pater est maior Christo—et—idem² Pater est³ simpliciter equalis Christo—: ymo, intelligendo personam Christi secundum duas naturas equivoce, non est repugnancia inter ista—Pater est maior Christo—⁴ et—idem Pater non est maior Christo—⁴: nec debent tales predicaciones abici de virtute sermonis quod sunt figurative, quia per idem⁵ negaretur hoc—Ego et Pater unum sumus—Pater et Filius et Spiritus Sanctus sunt unus Deus, etc :—cum in prima sit equivocacio, et in secunda concepcio personarum. Ymo, si non fallor, omnis locucio nostra de Deo est figurativa, ut quandocunque dicitur quod Deus est mobilis, natus, passus etc. est construccio non synecdochica sed figura que antropospatos dicitur, quando scilicet humana passio Deo attribuitur; ab antropos homo et patos passio. Non enim habemus nomina, que sine figura Deum signent.

And so the sense of the Apostle, Philippians 2, is to be understood: that Christ, "being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: But [emptied]{g:exinanire} himself, taking the form of a servant." For the understanding of which it is to be noted that as the form [114] of God is the [Godhead]{g:deitas},[^1] so the form of man is the [humanity]{g:humanitas}. For each is a [substantial form]{g:forma-substancialis}; since each divine [person]{g:persona} is that which is God, because [it is] God formally by the Godhead; as each person of man is that which is man, because he is man formally by the humanity.[^2] And he has each of these forms as their [subject]{g:subiectum}, formally; yet the one he has absolutely, of necessity, and the other he received in time. And this most lofty metaphysics of forms I would wish our theologians to learn compendiously, if not in the Metaphysics of Aristotle, at least in the words of the Apostle. From which it is clear that it was not from his own [estimation]{g:reputacio}, or his own judgement, without truth beneath it, that he claimed himself to be equal to the Father, as if he were seizing for himself the honour due to the Father, as Satan does; since to be God belongs to him naturally and in common with the Father and the Holy Spirit. That it belongs jointly to him and to the Father he had shown when he said, "I and the Father are one." And Augustine frames such an argument:[^3] "Nothing that is natural and essential to a [supposit]{g:suppositum} is robbery: now for Christ to be equal to God is natural and belongs [of itself]{g:per-se} to the [Word]{g:verbum}: therefore this is not robbery.[^4] But by making himself to be a visible [nature]{g:natura} he mercifully emptied himself: 'himself', it says, on account of the identity of the person who is the son of man and the Son of God: 'emptied', it says, because he made himself to be, outwardly, a corporeal nature, which in the beginning of the world, being prior in origin to its having form, is empty."[^5] To which sense, as Augustine expounds in Confessions 12, scripture says in the beginning of Genesis, "the earth was void and empty".[^6] Nor do I see how [emptying]{g:exinanicio} can be better understood than as a thing made outwardly empty, essentially, so that it is emptied. And this is true of Christ to the letter, since he is each one of three [115] natures.[^7] And hence the Truth truly says, John 14, "the Father is greater than I": for, since Christ is so [equivocally]{g:equivoce} the [divinity]{g:divinitas} and the humanity, he can, with respect to an absolute predicate, be understood according to the [aspect]{g:racio} of divinity or according to the aspect of humanity, as the predicate requires. And it is clear from what was said above that there is no [incompatibility]{g:repugnancia} [between] "the Father is greater than Christ" and "the same Father is [without qualification]{g:simpliciter} equal to Christ": indeed, understanding the person of Christ equivocally as to two natures, there is no incompatibility between these: "the Father is greater than Christ" and "the same Father is not greater than Christ"; nor ought such [predications]{g:predicacio} to be rejected, [in the proper sense of the words]{g:de-virtute-sermonis}, as being figurative,[^8] for by the same token this would be denied, "I and the Father are one", [and] "the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one God", etc., since in the first there is [equivocation]{g:equivocacio}, and in the second a conception of persons. Indeed, if I am not mistaken, all our speech about God is figurative, as whenever it is said "God is movable, born, has suffered", etc., it is not a [synecdochic construction]{g:construccio-synecdochica} but the figure that is called *antropospatos*, namely when a human passion is attributed to God; from *antropos*, man, and *patos*, passion.[^9] For we have no names that signify God without figure.

## ¶2 · 115.22–116.6

> [p. 115] Redeundo ergo ad propositum conceditur quod aliqua res, quia natura divina, est omnino immobilis, sic quod nullo modo moveri poterit, licet sit suppositum mobile, ut puta, Christus passus. Patet ista logica in exemplo. Nam natura divina est Verbum tam eternaliter quam temporaliter genitum, et tamen ipsa non est genita, et talis⁶ essencia materie prime est suppositum ignis compositum ex materia et forma: et tamen ipsa materia non potest sic componi. Petrus comedit, moritur, vel aliter transmutatur; et tamen natura specifica, que est Petrus, non potest taliter transmutari. Ideo in omnibus istis similibus oportet diligenter attendere ad predicacionem secundum essenciam et formalem. Et patet quod non sequitur—Deus est mobilis,⁷ et omne aliud [p. 116] a Deo est mobile: ergo omnis res est mobilis—quia aliqua res non est mobilis. Et si queratur—Quid non est mobile ?—potest dici equivocando, quod nichil non est mobile, quia natura divina est mobile: et sic, intelligendo predicacionem secundum essenciam non formalem,¹ omne, quod² est, est mobile.²

Returning, then, to the matter in hand, it is granted that some thing, because [it is] the divine nature, is altogether [immovable]{g:immobilis}, so that it can in no way be moved, although it is [a movable]{g:mobile} supposit, as, for instance, Christ who suffered. This logic is clear in an example. For the divine nature is the Word, begotten both eternally and temporally, and yet it is not begotten; and such an [essence]{g:essencia} of [prime matter]{g:materia-prima} is the supposit of fire, composed of matter and form: and yet that matter cannot be so composed. Peter eats, dies, or is otherwise changed; and yet the [nature of the species]{g:natura-specifica}, which is Peter, cannot be so changed. Therefore in all these and like [cases] one must attend diligently to predication by essence and to formal [predication].[^10] And it is clear that it does not follow, "God is movable, and everything other [116] than God is movable: therefore every thing is movable", because some thing is not movable. And if it is asked, "What is not movable?", it can be said, equivocating, that nothing is not movable, because the divine nature is a movable: and so, understanding predication by essence, not formal, everything that is, is a movable.[^11]

## ¶3 · 116.7–117.9

> [p. 116] Quod si dicatur istam responsionem, cum sapiat contradiccionem, interimere disputaciones theologicas, cum incidit in redargucionem, que est³ meta vilissima respondentis; dicitur quod multum proficeret modum disputandi sophisticum, principaliter propter apparenciam arguentis et redargucionem⁴ patulam respondentis, ex peccato Luciferi introductum, esse⁵ in scolis theologis pretermissum.⁶ In cuius signum prima questio, quam scriptura meminit,⁷ est a dyabolo⁸ introducta. ‘Cur,⁸ inquit serpens, precipit vobis Deus ut non comederetis de omni ligno paradisi?’ Gen. 3°. Unde omnis questio attestatur indubie super ignoranciam vel peccatum. Tamen non negandum est, quin questiones theologice possunt tractari meritorie, cum Veritas quesivit a peccatoribus plurimas questiones. Oportet tamen quod utrobique intendatur Dei gloria augmentanda, sophistarum superbia destruenda, et ignota veritas detegenda: omne⁹ autem quod amplius est in disputacione theologica ‘a malo est:’ ideo questio, sicut et iuramentum, occasione vel pena peccati introducta est. Et ista est sentencia Augustini 2° De Doctrina Christiana 31°, ubi docet in disputacionibus cavere rixandi libidinem et ostentacionem frivolam puerilem. Nec¹⁰ ducunt argucie sophistarum theologos¹¹ ad metam aliam, sed detegit eorum versuciam arundineam qua prius pompaverant; et hoc ex propriis eorum principiis. Unde frequenter, ut colores sapiencie sophistice minus appareant, obvio eis negando negativas, que ad sensum equivocum satis catholice concedi potuerunt¹²; ut, dando exclusivam huius [p. 117] universalis—omnis res est mobilis,—formo sic exclusivam, ut apparencia¹ sophistica sit minus evidens—tantum res mobilis est res—quod est falsum pro rebus eternis. Concedi tamen posset quod nichil aliud quam mobile est res, accipiendo ‘mobile’ essencialiter substantive; et tamen aliud quam mobile est res, posito quod ‘mobile’ predicetur formaliter adiective. Sed Augustinus et alii sancti non curarunt de istis apparenciis sophistarum. Unde in Dialogo ad Felicianum, ‘genuit, inquit, et non genuit Maria Filium Dei.’

And if it is said that this response, since it savours of contradiction, destroys theological disputations, since it falls into [refutation]{g:redargucio}, which is the vilest goal of the respondent, the answer is that it would profit much that the sophistical manner of disputing, introduced from the sin of Lucifer chiefly for the sake of the arguer's show and the respondent's open refutation, be left aside in the schools of theology.[^12] In sign of which, the first question that scripture mentions was introduced by the devil: "Why," says the serpent, "hath God commanded you, that you should not eat of every tree of paradise?" Gen. 3. Hence every question bears witness, without doubt, to ignorance or sin. Yet it is not to be denied that theological questions can be treated meritoriously, since the Truth asked very many questions of sinners. Yet it is necessary that on both sides there be intended the glory of God, to be increased, the pride of the sophists, to be destroyed, and unknown truth, to be [exposed]{g:detegere}: and whatever is more than this in theological disputation "is of evil":[^13] therefore the question, like the oath, was introduced by occasion of sin or as its penalty. And this is the [teaching]{g:sentencia} of Augustine, De doctrina christiana 2, [chapter] 31, where he teaches that in disputations one should beware of the lust for wrangling and frivolous, childish display.[^14] Nor do the [quibbles]{g:argucie} of the sophists lead theologians to any other goal, but [the theologian] exposes their reedy cunning, in which they had before [boasted]{g:pompare};[^15] and this from their own principles. Hence frequently, so that the [colours]{g:color}[^16] of sophistical wisdom may appear less, I meet them by denying negatives that, in an equivocal sense, could quite catholicly have been granted; as, in giving the exclusive of this [117] universal, "every thing is movable", I form the exclusive thus, so that the sophistical appearance may be less evident: "only a movable thing is a thing", which is false for eternal things. Yet it could be granted that nothing other than a movable is a thing, taking "movable" essentially, as a substantive; and yet that something other than a movable is a thing, supposing that "movable" is predicated formally, as an adjective. But Augustine and the other [saints]{g:sancti} did not care about these appearances of the sophists. Hence in the Dialogue to Felicianus: "Mary," he says, "bore and did not bear the Son of God."[^17]

## ¶4 · 117.10–117.27

> [p. 117] Sed ulterius arguitur quod Verbum Dei non sit simpliciter mobile racione assumpti hominis. Nam nulla persona movetur propter habitum vel² accidens essencialiter separatum²: sed humanitas Christi est habitus Verbo Dei accidentaliter copulatus: ergo propter eius laceracionem³ vel passionem non sequitur⁴ passio Verbi Dei. Confirmatur triplici exemplo. Primo ex hoc quod homo non laceratur vel comburitur, etsi vestimenta eius taliter⁵ paciantur. 2°. ex hoc quod humanitas conceditur per tempus notabile non fuisse: quando tamen non conceditur Verbum Dei conformiter non fuisse, non ergo sequitur—si ista humanitas movebatur post a non esse acquirendo essendi terminum,⁶ tunc et Verbum—. 3°. confirmatur ex figura Abrahe patriarche, qui cum voluit Gen. 22°. immolare⁷ filium suum Ysaac, non hunc occidit sed arietem, quem vidit post tergum⁸ herentem⁸ cornibus. Cum ergo passio Christi fuit per Ysaac allegorice figurata, videtur quod non persona Christi, sed eius humanitas, fuit passa.

But further it is argued that the Word of God is not movable without qualification by reason of [the assumed man]{g:homo-assumptus}. For no person is moved on account of a habit or accident essentially separate: now the humanity of Christ is a habit accidentally joined to the Word of God: therefore on account of its laceration or passion the passion of the Word of God does not follow.[^18] This is confirmed by a threefold example. First from this, that a man is not torn or burned, even if his garments suffer so. Secondly from this, that the humanity is granted not to have been for a notable time, while yet it is not granted, correspondingly, that the Word of God was not: it does not, then, follow, "if this humanity was moved afterwards from not-being, acquiring the term of being, then the Word [was moved] too". Thirdly, this is confirmed from the figure of the patriarch Abraham, who, when he willed, Gen. 22, to sacrifice his son Isaac, did not kill him but the ram which he saw behind his back, sticking fast by the horns.[^19] Since, then, the passion of Christ was allegorically figured by Isaac, it seems that not the person of Christ, but his humanity, suffered.

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** O reads *divinitas* and *divinitate* for *deitas* and *deitate* (p. 114), against A B C; the print stands, and the two words are kept apart in English.
[^2]: **T.** *Quod est Deus*, *quod est homo* ("that which is God", "that which is man") with O, against the print's *quo* ("that by which", 114.3, 114.4), with A B C. The print makes the person the form by which it is God, while the sentence goes on to make the person the subject that has both forms (114.5) and gives the Godhead as that by which it is God formally; the argument fails without O's reading.
[^3]: **S.** Augustine on Phil. 2:6, *non rapinam*; the syllogism is not found in this form; perhaps Ep. 140.12 (PL 33, 543), *neque enim usurpationis erat, ut rapina diceretur, sed naturae inerat, ut esset aequalis*, recast, with *Tract. in Ioh.* 67.1 (*cui natura est, non rapina*) and, for matter prior *origine* to form, *Confessions* XII.29.40 (LEDGER I07c-1, untraced).
[^4]: **T.** The minor premiss and conclusion, *sed Christum ... non est rapina*, are O's alone (A B C omit them), as are *hominis et Dei Filius*; A B C also omit *semetipsum* and *dicit* (p. 114).
[^5]: **T.** *Origine*, with C O, against the print's *originem* (A B); the editor's bracketed *[cum]* (114.24), his own supplement, falls with it. With *origine* the clause construes: matter is "prior in origin" to having form.
[^6]: **S/G.** Gen. 1:2, where Douay's "void" renders *inanis*, the adjective in *exinanivit*. Augustine, *Confessions* XII, who reads the verse as *terra erat invisibilis et incomposita* (XII.3.3) and expounds it of formless matter, prior to form *origine* (XII.29.40, PL 32, 842–43) (*verified*, LEDGER I07c-2).
[^7]: **C.** *Trium naturarum quelibet*: "each one of three natures", predicated of Christ, as at the end of the preceding chapter, *Christus est tres nature* (105.6).
[^8]: **C.** *De virtute sermonis* taken with *abici*: such predications are not to be shut out of the literal sense on the ground that they are figurative.
[^9]: **C.** *Concepcio* is the grammarians' figure (syllepsis) by which one plural verb gathers several persons (*sunt unus Deus*), as *antropospatos* is the figure that ascribes a human passion to God; Wyclif's spelling of the Greek is kept.
[^10]: **C.** The pair, predication by essence and formal predication, answers the argument of 105.11 ff.: "God is movable" holds by essence, since the divine nature is the movable supposit Christ, not formally.
[^11]: **C.** *Mobile*, neuter with *natura divina* and *omne*, is substantive, "a movable", as on p. 107. O omits *quod est, est mobile*; C reads *quod est mobile* (p. 116).
[^12]: **C.** *Redargucio* is the respondent's being brought to contradiction in the disputation, his defeat. The subject of *proficeret* is the accusative and infinitive *modum disputandi sophisticum ... esse ... pretermissum*.
[^13]: **S.** Matt. 5:37, Douay "that which is over and above these, is of evil", the verse on oaths, hence "like the oath".
[^14]: **S.** Augustine, *De doctrina christiana* II.31.48 (PL 34, 58), *cavenda est libido rixandi, et puerilis quaedam ostentatio* (*verified*, LEDGER I07c-3).
[^15]: **C.** *Detegit* has no expressed subject; taken as the theologian (O reads *theologum* for *theologos*). *Versucia arundinea*: a cunning as frail as a reed.
[^16]: **C.** *Color*: a plausible ground, as in "colour of title", here the sophists' semblance of wisdom.
[^17]: **S.** Pseudo-Augustine (Vigilius of Thapsus), *Contra Felicianum Arianum* 12 (PL 42, 1167), *Genuit ergo Maria et non genuit Filium Dei* (*verified*, LEDGER I07c-4).
[^18]: **C.** *Habitus* is the "habit" of Phil. 2:7 (*habitu inventus*), a thing had as a garment is had; the objection treats the humanity so, as the Lombard's third opinion does, *Sent.* III d. 6 c. 6 (PL 192, 769–70), *velut indumento … secundum habitum* (*verified*, LEDGER I07c-5). M reads *accionem* for *laceracionem* (p. 117).
[^19]: **S.** Gen. 22:13, in Douay's words "behind his back ... sticking fast by the horns".
