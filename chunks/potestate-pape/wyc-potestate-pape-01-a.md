# *De potestate pape*, chapter 1, part 1: what power is, and the senses of the word

## Headnote

**Occasion.** The *Tractatus de potestate pape* is a treatise in twelve chapters, written in 1379
on Loserth's dating `[verify]`, after *De ecclesia* and *De veritate sacre scripture*, both of
which it cites `[verify]`. It opens with *Iam ultimo restat*, "it remains, last of all", and so
presents itself as the closing stage of a larger inquiry into the church: having treated the
church, Wyclif now asks how Christ ordered its hierarchy `[verify]` (its place in the sequence of
his *Summa theologie*). Wyclif speaks in the first person, states conclusions, raises objections
and answers them.

**The text.** Johann Loserth's edition for the Wyclif Society (1907) rests on Prague,
Metropolitan Chapter C 73 (**A**), his base, with Prague, University Library III F 11 (**B**,
early fifteenth century), which he judges closely related to A `[verify]`; Prague, University
Library XI E 3 (**C**) supplies variants only near the end `[verify]`. Loserth prints the
marginal notes of A and B; A's copyist annotates against Wyclif `[verify]`. Our copy-text is
Loserth's printed text, read against the plates. In the scripture references the chapter numbers
are Wyclif's, the verse numbers Loserth's. The side-notes are Loserth's own English, printed
verbatim. Pages 398–406 of the edition print a medieval summary of the chapters (*Sentencia libri
… in compendio*) that came down with the work `[verify]` (its witnesses); it is translated here
like the text.

**The move.** Before the pope, power. Proceeding, as he says, from what is prior after the manner
of nature, Wyclif first settles what power (*potestas*) is and in how many ways the word is said:
the active and passive potencies of *Metaphysics* V, rational potency and Anselm's free choice,
the acquired habit, and from it the twofold power in man, supernatural and civil; then, from
scripture, the angelic order of "powers", sovereign authority, and the pretended power of
darkness. Only then does he turn to Peter's primacy, the pope as vicar of Christ, papal election,
the "caesarean" pope and the endowment, and Antichrist. Richard FitzRalph, archbishop of Armagh
(*dominus Ardmacanus*), and his *Summa in quaestionibus Armenorum* are quoted and answered
throughout `[verify]`. *Potestas* is always "power". *Potencia* is "potency" where Wyclif works
Aristotle's analysis of power, and "power" elsewhere. Wyclif himself treats the two words as
interchangeable (*potestas vel potencia*).

**Where it is answered.** Loserth notes passages that Hus took over word for word into his *De
ecclesia* `[verify]`. A direct answer to the treatise has not yet been traced.

## ¶1 · 1.1–1.14

> [p. 1] Iam ultimo restat videre quomodo Christus, dominus noster, caput universalis ecclesie, ordinavit sponsam suam secundum gradus ecclesiastice hierarchie. Pro cuius declaracione necesse est primo supponere stabilimentum autoritatis scripture, de quo patet capitulo XIV libri proximi. Secundo suppono vulgatas significaciones terminorum questionis, quas alibi expono diffusius. Quibus suppositis superest tractare de partibus, de quantitate, de qualitate et habitudine moderne ecclesie ad priorem. Et cum innata sit nobis via instar nature a priori procedere, necessarium videtur scholasticum stabiliri in materia de quidditate et accidentibus potestatis.

It remains, last of all, to see how Christ our Lord, [head]{g:caput} of the universal
[church]{g:ecclesia}, ordered his [bride]{g:sponsa} according to the grades of the ecclesiastical
hierarchy. To make this clear it is necessary, first, to presuppose the establishment of the
authority of scripture, as is clear from chapter 14 of the nearest book.[^1] Second, I
presuppose the common [significations]{g:significacio} of the terms of the question, which I
expound more fully elsewhere. These being presupposed, it remains to treat of the parts, of the
quantity, of the quality and of the [condition toward]{g:habitudo} the earlier [church] of the
modern church. And since the way innate to us is, after the manner of nature, to proceed from what
is prior, it seems necessary that the student be grounded in the matter of the
[quiddity]{g:quiditas} and accidents of [power]{g:potestas}.[^2]

## ¶2 · 1.15–2.22

> [p. 1] Potestas enim est universale instrumentum operandi et communicandi in ista materia. Describitur autem potestas ab Aristotele V Metaphysice quod est principium motus vel mutacionis aut in altero aut in quantum alterum. Et [p. 2] sic est communis ad omnem activam potestatem; quo modo videtur scripturam loqui Apocalypsis IX, 3 dicente: Quod data est potestas locustis, sicut habent potestatem scorpiones terre; racione cuius potestatis tenet dominus Ardmacanus II de questionibus Armenorum, capitulo IV, quod potestas est extensior quam nature racionalis potencia. Sed quia iuxta Aristotelem (ubi supra) potestas equivocatur ad quatuor, scilicet generaliter ad activam potenciam iam descriptam, que est vis transmutandi aliquid motu proprie dicto vel mutacione quantumlibet extensiva; et sic est vis generandi atque corrumpendi. Nec solum dicitur respectu essencie extra potens sed eadem essencia, in quantum activa, dicitur habere potenciam agendi intra se ipsam, in quantum est passiva, vel racione indisposicionis contrarie aut racione terminandi quomodolibet faccionem propriam ut passiva. Sic enim est potestas rectificandi se in virga, potestas frigefaciendi se in aqua, et potestas intelligendi se in anima; et sic de similibus.

For power is the universal instrument of working and of communicating in this matter. And power
is defined by Aristotle, *Metaphysics* V, as that which "is the principle of motion or change
either in another or in so far as [it is] other".[^3] And [2] so it is common to every active
power; and in this way scripture seems to speak in Apocalypse 9:3, saying "that power was given to
the locusts, as the scorpions of the earth have power";[^4] by reason of which power the lord of
Armagh holds, in book II of the *Questions of the Armenians*, chapter 4, that "power is wider than
the [power]{g:potencia} of rational nature".[^5] But since, according to Aristotle (in the place cited), power
is said equivocally in four ways: namely, generally, of the active potency already defined, which
is the force of transforming something by motion properly so called, or by change however
extended; and so it is the force of generating and corrupting.[^6] Nor is it said only with
respect to an essence outside the one that has the power; but the same essence, in so far as it is
active, is said to have a potency of acting within itself, in so far as it is passive, either by
reason of a contrary [unfitness]{g:indisposicio} or by reason of terminating in any way its own
making, as passive.[^7] For in this way there is a power of straightening itself in a rod, a power
of cooling itself in water, and a power of understanding itself in the soul; and so of like cases.

## ¶3 · 2.23–3.2

> [p. 2] Quamvis autem transmutatum in istis non sit aliud quam ipsum transmutans, tamen vel est alterum, vel ut terminans potenciam est alterius racionis. Et sic intelligendo potenciam patens est eius divisio, cum aliqua sit increata, et aliqua potencia est creata. Increata potencia potest intelligi dupliciter, vel personaliter ut pro patre in divinis, cuius actum terminat Verbum suum, qui non est aliud sed alter, et alius quam dicta potencia, quia genitus et causatus; vel essencialiter [p. 3] ut tota trinitas est una potencia intelligendi ac volendi se ipsam.

And although what is transformed in these cases is not another thing than the transformer itself,
yet either it is other,[^8] or, as terminating the potency, it is of another [aspect]{g:racio}. And
understanding potency in this way, its division is plain, since some is uncreated, and some potency
is created.[^9] Uncreated potency can be understood in two ways: either personally, as [it is taken]
for the Father in divine matters, whose act is terminated by his Word, who is not another thing
but another, and other than the said potency, because begotten and caused; or essentially, [3] as
the whole Trinity is one potency of understanding and of willing itself.[^10]

## ¶4 · 3.3–3.10

> [p. 3] Et sic potest vocari potestas illa solum ad actus intrinsecos vel ad extra, ut est effectus extrinseci productiva. Que potestas licet non sit dispar essencia, tamen dicit disparem relacionem racionis hic et ibi. Et omnis talis potestas est increata, immensa et eterna, sicut natura divina, que est illa potencia; et sic loquitur scriptura Danielis VI, 26: Potestas eius usque in eternum.

And so that [potency] can be called power, either with respect to intrinsic acts alone or with
respect to what is outside, as it is productive of an extrinsic effect.[^11] And though this power is not a
different essence, yet it implies a different relation of reason here and there. And every such
power is uncreated, immense and eternal, as is the divine nature, which is that potency; and so
scripture speaks in Daniel 6:26: "His power shall be for ever."

## ¶5 · 3.11–3.23

> [p. 3] Potestas autem creata vel est corporalis vel spiritualis, et sic subdividendo secundum differencias activorum, in quibus sunt multe differencie, quia spiritualis potestas racionalis creature est perpetua indelebilis sed restringibilis in effectu, corporalis autem potestas est delebilis et acquiribilis, cum sit qualitas de secunda specie qualitatis non inseparabiliter connaturalis substancie cui inest. Secundo vero modo sumitur potestas in textu Aristotelis, pro passiva potencia creature, secundum quam dicitur recipere congrue activi extrinseci accionem.

And created power is either corporeal or spiritual, and so [one goes on] subdividing according to
the [differences]{g:differencia} of active things, among which there are many differences; for
the spiritual power of a rational creature is perpetual, indelible, but restrictable in effect,
while corporeal power is delible and acquirable, since it is a quality of the second species of
quality, not inseparably connatural to the substance in which it is.[^12] And in the second way
power [is taken]{g:sumitur-pro} in Aristotle's text for the passive potency of a creature,
according to which it is said to receive fittingly the action of an extrinsic agent.

## ¶6 · 3.24–5.21

> [p. 3] Sed tercio pertinencius accipitur potestas pro principio faciendi aliquid, non quocunque modo, sed bene, quod Aristoteles dicit fieri secundum voluntatem. Et ista videtur mihi potencia rationalis; de qua nono Methaphisice 30 et 10. Et illa subdividitur secundum homines et angelos bonos vel malos; et illa potestas dicitur vel intrinsece vel extrinsece, proporcionaliter ut dictum est de potencia divina; unde potencia racionalis inseparabilis [p. 4] a creatura racionali servandi rectitudinem vocatur a venerabili Anshelmo, liberum arbitrium, ut patet in libello suo De Libero Arbitrio. Liberum, inquit, arbitrium est potestas servandi rectitudinem voluntatis propter ipsam rectitudinem; tales autem potenciae impediri potuerunt sed non extingui. Verumtamen potestas vel potencia sumitur quandoque pro sufficiencia meritorie operandi, vel incitantibus resistendi, et talis per peccatum potest diminui atque tolli. Quarto vero modo accipit Aristoteles potestatem sive potenciam pro habitu accidenter aquisito, secundum quod eius subiectum potens est alterius mutatum, ut ars medicinalis vel alii habitus operandi. Nec distat multum sufficiencia iam dicta ab ista potencia. Secundum hoc itaque inest homini duplex maneries potestatis, scilicet supernaturalis et civilis. Supernaturalis est potestas, quam Deus dat homini ad spiritualiter proficiendum ecclesie, cuiusmodi est potestas sacerdotalis in conficiendo vel ministrando sacramenta aut alia spiritualia bona ecclesie. Potestas autem civilis est potestas dominativa ad cohercendum rebelles ecclesie secundum penam sensibilem et dicit libertatem in potentatu et subdicione in extrinseco terminante. Et isto modo videtur Salvator dicere Math. XX, 25: Principes gencium dominantur eorum; et qui maiores sunt potestatem exercent in eos. Et Jeremie XXXIV, 9 refert Propheta quomodo Judei dimiserant servos suos et ancillas, ut essent liberi sue potestatis; quando [p. 5] enim homo habet libertatem sine coactivo preposito ad faciendum cum suis quod foret sibi expedicius, tunc dicitur sub nullius redigi potestate, ut loquitur Apostolus I. Corinth. VI, 12; et sic videtur Apostolum loqui I. Corinth. VII, 4 quando dicit quod coniuges non habent sui corporis potestatem, eo quod oportet eos ex debito servire sibi reciproce in actibus carnalibus, quando esset utilius contemplari. Et sic servitus et obligacio ad carnalem copulam in coniugio sunt status imperfecti quodammodo. Preter istas significaciones sumitur potestas in scriptura nunc pro sexto ordine angelorum, ut Colos. I, 16 et aliquando pro ipso potentatu, ut Rom. XIII, Omnis anima potestatibus sublimioribus subdita sit. Et quandoque sumitur equivoce pro potestate pretensa vel false palliata, ut Luce XXII, 53 dicit Christus suis captoribus: Hec est hora vestra et potestas tenebrarum. Et istas equivocaciones potestatum oportet diligenter notare secundum pertinencia materie ventilate.

But in the third way, and more pertinently, power is taken for the principle of doing something,
not in any manner whatever, but well, which Aristotle says "comes about according to
will".[^13] And this seems to me to be rational potency, of which [he treats in] *Metaphysics* IX,
3 and 1.[^14] And it is subdivided according to men and angels, good or evil; and that power is
spoken of either intrinsically or extrinsically, proportionally as was said of the divine potency;
hence the rational potency of keeping rectitude, inseparable [4] from a rational creature, is
called by the venerable Anselm [free choice]{g:liberum-arbitrium}, as is clear in his little book
*On Free Choice*. "Free choice," he says, "is the power of keeping rectitude of will for the sake
of rectitude itself";[^15] and such powers could be hindered but not extinguished.
Nevertheless, power or potency[^16] is sometimes taken for the sufficiency to work meritoriously, or to
resist those who incite [to sin], and such [power] can be diminished and taken away through sin.
And in the fourth way Aristotle takes power or potency for a habit accidentally acquired, according
to which its subject, [itself] changed, is capable of [changing] another, as the art of medicine or
other habits of working.[^17] And the sufficiency just mentioned differs little from this potency.
Accordingly, then, there is in man a twofold kind of power, namely supernatural and civil.
Supernatural power is the power which God gives a man for profiting the church spiritually, of
which kind is priestly power in confecting or ministering the sacraments or other spiritual goods
of the church. And civil power is lordly power for [coercing]{g:cohercere} rebels against the
church by [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} penalty, and it implies freedom in the one who holds
sovereignty and subjection in the extrinsic party in which it terminates.[^18] And in this way the Saviour seems to
speak in Matthew 20:25: "The princes of the Gentiles [lord it over]{g:dominari} them; and they that
are the greater, exercise power upon them." And in Jeremiah 34:9 the prophet relates how the Jews
had let their menservants and maidservants go, "that they might be free, in their own power"; for
[5] when a man has freedom, without a [coactive]{g:coactivus} superior, to do with his own what
would be more expedient to him, then he is said to be "brought under the power of none", as the
Apostle speaks in 1 Corinthians 6:12; and so the Apostle seems to speak in 1 Corinthians 7:4 when
he says that "spouses have not power of their own body", because they must, out of the
[marriage debt]{g:debitum}, serve one another in carnal acts when it would be more profitable to contemplate. And so servitude
and obligation to carnal union in marriage are in a certain way imperfect states.[^19] Besides
these significations, power is taken in scripture at one time for the sixth order of angels, as in
Colossians 1:16, and at another for sovereignty itself, as in Romans 13[:1], "Let every soul be
subject to higher powers."[^20] And sometimes it is taken [equivocally]{g:equivoce} for a power
pretended or falsely [cloaked]{g:palliare}, as in Luke 22:53 Christ says to his captors: "This is
your hour and the power of darkness." And these equivocations of powers must be carefully noted,
according to what pertains to the matter under discussion.

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 1.1: Our subject is: What kind of hierarchy Christ ordained for his Church.
- 1.9: We must first make clear what power is and what are its accidents.
- 1.15: Power is that by which we effect movement or change in another.
- 2.8: Various meanings assigned to 'power' by Aristotle.
- 2.23: It may be created or uncreated.
- 3.8: Created power may be corporal or spiritual.
- 3.20: 2. Power may be passive, responding to active power.
- 3.24: 3. Power is what enables us to do things well; i. e. according to our wishes.
- 4.1: The inalienable power of doing right is called by Anselm Free will.
- 4.11: 4. Power may be an acquired accident, e. g. medicinal skill.
- 4.18: It may be supernatural, as in the ministration of the sacraments, or civil, inflicting penalties on rebels to the Church,
- 5.11: Besides all these meanings 'power' denotes an order of angels,

## Notes

[^1]: **S/T.** *capitulo XIV libri proximi*, "chapter 14 of the nearest book". Loserth takes
    *libri proximi* for a scribal error: the nearest book is *De officio regis*, which treats only
    royal power, while the establishing of scripture's authority runs through *De veritate sacre
    scripture* (ed. Buddensieg). Which chapter 14 is meant is to be checked (LEDGER P01a-1).

[^2]: **G/S.** *Quiditas*, "quiddity", is what a thing is; its accidents are what belong to it
    beyond that. *A priori procedere* is "to proceed from what is prior", not the modern
    epistemic sense. The phrase *innata … nobis via* recalls Aristotle, *Physics* I.1 (*innata
    autem est nobis via ex notioribus nobis*), to be checked (LEDGER P01a-2).

[^3]: **S.** Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Δ 12 (Wyclif's book V). Loserth: "rectius Metaph. IV, 12, ed.
    acad. Borussica" (*principium motus aut transmutacionis in altero aut prout alterum est*); the
    modern form Δ 12, 1019a15–20 is ours (LEDGER P01a-3).

[^4]: **S.** Wyclif has *data est potestas locustis*; the Vulgate *data est illis potestas* (DR
    "power was given to them").

[^5]: **S/G.** FitzRalph, *Summa in quaestionibus Armenorum* II.4, as Wyclif cites it; Loserth
    could not check it ("mihi ad manum non est") (LEDGER P01a-4). The Latin is *potestas est
    extensior quam nature racionalis potencia*. The comparison is of extent (power in general, which
    the locusts have, against the power of rational nature), not of two concepts, so *potencia* is
    "power" here. Wyclif treats *potestas* and *potencia* as interchangeable in this chapter (see n.
    16).

[^6]: **C.** *Sed quia … potestas equivocatur ad quatuor* has no main clause: the period breaks
    off after the first sense, and the count resumes at 3.20 (*Secundo*), 3.24 (*tercio*) and 4.11
    (*Quarto*). The English keeps the break.

[^7]: **C/S.** *extra potens*, "outside the one that has the power": the patient distinct from the
    agent, as against the self-acting case that follows. Loserth refers this line (2.16) to
    Aristotle, *Metaph.* VIII, 1, 2 (= Θ 1, 1046a, our modern form) (LEDGER P01a-5).

[^8]: **C.** *aliud … alterum*: "another thing" (a different nature) as against "other" (the same
    thing taken otherwise), the distinction applied to the Trinity at 2.29–3.1.

[^9]: **T.** B lacks *et aliqua potencia est creata* (2.27–28); the print is translated.

[^10]: **C.** *qui non est aliud sed alter*: the Son is "not another thing but another", i.e. not a
    different nature but a different person. *Causatus*, "caused", is Wyclif's word for the Son and
    is kept.

[^11]: **C.** *Et sic potest vocari potestas illa*: we take *illa* (the uncreated *potencia* of
    2.26–3.2) as the subject and *potestas* as the predicate, so that Wyclif says the potency just
    divided may be called power. The alternative takes *potestas illa* together as the subject ("that
    power can be called [so]"). In God the "potency" of this analysis is active power, terminated by
    the Word, not unrealised potentiality; it stays "potency" (here and at 3.9, 3.31) because Wyclif
    is carrying Aristotle's division into the Trinity.

[^12]: **S/T.** The second species of quality is natural capacity or incapacity (Aristotle,
    *Categories* 8, 9a14), to be checked (LEDGER P01a-6). *Indelebilis* is Loserth's form (codd.
    *indelibilis*); for *delebilis* (3.16) B reads *debilis*, "weak".

[^13]: **S/T.** Aristotle, *Metaph.* Δ 12, 1019a23–26 (*kata proairesin*); Loserth refers 3.26 to
    *Metaph.* VIII, 1 (LEDGER P01a-7). B lacks *tercio* (3.24).

[^14]: **T.** The print has *nono Methaphisice 30 et 10* (checked on the plate); we take *30* and *10*
    for the ordinals 3° and 1°, the ° printed as a zero, chapters. Loserth: "Rectius Metaph. VIII, 2"
    (= Θ 2, 1046b, on rational potencies; our modern form) (LEDGER P01a-8).

[^15]: **S.** Anselm, *De libertate arbitrii* 3, to be checked (LEDGER P01a-9); Loserth gives
    Anselm, *Opera* (Paris, 1721), p. 118 (LEDGER P01a-10).

[^16]: **C.** *potestas vel potencia*, "power or potency" (so *potestatem sive potenciam*, 4.11):
    Wyclif doubles the two words as synonyms, as he calls the uncreated *potencia* *potestas* (3.3) and
    says every such *potestas* "is the divine nature, which is that *potencia*" (3.9). *Potestas* is
    always "power"; *potencia* is "potency" inside Aristotle's analysis and "power" elsewhere (Anselm's
    *tales potencie* just above, "such powers").

[^17]: **C.** *potens est alterius mutatum* does not construe cleanly: "its subject, changed, is
    capable of another". The bracketed words give the likelier sense (Aristotle's potency in
    another); *mutativum*, "capable of changing", may have been intended. Loserth records no
    variant.

[^18]: **T.** Loserth prints *et subdicione*; the manuscripts read *subdicionem* (4.27). The English
    follows the manuscripts: the print's ablative does not construe (it would put "subjection" under
    *in* beside "freedom", "freedom in sovereignty and in subjection"), while the accusative makes
    subjection a second thing that civil power implies, lodged in the party subjected. *Potentatus*
    is therefore concrete here, "the one who holds sovereignty", in parallel with *in extrinseco
    terminante*. Loserth also reports *Codd.: vel ut* at 4.21, where his print has *vel*
    (*conficiendo vel ministrando*).

[^19]: **S.** Jeremiah 34:9 tells of the release; the phrase *ut essent liberi et suae potestatis*
    is from 34:16 (DR "let go free, and set at liberty"), here without *et*. 1 Corinthians 6:12 Vg
    *sed ego sub nullis redigar potestate* (DR "I will not be brought under the power of any");
    7:4 is paraphrased.

[^20]: **R.** Loserth notes that Hus's *De ecclesia* (fol. 217b) gives 5.11–19 word for word
    (LEDGER P01a-11). *Potentatus* is rendered "sovereignty" (5.13) and, of its holder, "the one who
    holds sovereignty" (4.27).
