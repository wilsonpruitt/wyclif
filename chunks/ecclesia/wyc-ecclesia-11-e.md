# *De ecclesia*, chapter 11, part 5: common law and privilege; Christ's privileges and the temporal lord's part; whether sin is a privilege

## ¶1 · 246.26–248.20

> [p. 246] Sed quia videtur per verba decreti quod membra coincidunt, licet racio non sit diversa, ideo notandum quod lex potest dici communis dupliciter, scilicet obiectiva obligacione vel formali predicacione; primo [p. 247] modo est lex communissima quod Deus est super omnia appetendus, cum nedum omnem creaturam racionalem sed omnem creatam substanciam semper obligat. Nam Deus est bonum quod omnia appetunt, et sic omnia alia mandata decalogi sunt leges communes obiective, cum necessario omnes homines obligant. Leges autem communes predicacione sunt species vel genera legum que in suis individuis sunt fundata, ut clericum curare de sua fama et genera similia, sive accepta pro legum privatarum multitudine, sive pro universalibus quiditative predicatis de sua specie. Et sic iste due maneries legum non sunt contrarie in suo analogo, cum canon describit legem publicam que a sanctis patribus scriptis est confirmata; quod notum est competere cuilibet vero privilegio, sicut et descripcio privilegii competit legi communi. Unde illa lex que est communis private parti ecclesie est privilegium respectu sui tocius, ut lex que est communis singulis personis dati monasterii monachorum est privilegium toti domui, et dotacio in temporalibus, si est lex communis toti clero possessionato et singulis personis eiusdem, tunc est privilegium respectu persone cleri dotate, eo quod persone cleri exproprietarie viventi non convenit sed privatim persone priori. Cum ergo iuxta decretum omnis lex privata a spiritu sancto inscribitur, manifestum est quod omnis lex privata concessa est a principe ad utilitatem ecclesie vel persone, eo quod opera trinitatis sunt indivisa ad extra. Deus autem nescit docere vel dare nisi utilia, et sic omnis lex creata individua est privilegium. Et patet quomodo loquendum est logice de privilegiis, detecta equivocacione termini, ut oportet. Ad secundam instanciam negatur assumptum, cum Christus dedit ecclesie duplex genus privilegii, quoddam [p. 248] extrinsecum, affirmativum, negativum vel privativum, ut Matthei X°, 9, dicitur quomodo privilegiavit discipulos, quando misit illos ad predicandum: Nolite, inquit, possidere aurum neque argentum neque pecuniam in zonis vestris. Privilegia autem corporalia positiva concedit eis, ut patet Luce X°, 7: In eadem, inquit, domo manete edentes et bibentes que aput illos sunt; dignus est enim operarius mercede sua. Talia autem privilegia instrumentaliter et ministratorie concedi possunt a domino temporali; sed est dare spiritualia privilegia pociora que Christus prius concessit suis discipulis, ut patet Matthei X, 8: Infirmos, inquit, curate, mortuos suscitate, leprosos mundate, demones eicite, Talia autem potestativa privilegia appropriate conceduntur a domino, que licet princeps terrenus non posset concedere, tamen concedendo temporalia privilegia propriatorie potest disponere ut Deus concedat privilegia pociora, sicut aperiens fenestram vel removens prohibens est occasio, unde lumen solis per domum infunditur.

But because it seems from the words of the decree that the members [of the division] coincide, although the aspect is not different, it must therefore be noted that a law can be called common in two ways: namely, by objective obligation or by formal predication.[^1] In the first [247] way the most common law is that God is to be desired above all things, since it binds always not only every rational creature but every created substance. For God is the good which all things desire; and so all the other commandments of the decalogue are common laws objectively, since they necessarily bind all men. But laws common by predication are the species or genera of laws which are founded in their individuals, such as that a cleric should take care of his reputation, and similar genera, whether taken for the multitude of private laws or for universals predicated of their species as to their [quiddity]{g:quiditas}.[^2] And so these two kinds of law are not contrary in their analogue, since the canon defines public law as that which has been confirmed by the writings of the holy fathers; and this is known to fit every true privilege, just as the [definition]{g:descripcio} of privilege fits common law.[^3] Hence that law which is common to a private part of the [church]{g:ecclesia} is a [privilege]{g:privilegium} with respect to its whole: as a law which is common to the individual persons of a given monastery of monks is a privilege to the whole house; and an [endowment]{g:dotacio} in temporal things, if it is a law common to the whole [possessioner]{g:possessionati} clergy and to its individual persons, is then a privilege with respect to the endowed person of the clergy, because it does not belong to the person of the clergy that lives [without property]{g:exproprietarie}, but privately to the former person.[^4] Since, therefore, according to the decree every private law is written in by the Holy Spirit, it is manifest that every private law is granted by a prince for the benefit of the church or of a person, because the works of the Trinity outwards are undivided. Now God knows not how to teach or give anything but what is beneficial; and so every created individual law is a privilege. And it is clear how one must speak logically of privileges, once the equivocation of the term has been exposed, as is fitting.[^5] To the second [objection]{g:instancia} the [assumption]{g:assumptum} is denied, since Christ gave the church a twofold kind of privilege: one [248] extrinsic, affirmative, negative or privative, as it is said in Matthew 10:9 how he privileged the disciples when he sent them to preach: "Do not," he says, "possess gold, nor silver, nor money in your purses." And positive bodily privileges he grants them, as is clear in Luke 10:7: "In the same house," he says, "remain, eating and drinking such things as they have: for the labourer is worthy of his hire." Now such privileges can be granted instrumentally and ministerially by a [temporal lord]{g:temporales domini}; but there are spiritual privileges, of greater worth, which Christ granted his disciples before, as is clear in Matthew 10:8: "Heal," he says, "the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out devils."[^6] And such privileges of power are granted appropriately by the Lord; and although an earthly prince could not grant them, yet by granting temporal privileges he can, preparatorily, dispose [things] so that God grants the privileges of greater worth; as one who opens a window, or removes what hinders, is the occasion whereby the light of the sun is poured through the house.[^7]

## ¶2 · 248.21–249.2

> [p. 248] Ritus ergo sensibiles quos Christus instituit tamquam signa efficacia spiritualis privilegii sunt diligencius observandi, cum impossibile sit, ut rite observans hec privilegia sensibilia non privilegietur a Deo intrinsecus per privilegia pociora. Ideo (ut dixi proximo capitulo) sunt diligencius quam humanitus adinventa privilegia observanda, sicut patet in parabola domini de Recabitis a vino abstinentibus ex precepto patris sui Recab (ut exponit: Jeremie XXXV, 8). Et hec racio quare Christi privilegia sunt diligencius audienda, reccius exponenda et solicicius defendenda, quia ut illa spernuntur innitendo ad alia, perit religio christiana; et sic domini temporales tenentur privilegia [p. 249] Christi tam positiva quam privativa defendere et omnes ritus illis contrarios animose destruere.

The [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} rites, therefore, which Christ instituted as efficacious signs of spiritual privilege are to be observed more diligently, since it is impossible that one who duly observes these perceptible privileges should not be [privileged]{g:privilegiare} by God inwardly through privileges of greater worth. Therefore (as I said in the preceding chapter) they are to be observed more diligently than privileges devised by men, as is clear in the Lord's parable of the Rechabites, who abstained from wine by the precept of their father Rechab (as [the prophet] sets it out, Jeremiah 35:8).[^8] And this is the reason why the privileges of Christ are to be heard more diligently, expounded more rightly and defended more carefully: because, as they are spurned by leaning on others, the Christian religion perishes. And so temporal lords are bound to defend the privileges [249] of Christ, both positive and privative, and spiritedly to destroy all rites contrary to them.[^9]

## ¶3 · 249.3–249.34

> [p. 249] Unde videtur mihi quod collegium predicte abbathie primo attenderet, si vendicatum privilegium sit privilegiis Christi contrarium. Secundo quod lucrum reportabit ex privilegio sic defenso, quia indubie si non meritum quoad Deum sed nude mundi gloriam ex privilegio reportat anime detrimentum. Tercio, supposito quod reportet ex operibus misericordie indultis profugis salutem anime, attenderet cum suis fautoribus quod pro veris privilegiis plus necessariis defendendis diligenciam tantam apponerent vel maiorem. Sepe nempe mundo decepti cece preponderant folia fructui, umbram luci, ymmo venenum pruriens temporale perpetue saluti. Ad tercium dicitur quod falsum assumitur, nam peccatum cum non sit privata lex nec concessum principe non est privilegium. Quamvis enim princeps huius mundi excitat ad peccandum, non tamen potest concedere, sicut nec Deus, ad illud licenciam, et si per impossibile concederet ad hoc vel pretense licenciam, hoc non faceret ad privilegiandi commodum sed ad dampnum. Conceditur tamen ex immensitate potestatis veri principis quod racione cuiuscunque peccati occasionatur ecclesie Christi verum privilegium, cum timentibus Deum omnia cooperantur in bonum Rom. VIII°, 28; non quod peccatum sit eis privilegium, sed quod profectus de peccato occasionaliter ortus cedat eis in privilegium. Et hoc est verum non solum de alienis peccatis sed de peccatis propriis que predestinati perpetrant. Conceditur tamen quod pena cedit filiis in privilegium, cum stimulus carnis a Deo datus apostolo cessit sibi ad virtutis exercicium, ut patet IIa Cor. XII°.

Hence it seems to me that the college of the aforesaid abbey should attend, first, to whether the privilege claimed is contrary to the privileges of Christ. Second, to what gain it will carry away from the privilege so defended; because undoubtedly, if it carries away from the privilege not merit with respect to God but barely the glory of the world, it carries away harm to the soul. Third, supposing that it carries away the salvation of the soul from the works of mercy granted to the refugees, it should attend, with its supporters, to [this]: that they should apply as much diligence, or greater, to defending the true privileges, which are more necessary.[^10] For often those deceived by the world blindly weigh the leaves above the fruit, the shadow above the light, indeed an itching temporal poison above everlasting salvation. To the third [objection] [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that a falsehood is assumed; for sin, since it is neither a private law nor granted by a prince, is not a privilege. For although the prince of this world stirs [men] up to sin, yet he cannot, any more than God can, grant licence for it; and if, per impossibile, he granted licence for it, even a pretended one, he would do this not for the advantage but for the harm of the one privileged.[^11] Yet it is granted, from the immensity of the power of the true prince, that by reason of any sin whatever a true privilege is occasioned to the church of Christ, since "to them that fear God all things work together unto good" (Romans 8:28): not that sin is a privilege to them, but that the profit arising [by occasion]{g:occasionaliter} from sin turns to their privilege. And this is true not only of others' sins but of the sins of their own which the [predestinate]{g:predestinatus} commit. Yet it is granted that punishment turns to privilege for the sons, since the sting of the flesh, given by God to the Apostle, turned for him to the exercise of virtue, as is clear in 2 Corinthians 12[:7].[^12]

## ¶4 · 250.1–250.16

> [p. 250] Unde sancti plus contentantur de tribulacione quam de mundana prosperitate propter securitatem et fructum meriti ampliorem, dampnati autem habent quoddam bonum ultra bonum nature ex merito Christi et sua punicione iusta; quod bonum, licet sit bonum utile beatis et honestum domino punienti, non tamen videtur mihi quod illud bonum sit eis privilegium utile vel honestum sed bonum mitigativum malicie vel quomodocunque aliter apcius nominetur. Unde licet sit privata lex concessa a principe, non tamen ad utilitatem persone cui inest formaliter, nec contendo sed concedo ad sensum equivocum quod illa dampnacio est privilegium beatis, cum racione iuste punicionis suorum hostium qui serviunt eis perpetuo suum gaudium quodam modo maioratur.

Hence the saints are more content with tribulation than with worldly prosperity, because of the security and the ampler fruit of merit; and the damned have a certain good beyond the good of nature, from the merit of Christ and their own just punishment; and this good, although it is a useful good to the blessed and an honourable one to the Lord who punishes, yet it does not seem to me that it is a useful or honourable privilege to them, but a good that mitigates malice, or however else it may more aptly be named. Hence, although it is a private law granted by a prince, yet it is not for the benefit of the person in whom it formally inheres. Nor do I contend [against it], but I grant, in an equivocal sense, that that damnation is a privilege to the blessed, since by reason of the just punishment of their enemies, who serve them perpetually, their joy is in a manner increased.[^13]

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 246.26: Law is common in two senses; of obligation and assertion.
- 247.17: A law may be common as to a part, yet a privilege in respect of the whole.
- 248.1: b) It is true that the highest privileges are the gift of God only,
- 248.13: yet the ruler may so employ earthly privileges as to make the Church apt to receive higher gifts.
- 248.21: Rites or privileges instituted by Christ are to be specially observed.

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** B in the margin *Lex communis dupliciter*; A *Lex dicitur communis dupliciter* (246.25); side-note 246.26. *Membra*: the members of a division (public and private law in *Due sunt*, 11-d n. 14), not members of the church. *Licet racio non sit diversa*: so the print; "although the aspect (definition) is not different" sits oddly with *licet*, and *licet racio sit diversa* ("although their account differs") would be easier; the print is translated. *Obiectiva obligacione*: common by the range of what the law binds; *formali predicacione*: common as a universal is predicated of many.

[^2]: **C/T.** B reads *Deus bonum* (247.4). A and A₁ read *clericum creare* (247.9). *Clericum curare de sua fama*: an example of a law-type instantiated in individual cases. *Quiditative predicatis*: predicated as what the species is (in quid).

[^3]: **S/C.** The canon is C. 19 q. 2 c. 2, *Due sunt*, as in 11-d n. 14 (LEDGER E11e-1). *In suo analogo*: in the common term of which both are said analogically; *maneries*: kinds. *Quod notum est competere …*: every true privilege is also, in the canon's sense, confirmed by the fathers' writings, so the two members overlap.

[^4]: **C/T.** Side-note 247.17. B omits *dati … personis* (247.19). *Persona cleri dotate*: the endowed clergy taken as one person (body); *persone priori*: "the former person", i.e. the endowed clergy, as against the clergy living without property. The point: endowment is common to all possessioners, yet a privilege relative to the whole clergy.

[^5]: **S/C.** *Iuxta decretum*: *Due sunt* again (private law written in the heart by the Holy Spirit). *A principe*: here God, the giver of the private law. *Opera trinitatis sunt indivisa ad extra*: the axiom in its scholastic form; in substance Augustine, *De Trinitate* I.4.7, *sicut inseparabiles sunt, ita inseparabiliter operentur* (PL 42:824) (LEDGER E11e-2). *Omnis lex creata individua*: every individual created law.

[^6]: **S/C.** Side-note 248.1. Matthew 10:9, Luke 10:7 (Wyclif omits *autem*) and Matthew 10:8, as the Vulgate; DR. *Privativum*: the privilege of not possessing; *positiva*: food and lodging. *Est dare*: "there are" (one can point to). *Ministratorie*: as a minister, serving.

[^7]: **T/C.** A wrote *appropriata* and corrected it to *appropriate* (248.15). For *propriatorie* (248.17; so printed, plate), not a word, A and A₁ read *preparatorie*, which the side-note's "make the Church apt to receive" supports; translated "preparatorily". *Removens prohibens*: the scholastic "remover of an impediment", a cause only by accident, here an occasion (cf. Aristotle, *Physics* VIII.4; to be checked, LEDGER E11e-3). Side-note 248.13.

[^8]: **S/R.** A in the margin *Conclusio. Ritus* (248.21); B *Ritus sensibiles a Christo instituti sunt observandi plus quam alii* (248.24); side-note 248.21. *Proximo capitulo*: the place in cap. 10 is to be checked (LEDGER E11e-4). Jeremiah 35:8: the precept was Jonadab's, "the son of Rechab, our father" (DR); Wyclif names the father Rechab. A wrote *Racabitis* and corrected it (248.28). *Ut exponit*: subject unexpressed; taken as the prophet.

[^9]: **C.** *Ut illa spernuntur*: "as (in so far as) they are spurned". *Religio christiana*: the Christian religion, not *religio privata*.

[^10]: **C/T.** A in the margin *Nota tria* (249.3). *Predicte abbathie*: Westminster. *Reportabit … reportat*: "carry away" as gain. B omits *detrimentum … salutem* (249.8), so losing the end of the second point and the opening of the third. *Indultis profugis*: granted (as indulgence) to the refugees.

[^11]: **C.** *Ad tercium*: the third argument (246.1): every sin is a privilege. *Concessum principe*: ablative without *a*. *Vel pretense*: "even in pretence". *Privilegiandi*: of the one to be privileged.

[^12]: **S.** Romans 8:28: the Vulgate has *diligentibus Deum* ("to them that love God", DR); Wyclif's *timentibus Deum* is translated. 2 Corinthians 12:7, *stimulus carnis* (DR "a sting of my flesh"). A in the margin *Nota* (249.34). *Filiis*: the sons of God.

[^13]: **C/R.** Side-note 250.1. *Ex merito Christi et sua punicione iusta*: the good the damned have is that of being justly punished. *Honestum domino punienti*: honourable to the Lord in that he punishes justly. *Mitigativum malicie*: restraining their malice. *Nec contendo*: "nor do I contend [otherwise]". After *maioratur* A adds two lines of Czech verse, which Loserth renders "In England they imprison two faithful priests for the word of God" (250.16); a Bohemian scribe's addition, not Wyclif's (LEDGER E11e-5).
