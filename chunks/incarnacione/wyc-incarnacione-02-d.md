# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 2, part 4: Christ the most abject creature and the best person; Nestorius; "if you loved me, you would be glad"

## ¶1 · 25.1–26.3

> [p. 25] Sed obicitur quod dico horribilius Arrianis, quia ipsi dixerunt Christum esse primam et optimam creaturam; ego autem dico Christum esse materiam primam, et sic¹ abiectissimam creaturam. Hic dicitur quod non sequitur. Nam Christus est optimus homo possibilis, inter omnes creaturas particulares optima, in tantum quod est melior angelo²; et per consequens melior² illa creatura, si esset, quam³ finxit Arrius nimis stolide esse⁴ primam creaturam Dei, mediante qua⁵ Deus creavit Spiritum Sanctum et consequenter alias creaturas. Nam illa creatura non foret melior creato angelo: de Christo autem dicit Apostolus ad Hebr. 1° quod ‘est tanto melior angelis effectus quanto differencius pre illis nomen hereditavit.’ Et si obicitur per illud Ps. 8⁶ dictum de Christo ad litteram⁷ ‘minuisti eum paulo minus ab angelis’; dicitur quod Christus, sicut est natura increata et natura creata, sic secundum naturam creatam est simul⁸ tam natura corporea quam natura incorporea;⁹ secundum autem naturam corpoream⁹ est ‘paululum minoratus ab angelis,’ sicut¹⁰ sentenciat satis¹¹ psalmus; et secundum creatum spiritum per graciam unionis angelos¹² antecellit. Conceditur tamen¹³ quod abiectissima creatura que est Christus Deus, licet sit natura inferior quam angelus vel anima humana, est tamen optima et suprema persona possibilis: cum omnis natura que est filius¹⁴ Marie virginis hominis¹⁵ est et¹⁶ Filius Dei; et e contra omnis natura, que est Filius¹⁴ Dei, est filius hominis; quia natura divina est filius hominis, ut patet ex confessione beati Petri Mat. 16. querente Veritate, ‘Quem dicunt¹⁷ homines esse filium hominis?’ et respondente Petro de eadem humanitate que est Christus, de qua fit questio. ‘Tu es, inquit, Filius Dei vivi,’ intelligens indubie filium naturalem. Unde Veritas [p. 26] approbando responsionem¹ adiunxit premium, et monet eum:² ‘Beatus es, inquit, Symon Bariona, quia caro et sanguis non revelavit tibi, sed Pater meus.’

But [it is objected]{g:obicitur} that I say something more horrible than the Arians, since they said "Christ is the first and best [creature]{g:creatura}", but I say "Christ is [prime matter]{g:materia-prima}, and so the most abject creature". [The answer]{g:dicitur} here is that it does not [follow]{g:sequitur}. For Christ is the best man possible, the best among all particular creatures, so much so that he is better than an angel; and consequently better than that creature, if there were one, which Arius [exceedingly]{g:nimis} stupidly feigned to be the first creature of God, by means of which God created the Holy Spirit and consequently the other creatures.[^1] For that creature would not be better than a created angel; but of Christ the Apostle says, Hebrews 1, that he "is made so much better than the angels as he hath inherited a more excellent name than they." And if [it is objected, by appeal to]{g:obicitur-per} that [text] of Psalm 8, said of Christ literally, "Thou hast made him a little less than the angels", the answer is that Christ, as he is uncreated [nature]{g:natura} and created nature, so as to the created nature he is at once both bodily nature and incorporeal nature; but as to the bodily nature he is "made a little lower than the angels", as the psalm sufficiently [pronounces]{g:sentenciare}; and as to the created spirit, by the [grace of union]{g:gracia-unionis}, he excels the angels.[^2] Yet it is granted that the most abject creature which is Christ God, although it is a nature lower than an angel or a human soul, is yet the best and supreme [person]{g:persona} possible: since every nature that is the son of the virgin Mary, of man, is also the Son of God; and conversely every nature that is the Son of God is the son of man; for the divine nature is the son of man,[^3] as is clear from the confession of blessed Peter, Matthew 16, when the Truth asked, "Whom do men say that the Son of man is?" and Peter answered about the same [humanity]{g:humanitas} which is Christ, about which the question is put: "Thou art," he says, "the Son of the living God," understanding without doubt the natural son. Hence the Truth, [26] approving the answer, added a reward, and admonishes him: "Blessed art thou," he says, "Simon Bar-Jona: because flesh and blood hath not revealed it to thee, but my Father."[^4]

## ¶2 · 26.4–28.15

> [p. 26] Et per hoc destruitur heresis Nestoriana arguens ex diversitate naturarum in Christo quod sit diversitas personarum. Nesciunt enim quomodo humanitas assumpta et natura divina tam dispares sunt utraque³ eadem persona cum Verbo Dei, ut ostendit Anselmus de Incarnacione Verbi cap. 6.⁴ et sic posuit alium esse filium hominis et alium Filium Dei; sicut aliud est filius hominis et aliud Filius Dei; sicut dicit superius Augustinus. Et patet quantum differt ista posicio ab heresi Arriana et Nestoriana, cum quibus videretur minus intelligentibus maxime concordare. Et si obicitur quod non tollitur, quin Christus sit creatura⁵ et abiectissima, quod⁵ non posuit Arrius; concedo conclusionem. Unde inter alia, que accenderent⁶ virtutes theologicas in nobis⁷ viantibus, maxime videtur mihi debere gignere⁸ illa summa minoracio, illa summa exinanicio, illa summa⁹ obediencia vel humiliacio qua¹⁰ Verbum, manens substancialiter complete et eternaliter quod erat, assumpsit ypostatice quod prius non erat. ‘Nusquam¹¹ enim angelos apprehendit’ secundum Apostolum ad Hebr. 2°. sicut ficte garriunt Arriani; sed ‘semen Abrahe’¹² tam secundum naturam corruptibilem quam secundum¹³ naturam immortalem ‘ut fieret per omnia fratribus similis’: dicente Apostolo ubi supra, ‘non confunditur¹⁴ fratres eos vocare, dicens “narrabo nomen tuum fratribus meis”’: et sequitur ‘nusquam enim angelos apprehendit, sed semen Abrahe apprehendit: unde debuit per omnia fratribus similari, ut misericors fieret et fidelis pontifex ad Deum.’ Ex quo textu patet quod fides nostra viresceret ex hoc quod naturam nostram fragilem non pure angelicam dignatur [p. 27] suscipere. 2° cum non¹ potest frustrari, patet quod ex assumpcione nostre nature, quos vocat¹ fratres suos passus et temptatus² ad nobis auxiliandum, invalesceret spes³ humana. Et 3° visa humiliacione qua Dei Patris primogenitus naturalis dignatus est naturam nostram fragilem et abiectam assumere fortificaretur caritas ad ipsum et fratres suos in ipso⁴ carius diligendum. Non ergo iniuriatur nobis nec debet vilescere quod nos sic univit, non angelos; nec ab honore Patris degenerat, nec⁵ fundatur in ipso⁶ remissior nobis diligendi racio.⁷ Sed per omnia e converso. Unde Ioh. 14. vere dicit, ‘si diligeretis me, gauderetis utique quia vado ad Patrem.’ Pro cuius declaracione suppono: primo quod Christus, hoc dicens⁸ secundum humanitatem assumptam,⁹ sit eiusdem nature nobiscum. 2° suppono¹⁰ quod apostoli, quibus locutus est et alii sermones eius servantes sint nedum servi Domini sed amici. Patet per¹¹ illud Ioh. 15¹² ‘Vos amici mei estis si feceritis que precipio vobis: iam non dicam vos servos etc.’ 3° suppono quod de racione amicicie sit, cum amicus sit alter ipse, congratulari de prosperis coamici, specialiter si exinde¹³ speratur amicicie fructus uberior. Patet per Aristotelem 8 Ethic 5.¹⁴ Ex istis sic arguitur. Quilibet amicus debet gaudere de eventu prospero coamici amiciciam confirmante; sed Christo amico apostolorum ascendente in celum et collocato a dextra¹⁵ Dei Patris advenit summa felicitas: ergo de eius transitu fuit apostolis congaudendum.¹⁶ Et hinc dicit apostolus ad Hebr. 7 ‘quod¹⁷ Iesus accedit per semet ipsum ad Deum, semper vivens ad interpellandum pro nobis.’ Cui conformiter scribitur prima¹⁸ Ioh. 2° ‘sed et si quis peccaverit¹⁹ advocatum habemus apud²⁰ Patrem, Iesum Christum.’ Unde Augustinus super illo textu Omelia 78 sic scribit: ‘humane nature congratulandum [p. 28] est eo, quod assumpta est a Verbo ut immortalis¹ constitueretur in celo, atque ita fieret² terra sublimis, ut³ incorruptibilis pulvis⁴ sedeat ad dextram Patris; et sic Christum ire ad Patrem et recedere a nobis est mortale, quod ex nobis suscepit, levare in celum⁵ et facere immortale. Quis ergo non hinc gaudeat ut et suam naturam iam immortalem gratuletur in Christo, ac⁶ illud se speret esse futurum per Christum? Nec dubito, quin omnes moderni scholastici nescirent impugnare istas logicas de vi vocis. Et sic intelligit Jeronimus et alii rectiloqui quod tota spes et consolacio nostra est in summa humiliacione Christi, qua naturam nostram assumendo et nobis exemplum conversacionis tribuendo fecit se non angelum vel naturam alienam ab homine, sicut fabulantur heretici, sed verissime et univoce fratrem nostrum, ut post⁷ declarabitur.

And by this the Nestorian heresy is destroyed, which argues from the diversity of natures in Christ that there is a diversity of persons. For they do not know how the assumed humanity and the divine nature, so unlike, are each the same person with [the Word]{g:verbum} of God, as Anselm [shows]{g:ostendere}, *On the Incarnation of the Word*, chapter 6; and so [Nestorius] held that the son of man is one [person] and the Son of God another, as the son of man is one thing and the Son of God another, as Augustine says above.[^5] And it is clear how much this [position]{g:posicio} differs from the Arian and the Nestorian heresy, with which to those who understand too little it would seem most to agree. And if it is objected that this does not rule out that Christ is a creature, and the most abject, which Arius did not hold, I grant the [conclusion]{g:conclusio}.[^6] Hence among the other things that would kindle the theological [virtues]{g:virtus} in us [who are on the road]{g:viantes}, it seems to me that what ought most to engender them is that supreme [lowering]{g:minoracio}, that supreme [emptying]{g:exinanicio}, that supreme obedience or [humbling]{g:humiliacio}, by which the Word, remaining substantially, completely and eternally what he was, [assumed]{g:assumere} [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} what before he was not. "For nowhere doth he take hold of the angels", according to the Apostle, Hebrews 2, as the Arians fictitiously prattle; but [he takes hold of] "the seed of Abraham", as much according to the corruptible nature as according to the immortal nature, "that he might become in all things like to his brethren"; the Apostle saying in the place cited above, "he is not ashamed to call them brethren, saying: I will declare thy name to my brethren"; and there follows: "For nowhere doth he take hold of the angels: but of the seed of Abraham he taketh hold. Wherefore it behoved him in all things to be made like unto his brethren, that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest before God."[^7] From which text it is clear that our faith would grow green from this, that he deigns to take up our fragile nature, not a purely angelic one. [27] Second, since he cannot be frustrated, it is clear that from the [assumption]{g:assumpcio} of our nature, whom he calls his brethren, having suffered and been tempted in order to help us, human hope would grow strong.[^8] And third, seeing the humbling by which the natural firstborn of God the Father deigned to assume our fragile and abject nature, [charity]{g:caritas} would be strengthened towards loving him, and his brethren in him, more dearly. Therefore it does no injury to us, nor ought it to grow cheap, that he so united us, not angels, to himself; nor does he fall away from the honour of the Father, nor is a slacker reason for loving us founded in him; but in all things the reverse.[^9] Hence, John 14, he truly says: "If you loved me, you would indeed be glad, because I go to the Father." For the [making clear]{g:declarare} of this I suppose, first, that Christ, saying this as to the assumed humanity, is of the same nature with us. Second, I suppose that the apostles to whom he spoke, and others keeping his words, are not only servants of the Lord but friends. This is clear by that [text] of John 15: "You are my friends, if you do the things that I command you. I will not now call you servants, etc." Third, I suppose that it belongs to the nature of friendship, since a friend is another self, to rejoice with a [fellow-friend]{g:coamicus} in his prosperity, especially if a richer fruit of friendship is hoped for from it. This is clear by Aristotle, *Ethics* 8.5.[^10] From these it is argued thus. Every friend ought to rejoice at a prosperous event of a fellow-friend that confirms their friendship; now to Christ, the friend of the apostles, ascending into heaven and set at the right hand of God the Father, supreme felicity came; therefore the apostles ought to have rejoiced together at his passing. And hence the Apostle says, Hebrews 7, "that Jesus goes by himself to God, always living to make intercession for us."[^11] In conformity with which it is written, 1 John 2: "But if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ." Hence Augustine on that text, homily 78, writes thus: "Human nature is to be congratulated [28] in this, that it was assumed by the Word so as to be established immortal in heaven, and so earth became sublime, so that incorruptible dust sits at the right hand of the Father; and so for Christ to go to the Father and withdraw from us is to raise into heaven the mortal [thing] which he took from us, and to make it immortal. Who then would not rejoice at this, so as both to congratulate his own nature, already immortal in Christ, and to hope that he himself will be so through Christ?"[^12] Nor do I doubt that all the modern scholastics would not know how to impugn these [pieces of] logic [by the force of the words]{g:de-vi-vocis}. And so Jerome and the other [those who speak correctly]{g:rectiloqui}, understand that all our hope and consolation is in the supreme humbling of Christ, by which, by assuming our nature and giving us an example of [manner of life]{g:conversacio}, he made himself not an angel or a nature foreign to man, as the heretics fable, but most truly and univocally our brother, as will be made clear later.[^13]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *dico Christum esse materiam primam*: the objection's report of Wyclif's thesis, "Christ is prime matter", given as quoted; the answer grants the "most abject creature" below. *Si esset*: "if there were one", existential *esse*, since Arius's first creature is a fiction. **T.** *esse* (25): A B C *esset*; *qua*: B *quam*.
[^2]: **S.** Heb. 1:4; Ps. 8:6; *paululum minoratus ab angelis* is Heb. 2:9 (Vulgate *modico quam angeli minoratus*), given in DR's wording of 2:7, 9. **T.** *satis* (25): O *sacer* "as the sacred psalm pronounces"; *secundum … corpoream* omitted by O. **C.** *per graciam unionis*: by the grace of the union, the hypostatic union.
[^3]: **T.** *filius Marie virginis hominis* (25): O M omit *virginis*; Harris notes that either *virginis* or *hominis* should perhaps be omitted. The English keeps both: the son of the virgin Mary, [the son] of man. B omits *Marie … filius*. **C.** *Christus Deus* is bare apposition, kept as "Christ God".
[^4]: **S.** Matt. 16:13, 16–17, with cuts: Peter's answer omits *Christus*, and the blessing stops at *Pater meus* (Vulgate *qui in caelis est*). **T.** *monet eum* (26): all the manuscripts read *monentem*; Harris prints *monet eum*, and the English follows the print. B reads *confirmando questionem* for *approbando responsionem*.
[^5]: **S.** Anselm, *Epistola de incarnatione Verbi*, chapter 6 in all the manuscripts. In Schmitt's numbering this is c. 11 (II, 28–29): *ita in Christo una persona est plures naturae … Non enim est alius deus, alius homo in Christo, quamvis aliud sit deus, aliud homo*; cf. c. 9 (II, 24), *ut persona dei et hominis una eademque sit*. Schmitt's c. 6 is on another question, so Wyclif's 6 follows another division (*verified*, LEDGER I02d-1). "As Augustine says above" points back to the Pascentius passages (chapter 2, part 2). **C.** *alium … alium* (masculine: another person) against *aliud … aliud* (neuter: another thing): the son of man and the Son of God are other and other as things, not as persons. The subject of *posuit* is Nestorius, the heresy's author.
[^6]: **T.** The figure 5 stands twice (26): it marks one variant, *est abiectissima quomodo* in A B C for *et abiectissima, quod*.
[^7]: **S.** Heb. 2:11–12, 16–17. *Ut fieret per omnia fratribus similis* is Wyclif's own paraphrase of 2:17; the second citation of 2:17 has *similari* for the Vulgate's *similare*. **T.** *Nusquam* (26): O *nunquam*; *confunditur* (26): O *confundetur*, A B C as the Vulgate.
[^8]: **C.** *cum non potest frustrari*: "since he cannot be frustrated", or "since [this] cannot be frustrated"; the subject is not expressed. *Passus et temptatus ad nobis auxiliandum* takes up Heb. 2:18 ("he himself hath suffered and been tempted he is able to succour them"); the relative *quos* has *nature* (our nature, that is, men) for its antecedent. **T.** *spes* (27) is A's reading (with an erasure); B C O read *species* "the human species would grow strong". O omits *potest … vocat*.
[^9]: **C.** *nec ab honore Patris degenerat*: the subject is taken as the Word; it could be the uniting, "nor does it fall short of the honour of the Father". *Remissior … diligendi racio*: a slacker ground for loving us. **T.** *in ipso* (27): A B C *in nobis*; O *potencia* for *racio*.
[^10]: **S.** John 14:28; John 15:14–15; "keeping his words", cf. John 14:23. Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* (here "8.5"; A B C "11"), on the friend as "another self" (IX.4, 1166a31–32, with the friend who rejoices and grieves with his friend at 1166a7–8; cf. IX.9) (*verified*, LEDGER I02d-2).
[^11]: **S.** Heb. 7:25, paraphrased: the Vulgate has *accedentes per ipsum ad Deum* (DR "them that come to God by him"); Wyclif makes Jesus the one who goes to God by himself. 1 John 2:1 follows, stopping at "Jesus Christ" (DR adds "the just").
[^12]: **S.** Augustine, *Tract. in Ioh.* 78.3 (on John 14:28), PL 35, 1837 (*verified*, LEDGER I02d-3); Augustine has *immortalis* and *fieret*, with the print against A B C. The print opens the quotation at *humane* and does not close it; the English closes it at "through Christ". **T.** *immortalis* (28) omitted by A B C; *fieret*: A B C *in terra*.
[^13]: **S.** Jerome, no work named. Most likely *In Ephesios* I, on Eph. 2:10 (PL 26, 471A), the passage quoted in chapter 1 (01-b n10), where Jerome confesses Christ as *vermem, et hominem, et crucifixum, et maledictionem* with *tota spei nostrae fiducia* (*verified* in sense only, LEDGER I02d-4). **T.** *post* (28.15) omitted by A B C.
