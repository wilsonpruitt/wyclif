# *De eucharistia*, chapter 3, part 8: the case of the three ministers and the conversion into the whole Christ

## ¶1 · 80.11–82.8

> [p. 80] Tercio sequitur de istis possibili, quod nedum dubitanda est quidditas cuiuscunque substancie, sed concedi debet predicacio mutua cuiuslibet de quocunque, ut posito quod Deus communicet Petro sacerdoti potenciam transsubstanciandi hostiam in corpus dominicum, habeatque dyaconus suus potenciam transsubstanciandi eam in ecclesiam, et habeat subdiaconus suus potestatem transsubstanciandi ipsam in totum mundum et quamlibet eius partem, et posito quod isti tres simul incipiant et simul finiant super eadem hostia verba sua sacramentalia, et cum Deus non subtrahit potestatem conficiendi propter peccatum ministri, posito quod Deus concurrat cum quolibet istorum usque in finem perficiendo correspondenter ad potenciam illis datam; quo posito videtur quod in fine corpus Christi erit ecclesia et mundus cum qualibet sui parte, quia quodlibet istorum est res eiusdem sacramenti; et iterum cum tam ecclesia quam mundus manet secundum quamlibet sui partem, videtur quod mundus sit secundum se totum in qualibet sui parte. Et iterum videtur quod quelibet pars mundi [p. 81] potest perpetuo servari in hostia, licet corrumpatur in sui natura. Sic enim foret de corpore Christi sacerdote conficiente in sancto triduo, ipso servato in pixide. Et videtur possibilitas casus quoad Deum ex isto patescere quod eque facile foret Deo convertere panem in totum mundum sicut in corpus suum, cum tamen repugnat infinite parvitati hostie converti in corpus Christi septipedale sicut converti in totum mundum; nec plus obstat quod sint multe transsubstanciaciones sub uno hostie sacramento quam quod quelibet pars Christi tam secundum corpus quam secundum animam sit sub qualibet particula hostie consecrate; videtur enim quod panis transsubstanciatur nedum in carnem Christi et sanguinem sed in corpus et animam ac quamlibet eius partem; non enim est conversio unionis sed transitoria (ut dicit Hugo); et ad illam videtur sufficere quod succedat sub eisdem accidentibus convertendum. Ex quo videtur quod quelibet pars vini vel panis convertitur in quamlibet partem Christi, ymmo in quodlibet accidens eius absolutum, cum quodlibet tale accidens transit sub accidentibus panis et vini, et sicut concomitanter sunt anima et quelibet pars corporis Christi ad omnem punctum hostie consecrate, ita videntur concomitanter converti sicut et accidens; et pars principalis videtur habere principalem racionem conversionis, et ita videtur esse multiplex transaccidentacio sicut est multiplex transsubstanciacio. Et hoc graviter ferunt novelli baptiste signorum sentenciam dictam superius de transsubstanciacione et conversione [p. 82] Hugonis. Videtur enim quod oportet transsubstanciatum mutari; cuius mutacionis terminum oportet esse substanciam, quia aliter foret transinferioracio vel transsubieccio, sed iuxta viam predictam nec oportet panem perdere vel acquirere aliquam qualitatem sed solum respectum ad quem non est motus secundum philosophos, dum panis fit hostia consecrata.

Third, it follows, as possible on these [men's premisses],[^1] that [not only]{g:nedum} is the [quiddity]{g:quiditas} of every [substance]{g:substancia} to be doubted, but the mutual [predication]{g:predicacio} of anything of anything must be granted. As: supposing that God communicates to Peter the priest the [power]{g:potencia} of transubstantiating the [host]{g:hostia} into [the Lord's body]{g:corpus-dominicum}, and his deacon has the power of transubstantiating it into the [church]{g:ecclesia}, and his subdeacon has the [power]{g:potestas} of transubstantiating it into the whole world and every part of it;[^2] and supposing that these three begin together and finish together their sacramental words over the same host; and, since God does not withdraw the power of [consecrating]{g:conficere} on account of the minister's sin, supposing that God concurs with each of them to the end, accomplishing correspondingly to the power given them: which supposed, it seems that at the end [the body of Christ]{g:corpus-christi} will be the church and the world with every part of it, since each of these is the [reality]{g:res-sacramenti} of the same sacrament; and again, since both the church and the world remain according to every part of themselves, it seems that the world is, according to its whole self, in every part of itself. And again it seems that any part of the world [81] can be perpetually preserved in the host, although it be corrupted in its own nature. For so it would be with the body of Christ, if a priest consecrated in the holy [three days]{g:triduum}, it being preserved in the pyx.[^3] And the possibility of the case as regards God seems to come to light from this, that it would be as easy for God to convert [bread]{g:panis} into the whole world as into his body; while yet it is as [incompatible]{g:repugnare} with the infinite smallness of the host to be converted into the seven-foot body of Christ as to be converted into the whole world. Nor is it more of an obstacle that there be many [transubstantiations]{g:transsubstanciacio} under one [sacrament]{g:sacramentum} of the host than that every part of Christ, both as to body and as to soul, be under every particle of the consecrated host. For it seems that the bread is transubstantiated not only into the flesh and blood of Christ but into the body and soul and every part of him; for it is not a [conversion]{g:conversio} of union but a transitory [one] (as Hugh says); and for that it seems to suffice that what it is to be converted into succeed under the same [accidents]{g:accidens}.[^4] From which it seems that every part of the [wine]{g:vinum} or bread is converted into every part of Christ, indeed into every [absolute accident]{g:accidens-absolutum} of his, since every such accident passes under the accidents of the bread and wine; and as the soul and every part of the body of Christ are [concomitantly]{g:concomitanter} at every point of the consecrated host, so they seem to be concomitantly converted, as the accident also [is]; and the principal part seems to have the principal account of the conversion; and so there seems to be a manifold [transaccidentation]{g:transaccidentacio}, as there is a manifold transubstantiation.[^5] And at this the [newer]{g:novelli} [baptizers of the signs]{g:baptiste-signorum} take hard the [teaching]{g:sentencia} stated above on the transubstantiation and conversion [82] of Hugh.[^6] For it seems that what is transubstantiated must be changed, and the terminus of that change must be a substance, since otherwise it would be a [transinferioration]{g:transinferioracio} or a transsubjection; but according to the way stated, the bread need neither lose nor acquire any [quality]{g:qualitas}, but only a [relation]{g:respectus}, to which there is no motion according to the philosophers, while the bread becomes a consecrated host.[^7]

## ¶2 · 82.9–82.21

> [p. 82] Hic videtur quod terminus conversionis ex sinistro intellectu convertitur in venenum; potest ergo dici quod panis et vinum convertuntur principaliter in corpus Christi et sanguinem sed concomitanter et secundarie totam humanitatem Christi et quamlibet eius partem; nec panis aut vinum deterioratur sed melioratur, ad quam melioracionem est motus extense loquendo, manet namque utraque natura; sed sacramentum melioratur, cum fit sacrum, et corpus Domini est illud cui est toto animo attendendum; et hoc signatur per conversionem et transsubstanciacionem; et videtur quod in quamlibet partem humanitatis Christi potest concedi facta conversio.

Here it seems that the term "conversion", from a perverse understanding, is converted into poison.[^8] It can therefore be said that the bread and wine are converted [principally]{g:principaliter} into the body and blood of Christ, but concomitantly and secondarily [into] the whole [humanity]{g:humanitas} of Christ and every part of it;[^9] nor is the bread or wine made worse, but bettered, and to that betterment there is motion, [speaking in an extended sense]{g:extense-loquendo}; for each nature remains. But the sacrament is bettered, since it becomes [sacred]{g:sacer}; and [the body of the Lord]{g:corpus-domini} is that to which one must attend with the whole mind; and this is signified by "conversion" and "transubstantiation". And it seems that the conversion can be granted to have been made into every part of the humanity of Christ.

## ¶3 · 82.22–83.9

> [p. 82] Sed quantum ad verba consecracionis et intencionem colentis attinet, fit quadam principalitate conversio in corpus Christi et sanguinem et in alia concomitanter et accessorie vel confuse; et potest terminus ultra hoc quod philosophi noverunt extendi ad motum, cuius motus terminus a quo est panis nudus et terminus extrinsecus ad quem est corpus dominicum, sed terminus intrinsecus ad quem est sacramentalis sanctitas iuxta quod concedi potest extense loquendo de motu quod panis et vinum insensibiliter alterantur. Et sic ille motus dicit aggregative motus multiplices et [p. 83] multas motus materias, ut bonitatem signi, efficaciam signati et multas alias habitudines relativas; et quando dicimus quod corpus Christi non exinde movetur, loquimur equivoce et striccius tunc de motu, sicut non dicimus quod homo movetur ex hoc quod species sua diffusius multiplicatur per medium nec est michi vis de dictis institucionibus terminorum (ut patet posterius) etc.

But as far as concerns the words of consecration and the intention of the worshipper, the conversion is made, by a certain [principality]{g:principalitas}, into the body and blood of Christ, and into the other things concomitantly and accessorily, or [indistinctly]{g:confuse}; and the term can be extended, beyond what the philosophers knew, to motion: of which motion the terminus from which is bare bread, and the extrinsic terminus to which is the Lord's body, but the intrinsic terminus to which is sacramental holiness;[^10] according to which it can be granted, speaking of motion in an extended sense, that the bread and wine are imperceptibly altered. And so that motion [signifies]{g:dicere} [aggregatively]{g:aggregative} manifold motions and [83] many matters of motion, as the goodness of the [sign]{g:signum}, the efficacy of the [thing signified]{g:signatum}, and many other [relative respects]{g:habitudo-relativa}. And when we say that the body of Christ is not moved thereby, we then speak [equivocally]{g:equivoce} and more strictly of motion; as we do not say that a man is moved by the fact that his species is multiplied more widely through the medium.[^11] Nor do I care about the said institutions of terms (as will appear later), etc.[^12]

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 80.11: The same words might mean all sorts of different things.
- 81.18: Since the host is converted into the whole Christ, it includes His accidents, and so there is transaccidentation as well as transsubstantiation.
- 82.9: The change is principally into the body and blood of Christ, and secondarily into the whole manhood of Christ.

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Sequitur de istis possibili* (80.11) is taken as "it follows, as a possible [case], on these [men's premisses]": the third corollary drawn from the adversaries' case of A and B (77.29–80.10). *De istis* could also be the premisses themselves.
[^2]: **C.** *Potencia* (80.14, 80.16) and *potestas* (80.17) are both "power": the sentence uses them for the same thing, and the CONTEXTUAL *potencia* takes its "power" sense. The case gives the deacon and the subdeacon powers of transubstantiating; the clause on the minister's sin (80.20–21) removes the objection that God would not concur.
[^3]: **S.** The schoolmen's case of a host consecrated during the three days, when the body lay dead in the tomb (LEDGER K03h-1: Thomas, *ST* III q. 81 a. 4, *si … fuisset servatum in pyxide*, and on the Lombard's IV d. 11 in *Super Sent.* IV d. 11 q. 3 a. 4; the Lombard himself does not raise the case).
[^4]: **S/C.** Hugh of St Victor, cited in this chapter from 74.3 (LEDGER K03h-2: *De sacramentis* II p. 8 c. 9, PL 176, 468, *non secundum unionem sed secundum transitionem*): the conversion is "transitory", one thing passing into the other's place, not a "conversion of union", in which the two become one. *Convertendum* is taken as the term into which the bread is converted, succeeding it under its accidents. C first wrote *transicio* (81.16).
[^5]: **C.** *Transaccidentacio* (81.27) is formed on *transsubstanciacio*: if every part of Christ is a term of the conversion, so is every accident of his. *Ita videntur concomitanter converti* (81.25) keeps the print's passive, of the soul and the parts as terms of a concomitant conversion. E reads *subaccidentacio*.
[^6]: **C.** *Baptiste signorum*: those who christen signs, who deal in the names (cf. *doctores signorum*, *secta signorum*); B reads *moderni baptiste*. *Hoc* is taken as "at this", with *sentenciam* the object of *graviter ferunt*. *Sentencia*, CONTEXTUAL: here "teaching", the position stated in this chapter at 75.27–76.5.
[^7]: **C/S.** *Transinferioracio* and *transsubieccio* are formed on *transsubstanciacio*, for a change whose terminus would be inferiority or subjection, not substance. That there is no motion to relation: Aristotle, *Physics* V.2, 225b11 (LEDGER K03h-3).
[^8]: **C.** *Terminus conversionis* plays on two senses: the word "conversion" and the terminus of a change. The word is rendered "term" and the end-point "terminus" through 82.9–83.9.
[^9]: **T.** *Secundarie totam humanitatem* (82.13) has no preposition; "[into]" is supplied from the parallel *in corpus Christi*. D reads *concomitant varie*.
[^10]: **C.** The three termini: the bread from which; the Lord's body, the extrinsic terminus to which; sacramental holiness, the intrinsic terminus to which. *Extense loquendo*, "speaking in an extended sense" (here and at 82.15), marks motion taken beyond the philosophers' sense.
[^11]: **C.** *Species sua*: the visible species a man sends out through the medium, as in the optics of 84.21–26; not the sacramental species, and untagged. ABE read *sicut enim dicimus*, without the negative.
[^12]: **C.** *Nec est michi vis de* is taken as "nor do I care about": the institutions of terms are the agreed meanings of *conversio* and *motus*.
