# *De potestate pape*, chapter 2, part 1: idle priests, the shrinking of power, and miracles

## ¶1 · 22.2–22.7

> [p. 22] Sed adhuc videtur, cum omnis potestas sit a Deo, quod nemo habet potestatem ad faciendum aliquid, nisi quod Deus intendit ut faciat. Ex quo videtur quod sacerdotes ociantes excusantur, cum non habent potestatem ad faciendam aliquid quod omittunt.

But it still seems, since all [power]{g:potestas} is from God, that no one has power to do anything except what God intends that he do. From this it seems that idle [priests]{g:sacerdos} are excused, since they do not have power to do anything that they omit.[^1]

## ¶2 · 22.8–22.11

> [p. 22] Hic videtur mihi quod questio prima illata concedi debeat, nam Deus omnisciens non dat homini aliquid nisi propter finem, quem plenissime et distinccione intendit, quia aliter frustra daret.

Here it seems to me that the first question brought forward ought to be granted, for God, being omniscient, gives a man nothing except for an end, which he intends most fully and distinctly, since otherwise he would give in vain.[^2]

## ¶3 · 22.12–22.16

> [p. 22] Item, nemo habet potestatem ad aliquid faciendum, nisi liceat sibi illud facere, cum a Deo non sit potestas nisi legitima, et per consequens Deus obligat eum sub pena peccati vel amissionis premii ut illud faciat, sicut Deus intendit.

Again, no one has power to do something unless it is lawful for him to do it, since there is no power from God but lawful [power]; and consequently God binds him, under pain of sin or of the loss of reward, to do it, as God intends.[^3]

## ¶4 · 22.17–22.21

> [p. 22] Item; recipiens talem potestatem a Deo est ut sic obligacior, sed oportet Deum cognoscere excessum obligacionis, et per consequens, quid adequate potentatus debet facere ex illa potestate, et sic Deus intendit ut illud faciat.

Again: one who receives such power from God is, as such, the more bound; now God must know the excess of the obligation, and consequently what the potentate ought adequately to do from that power; and so God intends that he do it.[^4]

## ¶5 · 22.22–23.3

> [p. 22] Sed notandum est, quod sicut aliud est Deum velle quod Petrus sit salvus et aliud Deum velle quod ipse est salvus, sic aliud est Deum intendere, quod Petrus faciat hoc et aliud Deum intendere quod ipse facit hoc; quia eo ipso quo Deus obligat Petrum sub obtentu premii vel incursu pene quod Petrus faciat hoc; Deus intendit quod faciat hoc; et sic utrobique solum intendit et vult veritatem, cum veritas est quod [p. 23] Petrus debet facere hoc; licet non sit verum, quod hoc facit, et per consequens non intentum vel volitum a Deo.

But it must be noted that, just as it is one thing for God to will that Peter be saved and another for God to will that he is saved, so it is one thing for God to intend that Peter do this and another for God to intend that he does this; for by the very fact that God binds Peter, under the gaining of reward or the incurring of pain, that Peter do this, God intends that he do this; and so in both cases he intends and wills only truth, since the truth is that [23] Peter ought to do this, although it is not true that he does it, and consequently [that] is not intended or willed by God.[^5]

## ¶6 · 23.4–23.11

> [p. 23] Et patet quod non excusatur sed accusatur ocium sacerdotum dicente Apostolo: Hortamur vos ne in vacuum graciam Dei recipiatis. Quicunque enim aliquam potestatem a Deo receperit, per quam liceat sibi opus Dei facere et ex negligencia pretermittit, in vanum graciam Dei recipit. Et in hoc imitamur eos, qui ex omissione instanter operando bonum peccaverunt continue.

And it is clear that the idleness of priests is not excused but accused, as the Apostle says: "We exhort you that you receive not the grace of God in vain."[^6] For whoever has received any power from God by which it is lawful for him to do God's work, and through negligence passes it over, receives the grace of God in vain. And in this we imitate those who sinned continually by omission of urgently working good.[^7]

## ¶7 · 23.12–23.23

> [p. 23] In hoc tamen est duplex culpa, prima est negligencia potentatus, et secunda maior, crebrior et periculosior est vetacio sathane, qua adversans impedit ne potentatus exerceat potestatem; et hoc contingit per mille meandros tradicionis humane, et specialiter per ficta de iurisdiccione. Quando autem non stat per illum qui immediate habet potestatem sed per alium irracionabiliter restringentem, tunc non sibi sed alii est debitum imputandum; sed hoc raro contingit, cum causa martirii foret hominem se exponere pro distribucione prudenti thesauri Domini.

In this, however, there is a twofold fault. The first is the negligence of the potentate; and the second, greater, more frequent and more dangerous, is Satan's forbidding, by which the adversary prevents the potentate from exercising his power; and this happens through a thousand windings of human tradition, and especially through fictions about jurisdiction. But when it does not rest with the one who immediately has the power but with another who unreasonably restrains him, then the debt is to be imputed not to him but to the other; but this rarely happens, since it would be a cause for martyrdom for a man to expose himself for the prudent distribution of the Lord's treasure.[^8]

## ¶8 · 23.24–23.33

> [p. 23] Unde videtur multis, quod Deus non intendit ut potens faciat tale opus; nec habet potestatem ad hoc; sed habet potestatem ut mereatur ex hoc quod nititur ad sic agendum. Alii autem dicunt quod habet potestatem remotam ad sic faciendum, hoc est, potestatem mediate; qua potest sic facere, et tamen Deus non intendit simpliciter quod ita faciet, et sic habet potestatem sic faciendi, sed non ad finem ut sic faciat.

Hence it seems to many that God does not intend that the one who has power do such a work, nor does he have power for it; but he has power to merit from the fact that he strives to act so. But others say that he has remote power to do so, that is, power mediately, by which he can do so, and yet God does not intend [without qualification]{g:simpliciter} that he will do so; and so he has power of doing so, but not to the end that he do so.

## ¶9 · 24.1–24.29

> [p. 24] Sed dimissa ista dissensione logica, videtur quod omnis potestas secularis diminuetur usque ad non gradum, quia solum durat pro tempore vie, ut docet Apostolus prima Corinth. XV, 24: Deinde, inquit, finis, cum tradiderit regnum Deo et Patri cum evacuaverit omnem principatum et potestatem et virtutem. Ymo videtur, cum Christus limitat omni sacerdoti suo formam et numerum obiectorum, quod hodie sunt prepositi potestatis minoris quam erant in primitiva ecclesia, et sic usque ad non gradum. Et id considerarent qui stulte et nescie suam magnificant potestatem. Videtur enim quod proporcionabiliter ut plus meritorie nituntur ad edificandum ecclesiam, sic habent amplius potestatis, cum secundum illam mensuram Deus dat potestatem hominibus; quilibet autem clericus vel laicus habet potestatem sufficientem edificandi ecclesiam, si voluerit esse Dei adiutor, faciens quod Deus destinavit eum ad taliter faciendum. Noverunt, inquam, logici quod est in libera potestate hominis facere quod Deus destinavit eum vel ordinavit eum ad fines huiusmodi. Sed aliter loquuntur qui ponunt globum potestatis quam Deus ubilibet potest per se ponere. Secundo principaliter arguitur de potestate faciendi miracula, de potestate dandi Spiritum Sanctum vel graciam, de potestate ampla absolvendi vel dispensandi alia sacramenta.

But, setting aside this logical dissension, it seems that all secular power will be diminished down to no degree, since it lasts only for the time of [the way]{g:via}, as the Apostle teaches, 1 Corinthians 15:24: "Afterwards," he says, "the end: when he shall have delivered up the [kingdom]{g:regnum} to God and the Father: when he shall have brought to nought all principality and power and virtue." Indeed it seems, since Christ [assigns]{g:limitare} to every priest of his the form and number of objects, that today [superiors]{g:prepositi} are of less power than they were in the primitive [church]{g:ecclesia}, and so down to no degree.[^9] And those who foolishly and unknowingly magnify their own power should consider this. For it seems that in proportion as they strive more meritoriously to build up the church, so they have more power, since God gives power to men according to that measure; and any [cleric]{g:clericus} or layman whatever has power sufficient to build up the church, if he will be God's helper, doing what God destined him to do in such a way. Logicians know, I say, that it is in the free power of a man to do what God has destined him, or ordained him, to [do for] ends of this kind.[^10] But they speak otherwise who posit a mass of power which God can by himself place anywhere.[^11] In the second principal place, it is argued about the power of working miracles, about the power of giving the Holy Spirit or grace, and about the ample power of absolving or of dispensing the other sacraments.

## ¶10 · 24.30–26.8

> [p. 24] Quantum ad primum videtur mihi quod miraculum potest dici factum vel faccio quam Deus appropriate facit, et sic solus Deus agit miracula et longe crebrius quam putamus, quia quicunque creat licet solita, quia non miramur ea non [p. 25] vocamus miracula, ut declarat Augustinus super Joh. Omel., et sic quelibet creatura, sicut quilibet actus creandi potest dici miraculum, sic et quelibet faccio que Deo est propria. Unde idem esset creaturam vendicare potestatem miraculose agere, et vendicare quod sit alter Deus; quod Deus non potest creature concedere. Creare autem non potest Deus communicare creature, nec annichilare potest alicui competere, ut dictum est in materia de universalibus. Et patet quomodo debemus iudicare intellectu et fide miracula. Periculosum autem est instante tempore Antichristi magnificare signa sensibilia, cum Antichristi discipuli extollunt se super omne quod dicitur Deus, de principatu, de potestate, et virtute, quas Apostolus dicit evacuandos per Christum, Deus autem ad meritum hominis tam vivi quam mortui sepe facit miracula. Ad quem sensum dicitur corpora mortuorum facere miracula, ymo truncos lapides et multa putrida finguntur facere miracula propter lucrum. Sic autem equivocando in faccione miraculi, videtur quod omne membrum sancte matris ecclesie, de quanto sic meretur ut Deus operetur miraculose, facit miraculum, et pauci mortui qui sunt in statu merendi beati in patria. Illi autem qui dicunt quod lapis, herba et alia natura corporea habet virtutem faciendi miracula, videntur michi nimis confuse loqui, quia sic omnia forent miracula, cum nemo posset facere nisi miraculum quoad Deum. Alii autem dicunt quod oportet [p. 26] esse opus mirabile, et sic sunt illis quibus sunt mira insolita opera nature miracula, aliis autem non forent miracula, et sic de multis ficticiis infundabiliter adinventis. Vel ergo dicamus quod solus Deus habet potestatem faciendi miracula vel equivoce concedamus quod omnis homo promerens aput Deum, ut faciat ad preces suas miracula, facit quodammodo ipsa miracula.

As to the first, it seems to me that a miracle can be called a deed or making which God does as proper to himself; and so God alone works miracles, and far more often than we think; for whatever he creates, although customary, we do not [25] call miracles, because we do not wonder at them, as Augustine declares on John, in a homily;[^12] and so every creature, as every act of creating, can be called a miracle, and so too every making that is proper to God. Hence it would be the same for a creature to claim power to act miraculously and to claim that it is another God, which God cannot grant to a creature. And God cannot communicate creating to a creature, nor can annihilating belong to anyone [but him], as has been said in the matter of universals.[^13] And it is clear how we ought to judge miracles, by understanding and by faith.[^14] But it is dangerous, with the time of Antichrist at hand, to magnify [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} signs, since the disciples of Antichrist "lift themselves up above all that is called God" on the score of principality, of power and of virtue, which the Apostle says are "to be brought to nought by Christ".[^15] But God often works miracles for the merit of a man, living as well as dead. In this sense the bodies of the dead are said to work miracles; indeed stocks, stones and many rotten things are feigned to work miracles for the sake of gain. And speaking [equivocally]{g:equivoce} in this way about the making of a miracle, it seems that every [member]{g:membrum} of holy mother church, insofar as he so merits that God works miraculously, works a miracle, and few of the dead who are in the state of meriting, the blessed in [the homeland]{g:patria}.[^16] But those who say that a stone, a herb and other bodily nature have the [power]{g:virtus} of working miracles seem to me to speak [exceedingly]{g:nimis} [indistinctly]{g:confuse}, since in that way all things would be miracles, since no one could make anything but a miracle with respect to God. And others say that it must [26] be a wonderful work; and so the unusual works of nature are miracles to those to whom they are wonders, but to others they would not be miracles; and so with many [fictions]{g:ficticia} groundlessly invented. Either, therefore, let us say that God alone has power of working miracles, or let us grant equivocally that every man who merits with God that he should work miracles at his prayers does in some manner work the miracles himself.[^17]

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 22.2: No one has power to do anything but what God wills,
- 22.8: This is made an excuse by idle priests.
- 22.22: But we must note the difference between what God wills we should do and his will that we do it,
- 23.4: Idleness of priests condemned by St. Paul.
- 23.12: The fault is double; I. negligence of those who have power.
- 23.18: 2. Hindrances of jurisdiction.
- 24.8: Prelates have less power now than in the primitive Church.
- 24.26: Miracles are works done by God alone and all his works are miracles,
- 25.12: Danger of too much attention to wonders.

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** The print's *ad faciendam aliquid* (22.6) is a slip of gender for *faciendum*, translated as such. A reads *faciendum aliud nisi* at 22.2–3 for *faciendum aliquid, nisi*. A and B note in the margin *Potestatem habere (ad aliquid faciendum)*.

[^2]: **C.** *Questio prima illata* is taken as the first objection just brought in (¶1, no power except to what God intends), which Wyclif grants. *Distinccione* (22.10) is an ablative used as an adverb, "with distinction", rendered "distinctly".

[^3]: **T.** "As God intends" renders Loserth's *sicut Deus intendit* (22.16); the codices read *et sic Deus* ("and so God intends"), which he emends. The manuscript reading also construes and gives the same sense as the close of ¶4.

[^4]: **G.** *Potentatus* (22.20, 23.13–14) is here the person who holds power, "the potentate", not the abstract "sovereignty" of the GLOSSARY row (4.27). *Obligacior*, a comparative of the participle, "the more bound".

[^5]: **C.** The distinction turns on mood: *quod Petrus sit salvus / faciat* (subjunctive, what God wills or binds to be done) against *quod ipse est salvus / facit* (indicative, the fact). God intends the first, which is true (Peter ought to do it), and need not intend the second, which may be false.

[^6]: **S.** 2 Cor. 6:1 (editor's reference). The Vulgate has *Adiuvantes autem exhortamur ne in vacuum gratiam Dei recipiatis* (DR "And we helping do exhort you that you receive not the grace of God in vain"); Wyclif's *Hortamur vos* drops "helping".

[^7]: **T.** Loserth prints *operando* (23.11) and notes *Rectius: operandi*; *ex omissione … operando* does not construe, so his correction is translated ("by omission of urgently working good"). B lacks *et* at 23.10.

[^8]: **C.** *Non stat per illum* is the legal idiom "it is not owing to him". The last clause gives the reason the case is rare: the hindrance would be a cause worth martyrdom, so the one who holds the power ought to expose himself rather than yield, and the debt usually remains his. *Iurisdiccio* has no GLOSSARY row; rendered "jurisdiction".

[^9]: **T.** *Limitat* (24.8) stands with a capital in the established text; the reading is the plate's (scans *hmitat*). The sense "assigns" follows the GLOSSARY row for *limitare*.

[^10]: **T.** "Free power" renders Loserth's *libera potestate* (24.21); the codices read *libertate*, which he corrects, since *in libertate potestate* does not construe.

[^11]: **G.** *Globus potestatis*: of a thing, "mass" (GLOSSARY *globus*, untagged in this sense): power imagined as a stock God could set down anywhere, against Wyclif's view that power answers to the merit of the one who uses it.

[^12]: **S.** "Augustine on John, in a homily": Loserth compares Augustine, *Sermo* 80 (on John 6) and *Sermo* 126, which has the thought (126.3: *quotidiana miracula Dei non facilitate, sed assiduitate viluerant*) (LEDGER P02a-1). The theme, that God's daily works are not wondered at because they are customary, is also in *In Ioannis evangelium tractatus* 24.1 and 9.1 (*quotidiana miracula … assiduitate viluerunt*), which answer Wyclif's "on John" best (LEDGER P02a-2). **C.** *Quicunque creat* (24.32) is taken as *quecunque* ("whatever [things] he creates"); as printed the masculine has no construal.

[^13]: **S.** "In the matter of universals": Wyclif's *Tractatus de universalibus* (place untraced): creating belongs to God alone, and annihilating, as Wyclif says here, to no one (LEDGER P02a-3).

[^14]: **T.** "By understanding" renders Loserth's *intellectu* (25.11); the codices read *intellectum*, which he corrects.

[^15]: **S.** 2 Thess. 2:4 (editor's reference), turned to the plural: Vulgate *extollitur supra omne quod dicitur Deus*. "To be brought to nought" echoes 1 Cor. 15:24, quoted at 24.4 (*evacuaverit omnem principatum et potestatem et virtutem*); Loserth refers to 2 Cor. 3:7. *Evacuandos* is masculine against the feminine *quas*, as printed. **C.** *De principatu, de potestate, et virtute* is taken with *extollunt se*: they exalt themselves on the score of these.

[^16]: **C.** As printed the clause has no verb of its own and its sense is doubtful: the blessed in the homeland are not in the state of meriting. The likelier sense is that few of the dead work miracles in this way, since merit belongs to the wayfarer; a word (perhaps *non*, "not in the state of meriting") may be lost at 25.24–25. Translated as printed.

[^17]: **T.** B reads *Si autem* for *Sic autem* at 25.21–22, *promerent* for *promerens* at 26.7; A reads *sunt miracula* at 26.1–2. *Aput* is the print's spelling of *apud*.
