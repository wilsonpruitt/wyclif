# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 13, part 4: the arguments for the three cases answered; "insofar as God" and "insofar as man"; the end of the treatise

## ¶1 · 227.33–228.3

> [p. 227] Primo generatur ex hoc quod precipui moderni doctores catholici et subtiles in scripturis reliquerant possibilitates [p. 228] omnium istorum casuum cum suis probacionibus et solucionibus obiectorum; quod non est veri simile de talibus ac tantis doctoribus nisi subsit possibilitas eorundem: ergo etc.

First, [the argument] is generated from this: that the chief [modern doctors]{g:moderni-doctores}, catholic and subtle, left in their writings the possibilities [228] of all these cases, with their proofs and the solutions of objections; which is not likely of such and so great doctors unless the possibility of them underlay [it]: therefore etc.[^1]

## ¶2 · 228.4–228.21

> [p. 228] Hic dicitur ut supra quod variacio in methaphisica et honoris extraneandi ambicio est in causa. In cuius signum vix duos invenies quin discordent. Alii autem superiores doctores ut Augustinus, Ieronimus, Gregorius, et Anselmus cum suis sequacibus dicunt concorditer quod Christus post incarnacionem est aliquid, quod prius non fuerat, ut patet superius; et illi concordant cum racione, et verbaliter ac sentencialiter cum scriptura. Nec sequitur ex illo quod posteriores erant heretici, cum sentenciam hereticam potest catholicus sine pertinacia defendere, sicut credo in proposito contigisse. Verumptamen vanum est excludere illos simpliciter a peccato; cum laborando circa¹ veram sentenciam secundum studium bonum² de genere contingit catholicum presumptiva superbia vel³ inani gloria peccare facillime; quanto magis defendendo impossibile, licet intencio secundum aliquid sit recta et vera sentencia: quodlibet⁴ enim impossibile disperiret,⁵ si per se sine mixtura veri defensum fuerit!

Here the answer is, as above, that variation in metaphysics and the ambition for the honour of [estrangement]{g:extraneare} is the cause. In token of which you will scarcely find two who do not disagree. But the other, superior doctors, such as Augustine, Jerome, Gregory, and Anselm with their followers, say with one accord that Christ after the incarnation is something which he had not been before, as is clear above; and they agree with reason, and in word and in [teaching]{g:sentencia} with scripture.[^2] Nor does [it follow]{g:sequitur} from that that the later [doctors] were heretics, since a catholic can defend a heretical teaching without pertinacity, as I believe has happened in the case at hand. Nevertheless it is vain to exclude them [without qualification]{g:simpliciter} from sin; since in labouring about a true teaching, with a study [good in its kind]{g:bonum-de-genere},[^3] it happens most easily that a catholic sins by presumptuous pride or vain glory; how much more by defending the impossible, although the intention in some respect be right and the teaching true: for every impossible [position], indeed, would perish, if it were defended by itself without admixture of truth![^4]

## ¶3 · 228.22–228.26

> [p. 228] 2ᵈᵒ obicitur per raciones sancti Thome Questione 7. 3ⁱⁱ et duabus sequentibus. ‘Omnis, inquit, necessitas et impossibilitas Deo subicitur.’ Cum ergo voluntati Dei omnis impossibilitas subicitur, sequitur quod Patrem incarnari non sit sibi⁶ impossibile.

Secondly, [it is objected, by appeal to]{g:obicitur-per} the arguments of Saint Thomas, question 7 of the third [book], and the two following:[^5] "Every necessity", he says, "and impossibility is subject to God." Since, then, every impossibility is subject to the will of God, it follows that for the Father to be incarnated is not impossible for him.[^6]

## ¶4 · 228.27–229.8

> [p. 228] Hic videtur michi quod argumentum peccat tam in materia quam in forma. In materia, quia solum veritas Deo subicitur; nulla impossibilitas est veritas; ergo assumptum peccat. Quod si intelligamus per impossibilitatem signi impossibilis falsitatem vel veritatem negativam, que est absoluta necessitas, nichil ad propositum; sed ad oppositum adducitur. Nam non prodest quod hoc signum sit impossibile—Pater [p. 229] potest incarnari—vel—quod necessario non potest esse quod Pater potest incarnari—ad concludendum propositum. Nec oportet instare pro improbacione argumenti, cum reductum ad formam patenter peteret; sicut facit secundum argumentum, quo capit humanam naturam infinitum distare a qualibet persona divina, et omnium¹ eque distancium est eque possibilis coniunccio. Nam maior est impossibilis, et sensus minoris petitur.

Here it seems to me that the argument errs both in matter and in form. In matter, because only truth is subject to God; no impossibility is a truth; therefore [the assumption]{g:assumptum}[^7] errs. And if by "impossibility" we understand the falsity of an impossible [sign]{g:signum}, or a negative truth, which is absolute necessity, it is nothing to the purpose; rather, it is brought forward for the opposite. For it does not help, for [concluding]{g:concludere} the purpose, that this sign is impossible, "the Father [229] can be incarnated", or "necessarily it cannot be that the Father can be incarnated". Nor is there need to object in disproof of the argument, since, reduced to form, it would plainly [beg]{g:petitur} [the question]; as does the second argument, in which he takes "human nature is infinitely distant from each divine person, and of all things equally distant the conjunction is equally possible". For the major is impossible, and the sense of the minor is begged.[^8]

## ¶5 · 229.9–229.10

> [p. 229] 3° arguitur ex hoc quod posse assumere carnem est dignitatis² in Filio; ergo Patri potest competere.

Thirdly, it is argued from this: that to be able to [assume]{g:assumere} flesh is [a matter] of dignity in the Son; therefore it can befit the Father.[^9]

## ¶6 · 229.11–229.14

> [p. 229] Sed iuxta illud sequeretur quod gigni conveniat Patri, cum sit dignitatis in Filio. Dicitur ergo cum isto sancto quod Deus non potest incarnari in persona alia de potencia ordinata; sed, si voluerit de potencia absoluta.

But according to that it would follow that to be begotten befits the Father, since it is [a matter] of dignity in the Son. The answer, then, is, with this saint, that God cannot be incarnated in another [person]{g:persona} by [ordained power]{g:potencia-ordinata}; but, "if he should will, by [absolute power]{g:potencia-absoluta}".[^10]

## ¶7 · 229.15–229.30

> [p. 229] Et conformiter dicitur ad argumenta quibus videtur probare quod eadem natura potest assumi a qualibet personarum. Primo ex hoc quod omnis humanitas est indifferenter ad ymaginem Trinitatis, et³ illa est racio assumendi; ymo cum Verbum humanitatem assumpserit,⁴ est ipsa similior et habilior⁵ ut ab utraque reliqua assumatur. Constat quidem quod faccio ad ymaginem Dei non est communis racio ut quelibet humanitas⁶ a qualibet⁶ persona assumi poterit, quia sic omnis homo posset esse Trinitas increata; sed Christo inest racio singularis, unde ille singulariter a Verbo assumi poterit; et petitur utrobique probandum. Unde multum distat unio potenciarum anime eidem organo et⁷ unio humanitatis ad Filium; ita quod non sequitur per locum a simili—si multe potencie anime possunt simul uniri eidem organo,⁷ ergo per idem multe persone divine possunt uniri⁸ ypostatice eidem corpori—.

And conformably the answer is given to the arguments by which he seems to prove that the same [nature]{g:natura} can be assumed by any of the persons. First, from this: that every [humanity]{g:humanitas} is indifferently to the image of the Trinity, and that is the reason for assuming; indeed, since the [Word]{g:verbum} has assumed the humanity, it is the more like and the more [qualified]{g:habilis}[^11] to be assumed by either of the others. It is evident, indeed, that the [making]{g:faccio} to the image of God is not a common reason why any humanity whatever could be assumed by any person whatever, because then every man could be the uncreated Trinity; but in Christ there is a singular reason, whence he could singularly be assumed by the Word; and in both places what is to be proved is begged. Hence the [union]{g:unio} of the powers of the soul to the same organ is far different from the union of the humanity to the Son; so that it does not follow by the topic [from likeness]{g:a-simili}: "if many powers of the soul can be united at once to the same organ, therefore by the same token many divine persons can be united [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} to the same body".[^12]

## ¶8 · 229.31–230.18

> [p. 229] Quod si queratur⁹ racio diversitatis, satis est quod hoc facto tres persone possent¹⁰ esse unus homo et quelibet [p. 230] persona divina multi homines; sicut consequenter annuit sanctus Thomas, et¹ hoc posito non sic esset, ut patet ex dictis. Quantum ad argumenta, que facit Doctor subtilis in principio 3ⁱⁱ pro hac parte, respondet ipsemet satis clare. Quod si addatur— Verbum divinum liberius copulatum Christo quam aliquam² carnem carni ypostatice copulatam; sed anima potest multis carnibus copulari; ergo multo magis Verbum alii humanitati—; dicitur quod consequencia non valet per locum a simili. Nam Verbum liberius produxit hoc tempus perpetuum quam Petrus hunc filium; et tamen Verbum non potuit produxisse aliud tempus, quod nec sit illud nec eius pars, licet Petrus potuit alium filium produxisse, ymo filium qui non fuisset pertinens huic nato. Conceditur tamen quod quamcunque carnem spiritus assumpserit vel sibi personaliter uniret,³ foret solummodo idem homo. Et sic licet homo posset esse aliud quam est modo, quia alia natura corporea, non tamen aliud suppositum vel persona, ut patet in materia De Anima.

And if the reason for the difference is asked, it is enough that, this done, three persons could be one man and each [230] divine person many men, as Saint Thomas consequently grants; and, this posited, it would not be so, as is clear from what has been said.[^13] As for the arguments which the [Subtle Doctor]{g:doctor-subtilis} makes for this side at the beginning of the third [book], he himself answers them clearly enough.[^14] And if it is added: "the divine Word [is] more freely joined to Christ than another flesh hypostatically joined to flesh; but a soul can be joined to many fleshes; therefore much more the Word to another humanity", the answer is that the [inference]{g:consequencia} does not hold by the topic from likeness.[^15] For the Word more freely produced this perpetual time than Peter this son; and yet the Word could not have produced another time, which would be neither this one nor a part of it, although Peter could have produced another son, indeed a son who would not have been related to this one born. It is granted, however, that whatever flesh the spirit had assumed or personally united to itself, it would be only the same man. And so, although a man could be another thing than he is now, because another bodily nature, yet not another [supposit]{g:suppositum} or person, as is clear in the matter of the *De anima*.[^16]

## ¶9 · 230.19–231.22

> [p. 230] Ex istis colligitur quomodo aliqua insint Verbo pure in quantum Deus; aliqua⁴ in quantum homo; et⁴ aliqua mixtim. In quantum Deus, creavit mundum; in quantum homo, passus est mortem; et in quantum Deus et homo, redemit hominem. Nec debet signum reduplicatum intelligi utrobique, ut dicat⁵ consequenciam formalem logicam et causalem; sicut hic Christus in quantum homo est creatura; sed et⁶ quomodolibet dicit causam, ut licet potest esse Deus et non produxisse mundum, tamen deitas fuit⁷ causa quare produxit mundum et non sua humanitas. Et licet deitas fuit⁸ causa quare paciebatur, non tamen secundum illam; quia tunc fuisset causa formalis vel⁹ subiectum passionis; conceditur tamen quod humanitas fuit causa⁹ formalis Christi et causa subiectiva mortis: et sic in quantum homo passus est mortem, quia secundum humanitatem. Et [p. 231] intelligendum est in quantum, ut dicat¹ generaliter causam² quamlibet parcialem vel totalem, completam vel incompletam. Ego non video quin sane concedi poterit quod, in quantum Deus, paciebatur; cum deitas fuit per se causa illius passionis, sed non secundum deitatem, cum tunc deitas principaliter pateretur. Redempcio autem humani generis pro offensa dicit infinitatem precii proporcionaliter ad delictum; et sic oportet redimentem esse precium infinitum; et per consequens Deum; et preter hoc oportet quod distinguatur essencialiter a solvente, cum non sit possibile quidquam³ mercari primo et immediate secundum seipsum; et per consequens oportet precium, quod secundum deitatem est infinitum, esse naturam alienam⁴ creatam. Cum autem nulla alia potuit pertinere nisi illa que peccaverat, que compendiosissime sit⁵ et satisfaciens et precium, mercans et mercatum, reconcilians et reconciliatum; relinquitur quod oportet ipsam esse humanitatem ‘mediatorem⁶ Dei et hominum, hominem Christum Iesum.’ Et sic quidquid infuit sibi formaliter,⁷ infuit sibi⁷ in quantum Deus; licet non omne, quod sibi infuit, inerat secundum quod Deus, nec in quantum homo. Deitas enim fuit per se causa cuiuscunque causati, quod sic infuit Iesu nostro.

From these things it is gathered how some things belong to the Word purely [insofar as]{g:in-quantum} God; some insofar as man; and some [in a mixed way]{g:mixtim}. Insofar as God, he created the world; insofar as man, he suffered death; and insofar as God and man, he redeemed man. Nor ought the [reduplicated sign]{g:signum-reduplicatum} to be understood in both places as [signifying]{g:dicere} a formal, logical and causal inference, as here: "Christ insofar as man is a [creature]{g:creatura}"; but also as it signifies a cause in any way whatever; as, although he can be God and not have produced the world, yet the [Godhead]{g:deitas} was the cause why he produced the world, and not his humanity. And although the Godhead was the cause why he suffered, yet not as to it; because then it would have been the formal cause or the subject of the passion; it is granted, however, that the humanity was the formal cause of Christ and the subjective cause of death: and so insofar as man he suffered death, because [he suffered it] as to the humanity. And [231] "insofar as" is to be understood as it signifies generally any cause, partial or total, complete or incomplete.[^17] I do not see but that it could soundly be granted that, insofar as God, he suffered; since the Godhead was [of itself]{g:per-se} the cause of that passion, but not [as to the Godhead]{g:secundum-deitatem}, since then the Godhead would principally suffer. And the redemption of the human race for the offence signifies an infinity of price in proportion to the fault; and so the redeemer must be an infinite price, and consequently God; and besides this he must be distinguished essentially from the one paying, since it is not possible for anything to buy, first and [without intermediary]{g:immediate}, according to itself; and consequently the price, which as to the Godhead is infinite, must be a foreign created nature.[^18] And since no other could pertain except that which had sinned, which most compendiously would be both the satisfying and the price, the buyer and the bought, the reconciler and the reconciled, it remains that it must be the humanity, "the mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus".[^19] And so whatever was in him formally was in him insofar as (*in quantum*) God; although not all that was in him was in him [insofar as]{g:secundum-quod} (*secundum quod*) God, nor insofar as man.[^20] For the Godhead was of itself the cause of anything caused that was thus in our Jesus.

## ¶10 · 231.23–231.31

> [p. 231] Et si obicitur quod Christus, si in quantum Deus moriebatur, tunc est de racione Dei mori, et indifferenter quelibet persona divina morietur; patet quod consequencia non valet; cum non sit de racione hominis ita mori, cum homo posset esse naturalissime et nec mori⁸ nec esse mortalis, id est non habere aptitudinem naturalem nec necessitatem preternaturalem, ut suo tempore moriatur. Ideo tales reduplicative sumuntur satis equivoce ut patet in logica, quam equivocacionem specialiter in illa materia oportet attendere.

And if it is objected that, if Christ insofar as God died, then it is of the account of God to die, and each divine person indifferently will die, it is clear that the inference does not hold; since it is not of the account of man so to die, since a man could be most naturally and neither die nor be mortal, that is, not have a natural aptitude nor a preternatural necessity to die in his time.[^21] Therefore such [reduplicatives]{g:reduplicativa} are taken quite [equivocally]{g:equivoce}, as is clear in logic; which [equivocation]{g:equivocacio} it is especially necessary to attend to in that matter.

## ¶11 · 231.32–232.7

> [p. 231] Ex dictis⁸ ergo patet quodammodo qualiter scriptura sacra cum antiquis sanctis doctoribus in materia de Incarnacione [p. 232] Domini ad litteram est sustinenda; et quomodo iacula sophistica modernorum sunt scuto veritatis infallibilis repellenda, ac eorum discordancia equivoca detegenda; ut sic possit theologus in tractando dubia Incarnacionis realia libere sine formidine impugnacionis sophistice procedere in dicenda ad laudem, gloriam, et honorem eiusdem Domini nostri Iesu Christi. Amen.¹

From what has been said, then, it is clear in some measure how holy scripture, with the ancient holy doctors, is to be upheld to the letter in the matter of the incarnation [232] of the Lord; and how the sophistical darts of [the moderns]{g:moderni} are to be repelled by the shield of infallible truth, and their equivocal discordance [exposed]{g:detegere}; so that thus the theologian, in treating the real doubts of the incarnation, may proceed freely, without [misgiving]{g:formido} of sophistical attack, in what is to be said, to the praise, glory, and honour of the same our Lord Jesus Christ. Amen.[^22]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** "Is generated": *generatur*, of an argument that arises from a premiss; the first of the arguments for the three rejected cases (227.27–32).

[^2]: **C.** "As above": the variation in metaphysics and the moderns' ambition are charged earlier in the treatise; "as is clear above": the fathers' "Christ is something he was not before", argued in the earlier chapters.

[^3]: **C/T.** "A study good in its kind": *studium bonum de genere*, an act good by its kind (generically good), short of goodness in all its circumstances. B marks *circa* for omission (228.15); O reads *hominum* for *bonum* (228.16); A B C omit *vel* (228.17).

[^4]: **T.** "Indeed": *quidem*, with all the manuscripts, against the print's *enim* "for", which Harris supplied (228.19: *pro quidem in omn. codd. inventum enim supposui*). A B C read *quod* for *quodlibet*; the print, with O, is kept there. A B C read *disparet* for *disperiret* (228.20).

[^5]: **S.** Thomas, *Scriptum super Sententiis* III d. 1 q. 2 a. 3, counted serially as "question 7", with a. 4–5 "the two following": whether the Father or the Holy Spirit could have been incarnate, whether several persons could assume one nature, whether one person could assume two (LEDGER I13d-1, to be checked).

[^6]: **T.** "For him": *sibi*; A B C omit it (228.26).

[^7]: **C.** "The assumption": *assumptum*, the logician's premiss (here the quoted proposition), not the assumption of a nature (GLOSSARY *assumere*). "Errs": *peccat*.

[^8]: **C/T.** "In which he takes": *quo capit*, Thomas's second argument. "Of all things equally distant": *omnium*; A B C *omni* (229.6).

[^9]: **T.** "Of dignity": *dignitatis*; A B C *dignitas* "is a dignity" (229.10).

[^10]: **S/C.** "With this saint": Thomas, who grants the Father could have been incarnate, and (230.1–2) that three persons so incarnate would be one man (LEDGER I13d-2, to be checked). The last words are in Harris's italics, as a formula, and are quoted here; *ordinata / absoluta* is the pair of God's ordained and absolute power.

[^11]: **T.** "More qualified": *habilior*, the print's reading; A B C read *humilior* "humbler", and O has *hîlior*, an abbreviation that may stand for either (229.20). With O doubtful the manuscripts do not agree across the families, and the print stands. O reads *in* before *illa* (229.18), *assumpsit* (229.19), and omits *humanitas a qualibet* (229.22).

[^12]: **T.** B omits *et … organo* (229.26–29); for *uniri* (229.30) A B put *copulari* at the end of the sentence, and C has neither.

[^13]: **C.** The case yields both "three persons would be one man" (as Thomas grants) and, by what has been said, "it would not be so"; hence the case is impossible. A B C read *queritur* (229.31); A B *possunt* (229.32); O *in* for *et* (230.2).

[^14]: **S.** Scotus, *In III Sent.* (*Ordinatio*) d. 1, on whether several persons can assume one nature (LEDGER I13d-3, to be checked).

[^15]: **T.** "Another flesh": *aliam*, with C O, against the print's *aliquam* "any flesh" (230.6). "The topic from likeness": *locus a simili*.

[^16]: **S/T.** "The *De anima*": Harris italicizes the words as a title; whether Aristotle's or a treatise of Wyclif's is not determined (LEDGER I13d-4, to be checked). C reads *unierit*, O *vineret* for *uniret* (230.15).

[^17]: **T.** "As it signifies": *ut dicit*, with all the manuscripts, against the print's *ut dicat* "so as to signify" (231.1), Harris's emendation. B omits *causam*; O omits *aliqua … et* (230.20) and *ut dicat* (230.24), and reads *in* for *et* (230.26) and *divinitas sint / sit* for *deitas fuit* (230.27, 230.29); C omits *vel … causa* (230.30–31).

[^18]: **T.** "Anything": *quidquam*; O *quemquam* "anyone" (231.11). "Foreign": *alienam*, a nature other than the divine; O *aliam* "another" (231.13). A B C omit *sit* (231.15).

[^19]: **S.** 1 Timothy 2:5. A omits *mediatorem* (231.17).

[^20]: **C.** The two reduplications are kept apart here: *in quantum* in the wide causal sense just defined (231.1–3), *secundum quod* of the formal cause only, as *secundum deitatem* above. B omits *formaliter … sibi* (231.19).

[^21]: **C/T.** "Of the account of": *de racione*, part of what it is to be God or man. O reads *mîori* for *mori* (231.27).

[^22]: **R.** After *Amen* B adds: "Here ends the treatise *de incarnacione* of M. I. Wy., in the year of the Lord 1433. To the scribe for his pen let everlasting joys be given.
