# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 6, part 4: that Christ is created: the doctors' sense, and the objection "from nothing"

## ¶1 · 96.30–97.9

> [p. 96] Unde Doctor Subtilis super Distinccione 11. 3ⁱⁱ Sentenciarum dicit quod hec proposicio—Christus est creatura—est concedenda simpliciter, si bene intelligatur, sed negabatur propter hereses dicencium Christum esse pure creaturam: [p. 97] et fingit postmodum responsiones ei, cui non placet sic dicere. Concedit autem consequenter simpliciter cum Augustino quod Christus incepit esse¹ sicut generabatur ex tempore. Unde Doctor Bonaventura super eadem Distinccione dicit quod doctores magis hoc² respiciunt, quod potest esse via in falsitatem vel errorem, quam quod ipsa de se sit falsa vel erronea. Unde concedit³ consequenter quod Christus est homo creatus et non⁴ homo eternus, cum sit secundus Adam habens multos homines seniores.

Hence the [Subtle Doctor]{g:doctor-subtilis}, on distinction 11 of the third [book] of the *Sentences*, says that this [proposition]{g:proposicio}, "Christ is a [creature]{g:creatura}", is to be granted [without qualification]{g:simpliciter}, if it is well understood, but that it was denied because of the heresies of those who said "Christ is purely a creature":[^1] [97] and afterwards he devises replies for one who is not pleased to speak so. And consequently he grants without qualification, with Augustine, "Christ began to be", as he was begotten in time.[^2] Hence the doctor Bonaventure, on the same distinction, says that the doctors look rather to this, that it can be a way into falsity or error, than that it is of itself false or erroneous. Hence he consequently grants that Christ is a created man and not an eternal man, since he is the second Adam, having many men older [than he].[^3]

## ¶2 · 97.10–98.3

> [p. 97] Quandocunque ergo ego dico, quod Christus est creatus vel factus, intelligo catholice, sicut⁵ scriptura sacra et sancti doctores me edocent, quod Christus secundum humanitatem est creatus⁶ vel taliter temporaliter denominatus. Et illam regulam dat⁶ sanctus Thomas 4°. Contra Gentiles 48. ‘Nihil, inquit, Deo proprium enunciatur de Christo nisi racione humanitatis vel expressate vel subintellecte: nec⁷ e contrario quoad denominaciones eternas, quas homo ille recipit secundum divinitatem.’ Et sic eum gloso ac alios quosdam, quando dicunt quod Christus non est creatura; intelligunt enim quod Christus non est secundum divinitatem creatura: quod est necessarium, cum oppositum sit hereticum. Et quod ille sit sensus suus videtur, quia super 2ᵐ. Sentenciarum Dist. 11. Quest. 4⁸ dicit quod accidencia creata dicuntur de Filio Dei, non quod ipsa persona eterna his⁹ informatur, sed quod natura assumpta⁹ his¹⁰ informatur. Quod dictum ego nescio sane concipere nisi intelligendo quod persona Verbi non informetur¹¹ accidentibus creatis secundum deitatem, sed quod ipsa principaliter informetur eis secundum humanitatem. Nam Verbum predicat et agit actos humanos, informatur virtutibus et beatitudine, sicut substancialiter informatur anima intellectiva et creata racionabilitate.¹² Ideo certum est quod persona Verbi informatur istis accidentibus ac formis creatis, nisi coincidatur in opinionem negantem [p. 98] mobilitatem Christi simpliciter. Et illa glosa est evidencior et famosior quam glosa data a modernis ad scripturam sacram et dicta sanctorum.

Whenever, therefore, I say "Christ is created" or "made", I understand [it] catholicly, as holy scripture and the holy doctors teach me: that Christ [as to the humanity]{g:secundum-humanitatem} is created, or is in such a way named temporally. And Saint Thomas gives that rule, *Contra Gentiles* IV, 48: "Nothing proper to God," he says, "is stated of Christ except by reason of the [humanity]{g:humanitas}, either expressed or [understood as implied]{g:subintelligere}; and conversely as to the eternal denominations, which that man receives as to the [divinity]{g:divinitas}."[^4] And so I [gloss]{g:glosare} him and certain others, when they say "Christ is not a creature"; for they understand "Christ is not a creature as to the divinity": which is necessary, since the opposite is heretical. And that that is his sense appears, since on the second [book] of the *Sentences*, distinction 11, question 4, he says that created accidents are said of the Son of God, not because the eternal [person]{g:persona} itself is informed by them, but because the [assumed]{g:assumere} [nature]{g:natura} is informed by them.[^5] That saying I do not know how to conceive soundly except by understanding that the person of the [Word]{g:verbum} is not informed by created accidents as to the [Godhead]{g:deitas}, but that the person is principally informed by them as to the humanity. For the Word preaches and performs human acts, is informed with virtues and blessedness, just as he is substantially informed by an intellective soul and created rationality. Therefore it is certain that the person of the Word is informed by these accidents and created forms, unless one falls into the opinion denying [98] the [movability]{g:mobilitas} of Christ without qualification. And that [gloss]{g:glosa} is more evident and [better known]{g:famosior} than the gloss given by the moderns to holy scripture and the sayings of the saints.

## ¶3 · 98.4–98.19

> [p. 98] Quidam enim dicunt quod talis modus loquendi transit¹ in abusum: alii quod doctores desipuerunt ignorantes logicam: tercii² quod dixerunt impossibilia et heretica propter devocionem³ exhortacionis.³ Sed quarti recte intelligunt quod dicta intelliguntur de Christo secundum humanitatem; et ipsi vere intelligunt, specialiter si ponant quod Christus est illa humanitas et non in construccione synecdochica; cum illa humanitas sit totus Christus, et non⁴ pars eius⁴ formaliter. Synecdochica enim locucio⁵ vel evidencia est⁵ a quo⁶ ad simpliciter, quando parti principaliter attribuitur denominacio, et secundario suo toti; ut hic homo est racionalis vel quantus. Unde quantum ad priores glosantes dicitur quod affeccione inordinata extraneandi in logica, postposita glorificacione scripture, ‘dicentes se esse sapientes stulti facti sunt’: in cuius signum vix duo concordant tam in sentencia quam in glosa.

For some say that such a manner of speaking has passed into abuse; others, that the doctors were foolish, being ignorant of logic; a third [group], that they said impossible and heretical things out of the devotion of exhortation. But the fourth rightly understand that the sayings are understood of Christ as to the humanity; and these truly understand, especially if they posit "Christ is that humanity", and not in a [synecdochic construction]{g:construccio-synecdochica}; since that humanity is the whole Christ, and not a part of him formally. For a [synecdochic locution]{g:synecdochica-locucio} or [evidence]{g:evidencia} is from that in which [it holds] to [what holds] without qualification, when the [denomination]{g:denominacio} is attributed principally to a part, and secondarily to its whole; as "this man is rational" or "[quantified]{g:quantus}".[^6] Hence as to the former glossers [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that, by an inordinate affection for estrangement in logic, setting aside the glorifying of scripture, "professing themselves to be wise, they became fools":[^7] as a sign of which hardly two agree, either in [teaching]{g:sentencia} or in gloss.

## ¶4 · 98.20–98.33

> [p. 98] Sed obicitur tripliciter contra illud. Primo per hoc quod, si Christus creatus est, tunc est productus ex nichilo, et per consequens aliquando non fuit: quod est impossibile, cum sit Deus. Sed rogo sic arguentes attendere quomodo per⁷ idem non sequeretur⁷ quod, si⁸ Christus generatus est ex tempore, conceptus, et natus de virgine, ergo habuit esse post non esse: cum omnis generacio substancie sit produccio a non esse substancie ad esse secundum philosophos. Nec negandus est ‘liber generacionis Iesu filii David, filii Abrahe,’ ut patet Matth. 1°; vel conceptus⁹ aut partus virginis, ut patet Luce 1° et 2°; duplicem enim nativitatem Christi catholici concorditer confitentur. Nec est racio quod creacio non competit¹⁰ Christo secundum esse suum temporale, sicut competit sibi generacio aut incepcio.

But [it is objected]{g:obicitur} in three ways against that. First, by this: that if Christ is created, then he is produced from nothing, and consequently at some time he was not: which is impossible, since he is God. But I ask those who argue so to consider how, by the same token, it would not follow that, if Christ is begotten in time, conceived, and born of the virgin, therefore he had [being]{g:esse} after not-being: since every generation of a substance is a production from the not-being of the substance to being, according to the philosophers.[^8] Nor is "the book of the generation of Jesus, the son of David, the son of Abraham" to be denied, as is clear from Matthew 1;[^9] nor the conception or the childbearing of the virgin, as is clear from Luke 1 and 2; for catholics confess with one accord a twofold nativity of Christ. Nor is there a reason why creation should not belong to Christ as to his temporal being, just as generation or beginning belongs to him.

## ¶5 · 99.1–99.18

> [p. 99] Conceditur ergo quod Christus creatus est nedum secundum animam, que est Christus, sed secundum corpus. Nam generatus est secundum corpus, et hoc generacione univoca sicut univoce est homo. Sed omnis alia generacio substancie presupponit eius creacionem: ergo et Christi generacio temporalis. Omnis itaque temporalis nativitas, sicut et motus nature create sensibilis aut insensibilis, presupponit creacionem ut causam, cum prius naturaliter sit creacio simpliciter quam aliqua mocio naturalis. Ex quo plane sequitur, quod omne naturaliter motum prius naturaliter creatur quam naturaliter sic movetur. Cum ergo Christus naturaliter movetur, et per consequens accidens creatum inest sibi formaliter; sequitur quod prius naturaliter est creatus¹: quia aliter¹ indubie non esset denominatus univoce cum ceteris creaturis. Conceditur ergo quod Christus productus est ex nichilo, et sic aliquando non fuit. Primum patet de anima et de corporea essencia in mundi principio; et secunda pars patet intelligendo Christum secundum illam naturam, qua creatus est.

[99] It is granted, therefore, that Christ is created [not only]{g:nedum} as to the soul, which is Christ, but as to the body. For he was begotten as to the body, and that by a univocal generation, as he is [univocally]{g:univoce} man. Now every other generation of a substance presupposes its creation: therefore so does the temporal generation of Christ. So every temporal nativity, as also every motion of created nature, [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} or [imperceptible]{g:insensibilis},[^10] presupposes creation as its cause, since creation without qualification is naturally prior to any natural motion. From which it plainly [follows]{g:sequitur} that everything naturally moved is naturally created before it is naturally so moved. Since, then, Christ is naturally moved, and consequently a created accident is in him formally, it follows that he is naturally created first: for otherwise without doubt he would not be named univocally with the other creatures. It is granted, therefore, that Christ was produced from nothing, and so at some time was not. The first is clear of the soul, and of the bodily essence at the beginning of the world; and the second part is clear by understanding Christ as to that nature by which he was created.

## ¶6 · 99.19–99.33

> [p. 99] Sed in istis² oportet cavere diligenter et excludere tres sensus erroneos quando dicitur Christum aliquando non fuisse. Primo cavendo ne credatur Christum³ aliquando secundum deitatem non fuisse, cum Christus eternaliter fuit Deus. 2°· ne credatur Christum secundum esse intelligibile humanitatis ac predestinacionem Dei aliquando non fuisse, cum eternaliter habet esse intelligibile predestinatum esse Filius Dei. Et 3° ne credatur aliquando non habere esse⁴ essencie materialis, cum⁵ massa essencie materialis⁵ et corporee, que est Christus, sit in mundi principio et non possit deficere. Exclusis ergo istis tribus sensibus erroneis, claret quartus,⁶ verus sensus catholicus, quod Christus secundum existenciam humanitatis aliquando non fuit. Quod⁷ cum sit verum⁷ et implicans Christum aliquando non fuisse, eo quod ipse est illa humanitas, est catholice concedendum.

But in these [matters] one must take care diligently and exclude three erroneous senses when it is said "Christ at some time was not". First, by taking care that it is not believed "Christ at some time was not, as to the Godhead", since Christ was eternally God. Second, that it is not believed "Christ at some time was not, as to the [intelligible being]{g:esse-intelligibile} of the humanity and the predestination of God", since he eternally has intelligible being, predestined to be the Son of God.[^11] And third, that it is not believed "[he] at some time did not have the being of material essence", since the mass of material and bodily essence which is Christ is [there] at the beginning of the world and cannot fail. These three erroneous senses, therefore, being excluded, the fourth, the true catholic sense, shines out: "Christ, as to the existence of the humanity, at some time was not". Which, since it is true and implies "Christ at some time was not", because he is that humanity, is to be granted catholicly.

## ¶7 · 99.34–100.18

> [p. 99] Quod si moveat quemquam quod Christus est creatus vel [p. 100] factus ex nichilo; rogo, quomodo diceretur, si pro instanti incarnacionis corpus Christi subito per¹ totum creatum limitaretur,¹ et anima tunc² creata sibi pro eodem instanti uniretur, et pro eodem instanti natura completa ypostatice sumeretur? Numquid credimus quod Christus non tunc temporaliter produceretur? Baptisemus³ ergo illam faccionem sive produccionem subitam. Et patet quod non superest nomen competens nisi ‘creacio’ cum tota humanitas a solo Deo⁴ producitur ex puro⁵ nichilo precedente temporaliter in effectum: et cum produccio per se et proprie terminatur ad suppositum, relinquitur, quod Christus in illo casu sit factus ex nichilo; et sic omnia argumenta, que viderentur⁶ militare contra creacionem Christi in⁷ faccione ex virgine, procederent contra illud preter hoc, quod essencia corporea, que est Christus, creata⁸ est in mundi principio et interim per patriarchas ac demum per beatam virginem ministerialiter alterata; et revera hoc, licet sit⁹ gloriosius,⁹ est magis mirabile.

And if it troubles anyone that Christ is created or [100] made from nothing: I ask, how would it be put if at the instant of the incarnation the body of Christ were suddenly bounded, created throughout, and the soul, created then, were united to it at the same instant, and at the same instant the complete nature were taken [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice}?[^12] Do we believe that Christ would not then be produced in time? Let us baptise, then, that making or sudden production.[^13] And it is clear that no fitting name remains but "creation", since the whole humanity is produced by God alone from pure nothing that precedes the effect in time;[^14] and since production [of itself]{g:per-se} and properly terminates at the [supposit]{g:suppositum}, it remains that Christ in that case is made from nothing; and so all the arguments that would seem to militate against the creation of Christ in the making from the virgin would proceed against that [case], except for this, that the bodily essence which is Christ was created at the beginning of the world, and meanwhile altered [ministerially]{g:ministerialiter} through the patriarchs and at last through the blessed virgin; and in truth this, though it is more glorious, is more wonderful.

## Notes

[^1]: **S.** Duns Scotus, on *Sent.* III d. 11, whether "Christ is a creature" is to be granted, *Ord.* III d. 11 q. 1 n. 6 (Wadding VII/1, 240): *propositio est simpliciter concedenda, si bene intelligatur: negabatur tamen communiter a Sanctis propter haereses haereticorum dicentium Christum esse puram creaturam*, and n. 7, *Cui ista opinio non placet, potest dicere ipsam esse falsam*. Scotus reports the opinion (*aliter dicitur*), and Wadding's margin gives it to William of Ware (*verified*, LEDGER I06d-1).

[^2]: **S.** Scotus's grant, with Augustine, of "Christ began to be"; *Ord.* III d. 11 q. 3 (Wadding VII/1, 249–250): *de vi sermonis posset concedi, quod Christus … incepit esse*, with the argument *quod generatur incipit esse simpliciter … Christus generabatur*. Scotus names no Augustine there. The Augustine is the one the Lombard gives, *Sent.* III d. 12 c. 1, from *In Ioh.* 105, PL 35, 1907: *priusquam mundus esset, nec nos eramus, nec ipse mediator Dei et hominum homo Christus Jesus* (*verified*, LEDGER I06d-2). *Esse* here is existence, as the clause "as he was begotten in time" shows; O lacks *esse* (97.3).

[^3]: **S.** Bonaventure, *In III Sent.* d. 11 a. 2 q. 1 (Quaracchi III, 249–250): resp. *talis sermo est propinquus errori … male intelligibilis*, and ad 5, *ista admittatur et concedatur: Christus est homo creatus*. The second Adam with his *seniores* is Wyclif's own reason (*verified*, LEDGER I06d-3). For *non homo eternus* (97.8) A B C read *unus homo eternus*; the print follows O.

[^4]: **T/C.** *Et e contrario* "and conversely" (97.16), with A B C, against the print's *nec e contrario* "nor conversely" (O): the second clause states the converse rule (the eternal names are said of that man by reason of the divinity), and "nor" denies it. Thomas, *SCG* IV.48 (end): *Quod autem est personae proprium, de Christo non enuntiatur ratione humanae naturae nisi cum aliqua additione, vel expressa vel subintellecta*. Wyclif's *Deo proprium* is Thomas's *personae proprium* (being created without qualification), and his *nisi racione humanitatis* compresses Thomas's added determination. So the print has lost no negative. What is proper to the person is said of Christ, by reason of the humanity, only with that qualification expressed or understood. The converse clause is Wyclif's own (*verified*, LEDGER I06d-4).

[^5]: **S.** "On the second [book] … question 4" as printed; A B C give question 9 (97.23). The place is Thomas, *In III Sent.* d. 11 q. 1 a. 4 ad 1: *proprietates creatae dicuntur de filio Dei, non quasi ipsa persona aeterna his informetur, sed quia natura assumpta informatur eis*, as Harris saw. The print's 4 is the article, A B C's 9 does not fit, and the second book is an error for the third (*verified*, LEDGER I06d-5).

[^6]: **T.** *A quo ad simpliciter* (98.12–13): the print with O and A's second hand; B reads *a secundum*, C *a s'*, that is, "from 'in a certain respect' to 'without qualification'", the form the print gives at 94.28. All the manuscripts order the words *locucio est vel evidencia*, which changes nothing in the English.

[^7]: **S.** Rom. 1:22 (DR).

[^8]: **S.** Aristotle's account of generation as change from not-being to being in substance (*Physics* V.1, 225a12–17: change from not-being to being, its contradictory, is coming to be, and from unqualified not-being, coming to be without qualification; cf. *De generatione* I.3). Scotus uses the premiss on the same question, *Ord.* III d. 11 q. 3: *secundum Aristotelem 5. Physic. generatio est a non esse ad esse simpliciter* (*verified*, LEDGER I06d-6).

[^9]: **S.** Matt. 1:1, with *Iesu* for the Vulgate's *Jesu Christi* (DR "of Jesus Christ").

[^10]: **C.** *Sensibilis aut insensibilis* may go with *nature* rather than with *motus*: "of created nature, whether it perceives or not".

[^11]: **C.** *Predestinatum esse Filius Dei* echoes Rom. 1:4 (DR "who was predestinated the Son of God"). *Esse intelligibile* is being as known in God, set against the existence of the humanity (99.31).

[^12]: **C.** *Per totum creatum limitaretur*: "were bounded, created throughout", that is, the whole body given its limits at once, without growth. M, a summary, has *crearetur et liniamentaretur* "were created and given its lineaments" (100.2–3).

[^13]: **C.** *Baptisemus*: let us give it its name (O *baptizaremus*); the name follows in the next sentence.

[^14]: **C.** *Precedente temporaliter in effectum*: the nothing precedes in time the thing produced; *in effectum* is taken with *precedente*, not as the formula *in effectu* "in actual fact".
