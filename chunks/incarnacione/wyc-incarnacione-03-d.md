# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 3, part 4: the common speech of the faithful and the body in the tomb

## ¶1 · 40.16–42.2

> [p. 40] Et si⁶ obiciatur,⁷ quod ista posicio, vocando ‘hominem’ corpus mortuum et animam separatam, sequitur sentenciam plebeiorum,⁸ et per consequens est erronea, ut multa alia dicta vulgaria; dicitur quod non est color in consequencia; cum dicta vulgaria a prima veritate communitati⁹ hominum inspirata non possunt in toto esse falsa. Unde Salvator Ioh. 12°. approbat turbe¹⁰ sentenciam, que dixit: ‘Nos audivimus ex lege quod Christus manet in eternum. Adhuc, inquit, modicum lumen in vobis est; ambulate dum lucem habetis, ut¹¹ non tenebre vos comprehendant; dum lucem habetis,¹¹ credite in lucem, ut filii lucis¹² sitis.’ Quasi diceret¹² iuxta exposicionem beati Augustini Ome. 52. ‘Verum est principium quod de Christo concipitis, scilicet quod ipse eternaliter manet humanitus cognoscendo. Sed oportet ultra procedere, quod non obest eundem hominem¹³ esse mortuum corporaliter et cum hoc mente vivere. Ambulate, inquit Augustinus, attendite et totum intelligite tam moriturum¹⁴ Christum quam [p. 41] victurum in eternum. Cum aliquid, inquit, verum habetis, non sitis comprehensi tenebris ignorancie¹ contempnendo mortuum et occisum² hinc trahentem ad se omnia, et sic offendatis in lapidem offensionis et petram scandali.’ Unde Ome. 78³ super istud⁴ Ioh. 14. ‘non turbetur cor vestrum:⁵ declarando Christum esse duplicem formam, scilicet humanitatem et deitatem, subdit de eius humanitate. ‘Non, inquit, homo Christus, non dixit⁶ ‘‘Filius Dei,’’ quod eciam sola caro eius in sepulcro meruit appellari?’ Probat. ‘Cum,⁷ inquit, credimus in Dei Filium qui sepultus est, profecto et Filium Dei dicimus carnem que sola sepulta est.’ Et post declarando quomodo ad verba est⁸ verum, ‘Pater⁸ maior me est,’ per hoc quod est duarum formarum utraque, ita subdit: ‘Quid ergo, heretice?⁹ cum Christus sit Deus et homo, loquitur ut homo; et tu calumpniaris Deo?¹⁰ Ille in se commendat naturam humanam; et tu in illo audes deformare divinam? Infidelis, ingrate,¹¹ nonne tu minuis eum qui te fecit, quia ille dicitur factus¹² sic¹² propter te? Equalis enim Patri est Filius per quem factus est homo; ut minor esset Patre, factus est homo; quod¹³ nisi fieret, quid esset homo?¹³ agnoscamus, inquit, geminam substanciam Christi: divinam¹⁴ scilicet, qua equalis est Patri; et humanam, qua minor est Patre. Utrumque autem simul; non duo, sed unus est Christus, ne sit quaternitas non Trinitas Deus.¹⁵ Sicut enim unus est homo, anima racionalis et caro, Christum, inquit, in omnibus istis et¹⁶ singulis confitemur. Probat hoc. Quis est ergo, per quem factus est mundus? Christus Iesus; sed in forma Dei. Quis est sub Poncio Pilato crucifixus? Iesus Christus; sed in forma servi. Item de singulis ex quibus¹⁷ homo constat. Quis non¹⁸ derelictus est in inferno? Christus [p. 42] Iesus; sed in anima sola. Quis resurrecturus in triduo iacuit in sepulcro? Christus Iesus; sed in carne sola.’

And if it is objected that this [position]{g:posicio}, by calling a dead body and a separated soul "man", follows the [teaching]{g:sentencia} of the common folk, and consequently is erroneous, like many other popular sayings, [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that there is no [colour]{g:color} in the [inference]{g:consequencia};[^1] since popular sayings inspired by the first truth in the community of men cannot be wholly false. Hence the Saviour in John 12 approves the teaching of the multitude, which said: "We have heard out of the law that Christ abideth for ever. Yet a little while, he says, the light is among you. Walk whilst you have the light, that the darkness overtake you not; whilst you have the light, believe in the light, that you may be the children of light."[^2] As if he said, according to the exposition of blessed Augustine, Homily 52:[^3] "The principle that you conceive of Christ is true, namely that he abides eternally, knowing [humanly]{g:humanitus}.[^4] But you must go further: that nothing prevents the same man being dead bodily and with this living in mind. Walk, says Augustine, attend, and understand the whole: Christ as much about to die as [41] about to live for ever. Since you have something true, he says, be not overtaken by the darkness of ignorance, despising the one dead and slain who from there draws all things to himself, and so stumble against the stone of stumbling and the rock of scandal."[^5] Hence in Homily 78, on that [verse] of John 14, "Let not your heart be troubled",[^6] [making clear]{g:declarare} that Christ is a twofold form, namely [the humanity]{g:humanitas} and [the Godhead]{g:deitas}, he adds about his humanity: "Is not, he says, the man Christ [the] 'Son of God', which even his flesh alone in the sepulchre deserved to be called?"[^7] He proves it: "When, he says, we believe in the Son of God who was buried, surely we call the flesh too, which alone was buried, the Son of God." And afterwards, making clear how it is true to the words "the Father is greater than I",[^8] through this, that he is each of two forms, he adds thus: "What then, heretic? Since Christ is God and man, he speaks as man; and do you slander God? He commends human [nature]{g:natura} in himself; and do you dare in him to disfigure the divine? [Unbeliever]{g:infidelis}, ungrateful one, do you not diminish him who made you, because he is said to have been made thus for your sake? For the Son, through whom man was made, is equal to the Father; that he might be less than the Father, he was made man; and if this were not done, what would man be? Let us acknowledge, he says, the twin substance of Christ: the divine, namely, by which he is equal to the Father; and the human, by which he is less than the Father. And both together; not two, but one is Christ, lest God be a quaternity, not a Trinity. For as man is one, rational soul and flesh, [so] we confess Christ, he says, in all these and in each.[^9] He proves this. Who then is he through whom the world was made? Christ Jesus; but in the form of God. Who was crucified under Pontius Pilate? Jesus Christ; but in the form of a servant. Again, of each of the things of which man consists. Who was not left in hell? Christ [42] Jesus; but in the soul alone. Who, to rise again, lay in the sepulchre in [the three days]{g:triduum}? Christ Jesus; but in the flesh alone."

## ¶2 · 42.3–42.15

> [p. 42] Hec sentencia huius sancti que, sicut non potest dissolvi, sic non cognosci, nisi viso quod Christus est trium naturarum incommunicancium quelibet. Qui ergo voluerit illam materiam¹ verissimam de virtute sermonis infringere, incipiat communicacionem persone² Christi multis naturis, non multis personis, ut deitas, improbare. Parcant igitur emuli huic vie quod naturali instinctu a vulgo sit cognita; quod a scriptura sacra et sanctis doctoribus sit tam assidue declarata; et quod mentes fidelium substrata sentencia non fallaci sed veridica excitat pro cura mortuorum, ut sepultura cum aliis exequiis devocius peragenda. Eadem enim persona hominis, que heri viva conversata est cum proximis, deportatur hodie mortua ad ecclesiam, et deponitur in sepulcro.

This is the teaching of this saint, which, just as it cannot be dissolved, so cannot be known, except once it is seen that Christ is each of three natures [that do not communicate]{g:incommunicantes}.[^10] Whoever therefore would break down that most true matter [in the proper sense of the words]{g:de-virtute-sermonis}, let him begin by disproving the communication of the [person]{g:persona} of Christ to many natures, not to many persons, as the Godhead [is communicated].[^11] Let the rivals therefore spare this way, because it is known to the common people by natural instinct; because it is so assiduously made clear by holy scripture and the holy doctors; and because, the teaching laid beneath it being not deceptive but truthful, it stirs the minds of the faithful to the care of the dead, so that burial with the other [exequies]{g:exequie} is to be performed more devoutly. For the same person of a man who yesterday, living, kept company with his neighbours, is carried today dead to the [church]{g:ecclesia}, and laid in the sepulchre.

## ¶3 · 42.16–42.27

> [p. 42] Nec oportet timere ampullosas³ instancias sophisticas quibus nituntur istam sentenciam impugnare dicendo, quod frivolum est credere racione corporum mortuorum vel personarum sanctarum in celo Deum facere miracula: cum, ut false garriunt, non est corpus sancti mortuum,⁴ nec sunt reliquie sanctorum, nec est possibile Petrum Iacobum vel Iohannem ante resurreccionem orare, sicut in letaniis⁵ canit illusa ecclesia per ignoranciam logice⁶ minus caute. Quicunque ergo sophista logicus⁷ vel naturalis vel methaphisicus vere voluerit ista defendere vel probare, si dicta factis compensat,⁸ est simul excellencior⁹ philosophus et objector¹⁰ quam Deus hucusque dignatus est sensibus mortalium propalare.⁹

Nor need one fear the bombastic sophistical [objections]{g:instancia} with which they strive to attack this teaching, saying that it is frivolous to believe that God works miracles by reason of dead bodies or of holy persons in heaven: since, as they falsely prattle, there is no dead body of a saint, nor are there relics of the saints, nor is it possible that Peter, James or John pray before the resurrection, as the deluded church sings in the litanies, through ignorance of logic, too incautiously.[^12] Whoever therefore, a [sophist]{g:sophista}, whether logician or natural philosopher or metaphysician, would truly defend or prove these things, if he squares his deeds with his words, is at once a more excellent and a more abject philosopher than God has hitherto deigned to disclose to the senses of mortals.[^13]

## ¶4 · 42.29–43.16

> [p. 42] Nec credo sophistam ad tantum desipere quod credat, si sola caro Christi fuit Christus in sepulcro et sola anima fuit Christus in inferno, quod¹¹ tunc sola caro vel sola anima fuit [p. 43] Christus. Ac si sic¹ argueretur solus Petrus est homo² in domo ista: ergo solus Petrus est homo² alicubi tenendo quidem diccionem exclusive, sicut facit Augustinus quando dicit³ quod nec sola caro nec sola anima fuit Christus, sed humanitas completa, que differt a parte qualitativa: patet decepcio sophistica introducta; et sicut dixi superius, quando Augustinus dicit quod pars qualitativa non est homo, exponit se ipsum quod non est completus homo, id est, natura composita⁴ ex corpore et anima: et sic intelligit 17⁵ de Trinitate ca. 7. exponens se ipsum quod intelligit ‘hominem’ ut veteres diffinierunt⁶ per ‘animal racionale mortale,’ vel iuxta descripcionem suam ‘substanciam racionalem constantem ex corpore et anima.’ Et⁷ per hoc distinguit inter triplicitatem naturarum in homine et trinitatem anime ac Trinitatem increatam. Affirmative itaque sue sunt concedende et negative modificande.

Nor do I believe that a sophist is so far foolish as to believe, if the flesh of Christ alone was Christ in the sepulchre and the soul alone was Christ in hell, that then the flesh alone or the soul alone was [43] Christ. As if it were argued thus: "Peter alone is a man in this house: therefore Peter alone is a man anywhere", holding the word indeed exclusively, as Augustine does when he says that neither the flesh alone nor the soul alone was Christ, but the complete humanity, which differs from a [qualitative part]{g:pars-qualitativa}: the sophistical deception introduced is clear.[^14] And as I said above, when Augustine says that a qualitative part is not a man, he explains himself, that it is not a complete man, that is, a nature composed of body and soul: and so he understands [it in] *De Trinitate* 17, c. 7, explaining himself, that he understands "man" as the ancients defined [it], by "rational mortal animal", or according to his own [definition]{g:descripcio}, "a rational substance consisting of body and soul".[^15] And by this he distinguishes between the threeness of natures in man and the trinity of the soul and the uncreated Trinity. His affirmatives therefore are to be granted and his negatives qualified.

## ¶5 · 43.17–44.6

> [p. 43] Ulterius si queratur, utrum verum sit in triduo quod hoc est corpus Christi et hec anima Christi pro nunc; dicitur⁸ quod sic: cum⁹ Christus vere sit tam corpus quam anima pro triduo, quo simul iacet mortuus et descendit vivus ad inferos, et sic persona viva est mortua secundum diversas naturas. Et movent me tria. Primo auctoritas scripture et exposicio beati Augustini superius recitata.¹⁰ Secundo quod aliter non esset concedendum quod Christus sit homo per illud triduum, quod nimis invaderet fidem meam. Et tercio quod doctores tam priores sapienciores quam moderniores capaciores¹¹ dicunt concorditer quod Christus nichil de humana natura dimisit quod¹² assumpsit, sed mansit per triduum unitus tam carni¹³ quam anime sicut ante. Cum igitur unione ypostatica, que non est per accidens unio ypostatica, fuerat antea copulatus;¹⁴ sequitur quod per illud triduum manet tam caro quam anima sicut prius. Unde [p. 44] dicunt loquentes nostri temporis concorditer quod sacerdos conficiens pro illo triduo conficeret dicendo verba sacramentalia corpus mortuum. Sed quomodo, queso, per verba falsissima¹ quibus diceret ‘hoc est corpus meum’ vere conficeret, nisi corpus confectum pro tunc fuerit corpus Christi?

Further, if it is asked whether it is true in the three days that this is the body of Christ and this the soul of Christ for now, the answer is that it is: since Christ truly is both body and soul for the three days, in which he at once lies dead and descends living into hell, and so a living person is dead as to diverse natures. And three things move me. First, the [authority]{g:auctoritas} of scripture and the exposition of blessed Augustine recited above. Second, that otherwise it would not have to be granted that Christ is man through that three days' space, which would [exceedingly]{g:nimis} assail my [faith]{g:fides}. And third, that the doctors, the earlier and wiser as much as the more modern and more capable, say in concord that Christ [laid aside]{g:dimittere} nothing of the human nature that he [assumed]{g:assumere}, but remained through the three days united as much to the flesh as to the soul, as before.[^16] Since therefore he had been joined before by a hypostatic [union]{g:unio}, which is not a hypostatic union by accident, [it follows]{g:sequitur} that through that three days' space he remains both flesh and soul, as before.[^17] Hence [44] those who speak in our time say in concord that a priest [consecrating]{g:conficere} during those three days would, by saying the sacramental words, consecrate the dead body. But how, I ask, by most false words, in which he would say "This is my body", would he truly consecrate, unless the body consecrated were at that time the body of Christ?

## ¶6 · 44.7–44.26

> [p. 44] Unde sanctus Thomas de Christo ca°. 50 videtur dicere² quod idem sit corpus Christi mortuum³ pro illo triduo quod⁴ fuit corpus Christi post et ante; et hoc racione unionis ad Verbum; licet quelibet pars quantitativa⁵ illius corporis racione forme substancialis substancialiter variatur. Teneo igitur⁶ primam partem huius sentencie et vario in 2ᵃ.; cum oportet formas substanciales esse subordinatas anime, ut patet ex dictis De Anima. Nec video quin, si ad omnem punctum corporis Christi generata sit nova forma⁷ substancialis preter quamcunque formam substancialem que infuit Christo vivo, foret aliud novum corpus mortuum secundum quamlibet eius partem quantitativam quam fuerat corpus vivum. Nec video 2°. quomodo illud esset tunc corpus Christi, nisi⁸ tunc Christus esset Christus, et⁸ esset illi novo⁹ corpori nova unione ypostatice copulatus.¹⁰ Nec video 3°. quomodo illud corpus sit mortuum, cum sit per totum noviter generatum, et nunquam antea fuit vivum. Dico ergo quod idem corpus in numero tam secundum materiam quam secundum¹¹ formam corporis, quod prius participative vixit, est in triduo corpus mortuum et cum hoc Christus Deus noster.

Hence Saint Thomas, *On Christ*, c. 50, seems to say that the dead body of Christ for those three days is the same as was the body of Christ after and before; and this by reason of the union to [the Word]{g:verbum}; although each [quantitative part]{g:pars-quantitativa} of that body is varied substantially by reason of the substantial form.[^18] I hold therefore the first part of this teaching and differ in the second; since the substantial forms must be subordinated to the soul, as is clear from what is said in *De anima*.[^19] Nor do I see but that, if at every point of the body of Christ a new substantial form were generated beyond whatever substantial form was in Christ living, there would be another new dead body, as to each quantitative part of it, than the living body had been. Nor do I see, second, how that would then be the body of Christ, unless then Christ were Christ, and were joined [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} to that new body by a new union.[^20] Nor do I see, third, how that body is dead, since it is newly generated through the whole, and never before was alive. I say therefore that the same body in number, as to the matter as much as to the form of the body, which before lived [participatively]{g:participative}, is in the three days a dead body and with this Christ our God.

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** 40.19 *non est color*: "colour" in the period sense, a plausible ground: the inference from "the common folk say it" to "it is erroneous" has none. *Sentencia* (40.17) is "teaching" throughout this chunk, a position held, not a sentence passed.
[^2]: **S.** John 12:34–36. Wyclif runs the multitude's words (12:34) into Jesus' reply (12:35–36) with *inquit*, leaving out the Vulgate's *Dixit ergo eis Jesus* ("Jesus therefore said to them") and 12:35b ("and he that walketh in darkness knoweth not whither he goeth"); *quod* for the Vulgate's *quia*. A B C shorten 40.24–25 to *etc. et*.
[^3]: **S.** Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 52 (on John 12:31–36), given as a paraphrase, not a quotation; the place is to be checked (LEDGER I03d-1).
[^4]: **C.** 40.29 *manet humanitus cognoscendo*: the gerund is taken with *ipse* ("he abides, knowing humanly"); it may instead qualify the hearers' grasp of *manet* ("abides, if one understands it humanly").
[^5]: **S.** 41.3 *trahentem ad se omnia*: John 12:32, "will draw all things to myself". 41.4: Rom. 9:33 and 1 Pet. 2:8 (Isa. 8:14), "a stumbling-stone and a rock of scandal".
[^6]: **S.** Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 78 (on John 14:28); "Let not your heart be troubled" stands at John 14:27 as well as 14:1. To be checked (LEDGER I03d-2).
[^7]: **T.** 41.8 *non "Filius Dei"*, with A B C, against the print's *non dixit "Filius Dei"* (O); the print (*Non, inquit, homo Christus, non dixit "Filius Dei"* ...) does not construe ("Not, he says, the man Christ, did he not say 'Son of God' ...").
[^8]: **S.** John 14:28, "the Father is greater than I".
[^9]: **T.** 41.24–26 *Sicut enim unus est homo, anima racionalis et caro, Christum ... confitemur*: the comparison has no second limb, and "[so]" is supplied. Augustine's tractate is to be checked for a lost clause, such as "so one is Christ, God and man" (LEDGER I03d-2).
[^10]: **C.** 42.3 *Hec sentencia huius sancti que ...*: the period has no main verb; "This is" is supplied, taking *hec* as the subject and *sentencia* as the predicate. The three natures are the Godhead, the soul and the body.
[^11]: **C.** 42.7–8 *ut deitas*: the Godhead is communicated to many persons (the Trinity); Christ's person is communicated to many natures, not to many persons.
[^12]: **C.** 42.19–23 *cum, ut false garriunt ...*: the opponents' claims, with their gibe at the church. On their view a saint is a man and a man is body and soul together, so before the resurrection there is no saint's dead body and no Peter to pray, as the litanies ask (*Sancte Petre, ora pro nobis*).
[^13]: **T.** 42.26 *abiectior*, with A B C O (O abbreviated *abic'or*), against the print's *objector* ("a more excellent philosopher and objector"). A B C have the whole clause otherwise: *excellencior vel abiectior philosophia* (C *philosophus*) *quam deus unquam hucusque sensibus mortalium voluit propalare*, "a more excellent or more abject philosophy than God has ever yet willed to disclose"; the English follows O's wording, the print's but for that word. 42.25 *si dicta factis compensat*: if he lives as he talks (shunning the relics); O *dictis facta compenset*.
[^14]: **C.** 43.1–3 *tenendo quidem diccionem exclusive*: *diccio* is the word *sola*/*solus*, taken as an exclusive. "The flesh alone was Christ in the sepulchre" no more yields "the flesh alone was Christ" than "Peter alone is a man in this house" yields "Peter alone is a man anywhere". O omits 43.1–2 *in domo ... homo*.
[^15]: **S.** 43.9 *17 de Trinitate ca. 7*: O reads *19*; *De Trinitate* has fifteen books. Both definitions stand in book 15, c. 7 (15.7.11). To be checked (LEDGER I03d-3). *Diffinierunt* and *descripcionem* stand together here: both are "define / definition".
[^16]: **T.** 43.27 *moderniores capaciores*: A B C read *capciosiores*, "the more modern and more captious".
[^17]: **C.** 43.31–32 *manet tam caro quam anima*: the subject is Christ, carried on from *copulatus*: he remains both flesh and soul, as he was both before.
[^18]: **S.** Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* III q. 50 a. 5 (whether Christ's dead body was the same in number), the part of the *Summa* on Christ; to be checked (LEDGER I03d-4). B omits *dicere*; A B C omit *quantitativa*.
[^19]: **S.** 44.13 *ut patet ex dictis De Anima*: Harris italicises *De Anima* as a title; whether Aristotle's *De anima* or a treatise of Wyclif's own on the soul is meant is to be checked (LEDGER I03d-5).
[^20]: **T.** 44.19–20 *nisi tunc Christus esset Christus*: A reads *nisi Christus tunc esset christo*; C *nisi illud tunc esset christus*, "unless that [body] were then Christ", which fits the argument better. The print is rendered: Christ would have to be Christ over again, by a new union.
