# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 12, part 2: against one man with many humanities: motion, goodness, size, and multiplied principles

## ¶1 · 205.25–206.3

> [p. 205] Sed contra illud tripliciter arguitur.⁷ Primo posito quod duo corpora coextendantur et utrumque simul pro eodem loco et tempore gradiatur, videtur quod sunt duo motus progressivi,⁸ sicut due quantitates et cetera accidencia coextensa. Oppositum tamen videtur, cum motus individuatur sufficienter a spacio, tempore, et subiecto; sed idem est homo in numero, idem tempus et spacium; ergo cum nulla pars quantitativa hominis graditur, eadem est mocio progressiva. Et per idem posito quod secundum unam humanitatem [p. 206] penetraret progrediendo multas continuas humanitates¹ quiescentes continue,² idem Christus simul et semel secundum idem quiesceret,² et motu contrario moveretur.

But against that, in three ways.[^1] First, supposing that two bodies are coextended and each walks at once for the same place and time, it seems that there are two progressive motions, just as [there are] two quantities and the other [accidents]{g:accidens} coextended. Yet the opposite seems [true], since a motion is individuated sufficiently by space, time and [subject]{g:subiectum}; now the man is the same in number, the time and the space the same; therefore, since no [quantitative part]{g:pars-quantitativa} of the man walks, the [progressive motion]{g:mocio-progressiva} is the same.[^2] And by the same token, supposing that as to one [humanity]{g:humanitas} [206] he were to penetrate, in going forward, many continuous humanities that are continually at rest, the same Christ would at one and the same time be at rest in the same respect, and be moved with a contrary motion.[^3]

## ¶2 · 206.4–207.10

> [p. 206] Similiter iuxta illud sequitur quod Christus infinitum bonum animal potest esse, quia mensura bonitatis rei composite capitur a principiis intrinsecis cum disposicionibus formalibus in eadem. Ex quo sequitur quod Christus racione multitudinis humanitatum foret melior homo quam si solum unam assumeret. Patet consequencia, supposito primo quod Christus sit tantum finite bonus iuxta illam vocem Veritatis, ‘Pater maior me est,’ Ioh. 14.³ Ymo si foret infinitum bonus homo racione deitatis, foret per idem infinitum antiquus, et per consequens primus homo, consequens contra Apostolum prima ad Cor. 15. ‘primus, inquit, homo de terra terrenus, et secundus homo de celo celestis.’ Et ita oportet limitare in Christo omnia alia⁴ accidencia, que habet humanitus, sic quod non infinitum bonum corpus vel animam habet Christus; et per idem non infinitum magnus senex vel antiquus homo est Christus, nec infinitum potens ac virtuosus vel sciens homo, quia sic excederet limites speciei eciam secundum hominem assumptum; et per consequens non conveniret nobiscum in specie; que contradicunt supradictis. Quo supposito patet quod Christus existens bonus homo racione unius humanitatis foret melior homo racione alterius humanitatis incommunicantis coassumpte; cum bonitatem magnam acquireret nullam aliunde deperdens. Nec superest in quo per humanitatem melioraretur⁵ nisi in esse hominis. Et per idem foret in duplo melior homo per assumpcionem duarum humanitatum quam per assumpcionem unius solummodo; et sic de aliis denominacionibus accidentalibus⁶ consequentibus ad easdem. Ex quo videtur primo quod infinitas humanitates assumendo foret eque bonus homo ut Deus, vel unum infinitum foret reliquo maius. Videtur 2ᵈᵒ [p. 207] quod infinitum melior potest¹ esse asinus quam homo, posito quod infinitas naturas asininas Verbum assumeret naturam hominis dimittendo. Et per consequens sequitur 3° quod species non assimilatur numeris, cum omnis creata substancia magis et minus susciperet² ut unus homo³ esset⁴ reliquo in duplo magis homo propter humanitatem duplicem; et sic de qualibet proporcione racionali reali;⁵ et per consequens Christus racione multitudinis naturarum excederet in humanitate quodcunque aliud individuum speciei.

Likewise, according to that, it follows that Christ can be an infinitely good animal, because the measure of the goodness of a composite thing is taken from its intrinsic principles together with the formal dispositions in it. From which it follows that Christ, by reason of the multitude of humanities, would be a better man than if he [assumed]{g:assumere} only one. The [inference]{g:consequencia} is clear, it being supposed first that Christ is only finitely good, according to that word of the Truth, "The Father is greater than I," John 14[:28].[^4] Indeed, if he were an infinitely good man by reason of the [Godhead]{g:deitas}, he would by the same token be infinitely old, and consequently the first man: a [consequent]{g:consequens} against the Apostle, 1 Corinthians 15[:47]: "The first man," he says, "was of the earth, earthly, and the second man, from heaven, heavenly."[^5] And so one must limit in Christ all the other accidents which he has [humanly]{g:humanitus}, so that Christ does not have an infinitely good body or soul; and by the same token Christ is not an infinitely great old man or ancient man, nor an infinitely powerful and virtuous or knowing man, because so he would exceed the limits of the species even as to the [assumed man]{g:homo-assumptus}; and consequently he would not agree with us in species: which contradicts what was said above.[^6] This supposed, it is clear that Christ, being a good man by reason of one humanity, would be a better man by reason of another humanity [that does not communicate]{g:incommunicans} [with it], assumed along with it; since he would acquire great goodness, losing none from elsewhere. Nor is anything left in which he would be bettered through the humanity, except in the being of a man. And by the same token he would be a man doubly better by the [assumption]{g:assumpcio} of two humanities than by the assumption of one only; and so of the other accidental [namings]{g:denominacio} following on them.[^7] From which it seems, first, that by assuming infinite humanities he would be as good a man as God, or one infinite would be greater than another. It seems, second, [207] that an ass can be infinitely better than a man, supposing that the [Word]{g:verbum} assumed infinite asinine natures, [laying aside]{g:dimittere} the [nature]{g:natura} of man. And consequently it follows, third, that species are not likened to numbers, since every created substance would admit of more and less, so that one would be doubly more a man than another on account of a double humanity; and so of any [rational proportion]{g:proporcio-racionalis}; and consequently Christ, by reason of the multitude of natures, would exceed in humanity any other [individual]{g:individuum} of the species.[^8]

## ¶3 · 207.11–208.5

> [p. 207] 3° videtur quod ex omni genere accidentis corporei potest esse composicio parcium intensive. Nam coextensis humanitatibus secundum materias possent partes quantitative materierum et suorum accidencium coextendi; et per consequens, cum omnia sunt forme vel materie eiusdem suppositi, sequitur quod per talem composicionem posset unum componi non extensive; ergo intensive; quod est impossibile. Et [sic]⁶ de materia et ceteris accidentibus, que secundum philosophos⁷ non possunt magis vel minus suscipere. Nam eque magnus foret Christus secundum unam humanitatem, sicut⁸ foret⁹ Christus secundum⁹ infinitas equales insimul coextensas. Nec sapit quod Christus sit intensius septipedalis quam prius. Et hic videtur possibile¹⁰ quod infinitum magnus iam est Christus, et nullam quantitatem deperdet, et tamen in fine erit solum septipedalis, posito quod infinitas humanitates equales assumat ad modum coree¹¹ iuxta positas; sic¹² quod omnes ille sese penetrantes coextendantur in fine sine augmentacione¹³ vel diminucione; et patet conclusio. Et sic infinitum magnum potest continue maiorari continue adquirendo quantitatem ac nullam penitus deperdendo quousque infinitum parvum fuerit, ut alibi declaratur. Et conformis est consideracio [p. 208] de motu, ut infinitum intensus potest esse motus per motuum incommunicancium extensionem,¹ cum hoc quod mobile quantumcunque tarde ad velocissimum moveatur. Et ex istis potest calculator elicere quotlibet conclusiones nimis mirabiles, quia regularum de velocitate motuum destructivas.

Third, it seems that in every genus of bodily accident there can be a composition of parts intensively. For, with humanities coextended as to their matters, the quantitative parts of the matters and of their accidents could be coextended; and consequently, since all are forms or matters of the same [supposit]{g:suppositum}, it follows that by such a composition one [thing] could be composed not extensively; therefore intensively; which is impossible.[^9] And [so] of matter and the other accidents, which according to the philosophers cannot admit of more or less.[^10] For Christ would be as great as to one humanity as Christ would be as to infinite equal [humanities] coextended all together. Nor does it make sense that Christ should be more intensely seven feet tall than before. And here it seems possible that Christ is now infinitely great, and will lose no quantity, and yet at the end will be only seven feet tall, supposing that he assume infinite equal humanities placed side by side after the manner of a ring-dance, so that all of them, penetrating one another, are coextended at the end without [augmentation]{g:augmentacio} or diminution; and the conclusion is clear.[^11] And so the infinitely great can continually be increased, continually acquiring quantity and losing none at all, until it is infinitely small, as is made clear elsewhere.[^12] And the consideration [208] of motion is conformable: a motion can be infinitely intense through the extension of motions that do not communicate, with this, that the [movable]{g:mobile} is moved however slowly toward the swiftest. And from these a calculator can elicit any number of [exceedingly]{g:nimis} marvellous conclusions, since they destroy the rules on the velocity of motions.[^13]

## ¶4 · 208.6–208.17

> [p. 208] Item hoc corpus et hec anima cum ista unione ypostatica et aliis que secuntur sunt sufficiencia principia ad faciendum Christum esse hominem; cum illis sive² aliis quibuscunque² positis, hoc sequatur. Sed multiplicatis completis principiis oportet multiplicari effectum formaliter consequentem. Ergo si sint multe humanitates assumpte, sunt multi homines; ut si Christus iam ad humanitatem habitam assumeret aliam humanitatem incommunicantem, illa assumpcio cum sit sufficiens principium, sufficit facere Christum iterum esse hominem; et sic quociens illa principia fuerint geminata.³ Ex quo sequitur quod si Christus multas humanitates assumeret, foret multi homines consequenter.

Again, this body and this soul, with this hypostatic [union]{g:unio} and the other things that follow, are sufficient principles for making Christ to be a man; with these, or with any others whatever, posited, this would follow. Now when complete principles are multiplied, the effect that follows formally must be multiplied. Therefore, if there are many humanities assumed, there are many men; as, if Christ now, besides the humanity he has, assumed another humanity that does not communicate [with it], that assumption, since it is a sufficient principle, suffices to make Christ to be a man again; and so as often as those principles have been generated.[^14] From which it follows that, if Christ assumed many humanities, he would consequently be many men.

## ¶5 · 208.18–208.30

> [p. 208] Confirmatur tripliciter. Primo si aliqua principia individuant Christum, ut homo potissime foret⁴ corpus et anima; et per consequens variatis illis oportet individuum hominis variari. Aliud ergo est Christum esse istum hominem ex hoc corpore et hac anima integratum, et aliud esse⁵ istam personam ex alio corpore et alia anima compositum quia aliter indubie non foret Christus aliud per humanitatis assumpcionem quam foret eternaliter, si solummodo foret Deus. Et patet sentencia sancti Thome, quod Verbum si assumeret multas humanitates ut Iohannem, Petrum et cetera, foret correspondenter multi homines. Verbum igitur esse hominem foret communis humanitas ad ipsum esse Iesum, Petrum etc.

This is confirmed in three ways. First, if any principles individuate Christ, as a man, it would above all be body and soul; and consequently, when these are varied, the individual of man must be varied.[^15] It is one thing, therefore, for Christ to be this man, integrated from this body and this soul, and another to be that [person]{g:persona}, composed from another body and another soul; because otherwise, without doubt, Christ would not be anything other through the assumption of the humanity than he would be eternally if he were only God. And the [teaching]{g:sentencia} of St Thomas is clear, that the Word, if he assumed many humanities, as John, Peter and so on, would correspondingly be many men.[^16] The Word's being a man, therefore, would be a humanity common to his being Jesus, Peter, etc.[^17]

## ¶6 · 208.31–209.9

> [p. 208] Similiter impossibile est formas completas eciam accidentales multiplicari simul et semel, nisi eo ipso multiplicentur supposita. Sed omnis humanitas est forma, cum sit illud [p. 209] quo substancia formaliter est homo; ergo multiplicatis humanitatibus multiplicabuntur et homines. Assumptum declaratum est de formis, accidentibus, et earum substanciis proximis, ut si sint multe ambulaciones simul et semel oportet multa supposita ambulare; et ita de aliis; ergo a maiori vel pari, cum humanitas sit suppositum esse hominem non individuata ex successione temporis vel adquisicione adventicii accidentis, quod positis multis humanitatibus multi homines consecuntur.

Likewise, it is impossible for complete forms, even accidental ones, to be multiplied at one and the same time, unless by that very fact the supposits are multiplied. Now every humanity is a form, since it is that [209] by which a substance is formally a man; therefore, when humanities are multiplied, men too will be multiplied. [The assumption]{g:assumptum} has been [made clear]{g:declarare} of forms, accidents, and their proximate substances: as, if there are many walkings at one and the same time, many supposits must walk; and so of the others; therefore, from the greater or from the equal, since the humanity is a supposit's being a man, not individuated by succession of time or by the acquiring of an adventitious accident, [it follows] that, when many humanities are posited, many men follow.[^18]

## ¶7 · 209.10–209.26

> [p. 209] 3°· idem patet, posito quod Verbum assumat multas embriones et post completas humanitates¹ animabus congenitis, patet quod anime advenientes, cum sint forme substanciales, faciunt Verbum esse aliud; et per consequens non relinquitur quid² aliud nisi illum hominem. Prius enim fuit completus Iesus et per consequens per posteriores animas non fit Iesus. Et si dicatur quod anime advenientes non faciunt Christum esse aliud sed alterius³ modi,³ tunc sunt forme accidentales advenientes enti in actu et non eiusdem racionis cum ceteris animabus, que in quantum⁴ huius modi, faciunt corpora animata. Patet igitur, si experiencia et principia philosophica salvari debeant, quod Verbo assumente multas humanitates est multi homines. Nam numerus hominum, qui est sensibile commune ex 2ᵈᵒ De Anima, ita patescit sensui secundum quantitates continuas dislocatas et secundum quotlibet differencias accidentales illarum naturarum, sicut innotescit aliquis sensibilis numerus alicui.

Third, the same is clear, supposing that the Word assume many embryos, and, after the humanities have been completed with souls generated along with them, it is clear that the souls that come to them, since they are [substantial forms]{g:forma-substancialis}, make the Word to be [another thing]{g:aliud}; and consequently nothing else is left but that man.[^19] For before, Jesus was complete, and consequently he is not made Jesus through the later souls. And if it be said that the souls that come do not make Christ to be another thing but [to be] of another mode, then they are accidental forms coming to a [being]{g:ens} in act, and not [of the same kind]{g:eiusdem-racionis} as the other souls, which, [insofar as]{g:in-quantum} [they are] of this kind, make bodies [animate]{g:animare}.[^20] It is clear, therefore, if experience and the principles of philosophy are to be saved, that, the Word assuming many humanities, he is many men. For the number of men, which is a [common sensible]{g:sensibile-commune}, from the second [book] *On the Soul*, is as plain to sense, according to continuous quantities set in different places and according to any number of accidental [differences]{g:differencia} of those natures, as any perceptible number becomes known to anyone.[^21]

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** "In three ways": C O omit *arguitur* "it is argued" (205.25), A B with the print. O with C, across the families, against the print: the English follows them, and the verb is understood. The three: motion (205.25), goodness (206.4), intensive composition (207.11). B reads *successivi* "successive" for *progressivi* (205.28).

[^2]: **C.** "Now the man is the same": *sed* brings the minor premiss, closed by *ergo*. The argument sets the two coextended bodies of Christ's two humanities against the rule that a motion is individuated by space, time and subject: one subject, one motion. *Motus* and *mocio* are both "motion" here; Wyclif does not set them against each other.

[^3]: **C/T.** "In the same respect": *secundum idem*, the condition under which contraries cannot hold of one thing; one humanity moves through the others while they rest, so Christ both rests and moves. O omits *humanitates* (206.1) and *continue … quiesceret* (206.2–3), losing the rest.

[^4]: **T.** John 14:28; O cites chapter "10" (206.11).

[^5]: **S.** 1 Corinthians 15:47; Wyclif adds *inquit* and *et* "and" before *secundus*, which the Vulgate lacks. The argument: an infinitely good man by reason of the Godhead would by parity be infinitely old, the first man, against Paul's "second man".

[^6]: **C.** "Limit": restrict, not assign. "As to the assumed man": the limits of the species bind Christ even as he is the assumed man; if any accident of his humanity were infinite he would not share our species, against the identity of species argued in earlier chapters. A reads *illa* for *alia* (206.16).

[^7]: **C/T.** "Doubly better": *in duplo melior*, goodness counted per humanity, the absurdity the argument draws out. O reads *melioratur* (206.28); A B C omit *accidentalibus* "accidental" (206.31), O with the print.

[^8]: **C/S/T.** "Species are not likened to numbers": Aristotle, *Metaphysics* VIII.3 (1043b33–1044a11), that a definition, like a number, becomes another species by any addition, and that, as number does not admit of more and less, neither does substance as form (1044a9–11; LEDGER I12b-2, verified); "admit of more and less": substance does not, *Categories* 5 (3b33–4a9; LEDGER I12b-3, verified). *Suscipere* here is "admit of", not the taking-up of a nature. "Rational proportion": a ratio of whole numbers. "So that one would be": *ut unus esset*, with C O, against the print's *ut unus homo esset* (207.5); "any rational proportion": with C O, who omit *reali* "real" (207.7). O reads *suscipiat* (207.5), *est* (207.6); B omits *potest* (207.1).

[^9]: **C.** "Intensively": as a quality grows in degree in one subject, not by adding parts side by side; coextended humanities would compose one quantified thing without extension, so quantity would be intensified, which is impossible.

[^10]: **T/C.** "And [so] of matter": the manuscripts read *Et de materia* (O *de materia*); Harris supplies *sic* against them all (207.18). The English follows the manuscripts and supplies "so" in brackets as the ellipsis requires. B reads *philosophum* "the Philosopher" (207.19).

[^11]: **C/T.** "After the manner of a ring-dance": *coree*, A B C (O *sefẽ*); Harris asks whether *choreae* is meant, as at 196. The humanities stand in a ring and then all penetrate one another, so that infinite quantity ends as seven feet without loss. A B C omit *Christus secundum* (207.21), and A C first wrote *simul* for *sicut*; B reads *impossibile* (207.24); O omits *sic* (207.27). O and M read *alteracione* "alteration" for *augmentacione* (207.28): the print keeps the pair of quantity, augmentation and diminution.

[^12]: **S.** "As is made clear elsewhere": not identified; Harris gives no reference, and the Wyclif Society volumes searched have no parallel (cf. the sophismata on *infinitum magnum* and *infinitum parvum* in *De logica*; LEDGER I12b-4, untraced).

[^13]: **C/T.** "Toward the swiftest": *ad velocissimum*, perhaps "up to the swiftest [speed]"; the motion would be infinitely intense though the movable moves however slowly. The "calculator" is one practised in the Oxford calculations of intension and velocity. O reads *coextensionem* for *extensionem* (208.2), in keeping with the coextended humanities; the print stands.

[^14]: **T/C.** "Generated": *genita*, with B C O, against the print's *geminata* "doubled", A's reading (208.15); the principles are produced again with each new humanity. "Now when complete principles": *sed*, the minor, closed by *ergo*. "With these, or with any others whatever, posited": O reads *sive plurimum positis* (or *positivis*) (208.8).

[^15]: **C/T.** "As a man": *ut homo*; or "so that the man would above all be body and soul". A B C read *forent* "they would be" (208.19). "To be that person": *esse*, A B C *est* (208.22), which Harris thinks may stand for *est esse*.

[^16]: **S.** Thomas, *Scriptum super Sententiis* III d. 1 q. 2 a. 5, where the Word assuming many humanities would be many men; not *Summa theologiae* III q. 3 a. 7, which concludes "one man" (ad 2: *Petrus et Jesus non sunt unus homo, sed duo homines*; ad 3: *plures homines, et non unus homo*; LEDGER I12b-1, verified; cf. Iprol-3, I12a-2). "As John, Peter": the humanities of John, of Peter.

[^17]: **C.** The inference turns Thomas's teaching against the alternative: if the Word with many humanities were one man, his being a man would be one humanity common to his being Jesus and his being Peter, a common humanity in place of singular ones.

[^18]: **C.** "The assumption": *assumptum*, the logician's premiss (the minor, that complete forms multiply supposits), not the assumption of a nature. "From the greater or from the equal": *a maiori vel pari*, the argument from a stronger or like case. "A supposit's being a man": cf. 203.22–23 (*personam esse hominem*).

[^19]: **C/T.** The case: the Word assumes many embryos and the humanities are later completed by souls; the later souls make him another thing, and what is left for them to make is only "that man", another man. B reads *humanitatibus* (209.11); C *quin* for *quid* (209.14).

[^20]: **T/C.** "Of another mode": A B C read *alterum* "another one" for *alterius modi* (209.17), O with the print; the reply would make the souls accidental forms, against their being of the same kind as the souls that animate bodies. O reads *christum* for *in quantum* (209.19).

[^21]: **S/G.** Aristotle, *De anima* II.6 (418a17–20): number is among the common sensibles (LEDGER I12b-5, verified). *Sensibile commune*: the "common sensible" of the schools, perceived by more than one sense.
