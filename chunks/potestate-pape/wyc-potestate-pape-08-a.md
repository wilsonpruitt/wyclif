# *De potestate pape*, chapter 8, part 1: the name "pope", and the reason from Rome as capital

## ¶1 · 165.2–166.25

> [p. 165] Iam restat videre si oporteat regulariter Romanum pontificem esse papam et inmediatum ac proximum vicarium Christi in terris. Sed pro nomine pape, notandum, quod in scriptura sacra, si bene habeo, non exprimitur, sed est ab interieccione pape, quod admiracionem signat, derivatum. Unde eis convenit proprie qui in conversacione sunt admirabiles sanctitate; ad quem sensum Ihesus noster est summus papa possibilis, cum de eo scribitur Ysaias IX, 6: Et vocabitur nomen eius admirabilis, consiliarius, Deus fortis, princeps pacis. Olim autem vocati sunt quicumque sancti episcopi sancti pape. Unde epistola VIII jeronimi ad Augustinum sic loquitur: Domino vere sancto et beatissimo pape Augustino Jeronimus salutem. Item, Augustinus epistola XVIII ad Paulinum sic loquitur: Libros beatissimi pape Ambrosii credo habere sanctitatem tuam, eos autem multum desidero quos adversus nonnullos imperitissimos et superbissimos, qui de Platonis libris dominum profecisse contendunt, diligentissime ac copiosissime scripsit. Item, epistola X ad Aurelium scribunt Alpius et Augustinus: Domino beatissimo pape Aurelio; nec fuit iste Aurelius papa episcopus Romanus, ut patet epistola CXXXIX ad Generosum, in qua enumerat omnes episcopos a beato Petro usque ad Anastasium, qui tunc eandem [p. 166] cathedram rexit, et olim communiter vocabantur episcopi pape. Unde Bonifacius comes epistola CII scribit Augustino: Tue sanctitatis epistolam admonitu me terrificantem suscepi; etenim venerabilis papa: Si thorus ille legitimus pavidos habet accessus quo debet puniri supplicio qui divinas temerat raciones? Et epistola III sic in fine alloquitur idem Bonifacius Augustinum: Ora igitur, venerabilis papa, ut talis de inimicis sepe ulcio procedat, ymo ut recitat Archidiaconus: Greci vocant cunctos presbiteros papas; unde ra- cione dotacionis atque dominii baptizatus fuit terminus ut approprietur Romano pontifici. Sed quia non est vis in servacione termini, suppono quod specialiter significet illum episcopum, qui gerit altissime, simillime et propinquissime vicem Christi, sicut fecit Petrus post ascensionem, et regulariter dicimus quod faciunt Romani pontifices. Sed quia videtur mirabilis saltus et infundabilis a Christo ad totum genus Romanorum pontificum regulariter sic saltare, ideo multi adinveniunt vias varias ad istam ficticiam colorandum. Quidam autem dicunt hoc procedere per viam racionis, quidam per viam Petri successionis, et quidam per viam eleccionis.

It now remains to see whether the [Roman pontiff]{g:romanus-pontifex} must as a rule be [pope]{g:papa} and the immediate and nearest [vicar of Christ]{g:vicarius-christi} on earth. But as for the name "pope", note that it is not expressed in holy scripture, if I am right, but is derived from the interjection *papae*, which signifies wonder.[^1] Hence it properly befits those who are wonderful for holiness in their [manner of life]{g:conversacio}; in which sense our Jesus is the highest pope possible, since it is written of him, Isaiah 9:6: "And his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, God the Mighty, the Prince of Peace."[^2] And formerly all holy [bishops]{g:episcopus} whatever were called holy popes. Hence Jerome's eighth letter to Augustine speaks thus: "To the lord truly holy and most blessed pope Augustine, Jerome [sends] greeting." Again, Augustine in letter 18 to Paulinus speaks thus: "I believe your holiness has the books of the most blessed pope Ambrose; and I greatly desire those which he wrote most diligently and copiously against certain most unskilled and most proud men, who contend that the Lord profited from the books of Plato." Again, in letter 10 to Aurelius, Alypius and Augustine write: "To the most blessed lord pope Aurelius"; and this pope Aurelius was not the Roman bishop, as is clear from letter 139 to Generosus, in which he enumerates all the bishops from blessed Peter down to Anastasius, who then ruled the same [166] see;[^3] and formerly bishops were commonly called popes. Hence Count Boniface, in letter 102, writes to Augustine: "I have received your holiness's letter, terrifying me by its admonition; for, venerable pope, if that lawful bed has fearful approaches, with what punishment must he be punished who violates the divine reasons?"[^4] And in letter 3 the same Boniface addresses Augustine thus at the end: "Pray therefore, venerable pope, that such vengeance may often go forth upon enemies." Indeed, as the Archdeacon relates: "The Greeks call all [presbyters]{g:presbiter} popes";[^5] hence by reason of [endowment]{g:dotacio} and [lordship]{g:dominium} the term was baptized so that it should be [appropriated]{g:appropriare} to the Roman pontiff. But since no weight lies in keeping to a term,[^6] I suppose that it signifies specially that bishop who bears the place of Christ most highly, most like him and most nearly, as Peter did after the ascension, and as we say the Roman pontiffs do as a rule. But since it seems a wonderful and groundless leap to leap thus as a rule from Christ to the whole line of Roman pontiffs, therefore many devise various ways to [give colour to]{g:colorare} this [fiction]{g:ficticia}. And some say that this proceeds by way of reason, some by way of the succession of Peter, and some by way of election.

## ¶2 · 166.26–166.30

> [p. 166] Quantum ad primum fingit dominus Ardmacanus libro VII De Questionibus Armenorum racionem multiplicem, prima quia Roma est capitalis civitas christianismi, ideo decet quod capitalis episcopus ibi regat.

As to the first, [the lord of Armagh]{g:dominus-ardmacanus}, in book 7 of the *Questions of the Armenians*, feigns a manifold reason:[^7] first, because Rome is the capital city of Christendom, therefore it is fitting that the capital bishop should rule there.

## ¶3 · 166.31–167.12

> [p. 166] Sed quantum ad istam evidenciam videtur, quod accusat summum pontificem dominum Ihesum Christum, qui elegit Jerusalem passioni, et hoc [p. 167] videtur racionabile, quod ille qui venerat totum genus humanum redimere; in medio terre habitabilis ostenderet medicinam iuxta illud Psalmi LXXIII, 12: Deus autem rex noster ante secula operatus est salutem in medio terre. Secundum cosmimetras Jerusalem ponitur in medio habitabilis, locus autem ille foret convencior summo pontifici quam angulus Europe Roma, quia aliter Christus indubie non taliter elegisset; potissime cum tempore Christi et Augusti Cesaris fuit Roma in medio florum sui dominii; et imperator minus impius quam sequentes tempore apostolorum.

But as to this [evidence]{g:evidencia}, it seems that it accuses the [highest pontiff]{g:summi-pontifices}, the Lord Jesus Christ, who chose Jerusalem for his passion; and this [167] seems reasonable, that he who had come to redeem the whole human race should show the medicine in the middle of the habitable earth, according to that of Psalm 73:12: "But God is our king before ages: he hath wrought salvation in the midst of the earth." According to the cosmographers, Jerusalem is placed in the middle of the habitable [world]; and that place would be more fitting for the highest pontiff than Rome, a corner of Europe,[^8] because otherwise Christ undoubtedly would not have chosen as he did; above all since in the time of Christ and of Augustus Caesar Rome was in the full flower of its lordship, and the emperor less impious than those who followed in the time of the apostles.

## ¶4 · 167.14–167.31

> [p. 167] Iterum videtur quod racio ista, cum stat continue, accusat papas sequentes, qui in maiori parte Romam non rexerant, sed incrassati quieverunt Avinione; et cum pocior racio nunc subesset, eo quod ille locus imperii dicitur esse de pape dominio; et sic remanet multiplex racio; primo et principaliter regendi hunc populum; cum crescentibus racionibus donorum debet secundum beatum Gregorium crescere racio humilius et promcius ministrandi. Nam secundum Apostolum prima Thym. V; Qui non providet suis et maxime domesticis fidem negat et est infideli deterior, papa igitur provideret specialiter Romanis de bonis spiritualibus; cum fit eorum dominus et curatus. Racione dominii debet eos seculariter defendere et racione patris spiritualis et peculiaris debet eos instar Pauli spiritualiter gignere et fovere.

Again, it seems that this reason, since it stands continually, accuses the popes who followed, who for the most part did not rule Rome but, grown fat, rested at Avignon;[^9] and that although a stronger reason would now be present, in that that place of the empire is said to be of the pope's lordship. And so a manifold reason remains: first and principally, the reason for ruling this people; since, as the reasons of gifts grow, the reason for ministering more humbly and more readily ought to grow, according to blessed Gregory.[^10] For according to the Apostle, 1 Tim. 5[:8]: "He that hath not care of his own and especially of those of his house hath denied the faith and is worse than an infidel";[^11] therefore the pope should provide especially for the Romans in spiritual goods, since he is made their [lord]{g:dominus} and [curate]{g:curatus}. By reason of lordship he ought to defend them in secular wise, and by reason of [being] their spiritual and particular father he ought, like Paul, to beget and cherish them spiritually.[^12]

## ¶5 · 167.32–168.26

> [p. 167] Item; si super ista racione dependet racio papatus Romani pontificis, cum ista racio sit secundum fortunam valde mutabilis, sequitur quod oportet papatum proporcionaliter variari, nam [p. 168] tempore apostolorum et diu post non fuit Roma civitas christianismi, vel plenius civitas synagoge sathane, cum imperator et maior pars populi fuerat infidelis, ymo ut declarat dominus Ardmacanus libro VII, cap. XVI De Questionibus Armenorum, ad tantum restrictum est Romanum imperium, ut non solum imperio Magni Canis de Cathay, sed modo insuper imperio Persarum, imperio Usbeck, imperio Soldani et imperio barbarorum sit minor dominio seculari, ymo secundum relacionem eorum, qui remotas visitarunt contratas, sunt multe civitates, que Romam exuperant. Ideo procul a catholico, ut dicat locum illum vel populum necessitare, ut episcopus suus sit optimus sequax Christi. Et ideo infra dicit dominus Ardmacanus cap. XVIII: Mihi, inquit, videtur quod papatus et pontificatus Romanus non sic connectuntur quin ex causa racionali poterunt separari. Et conformiter tollitur secunda racio, qua fingitur quod in capite mundi, ubi est generalior confluxus populi, reciperet ipse efficacius doctrinam Christi et exemplaretur apcius, quomodo principes Romani forent Christo subiecti, nec non tercio fons cleri assisteret fonti secularis dominii, ut milicia cleri corroboret, et clerus principibus consulat et regat secundum evangelium Ihesu Christi.

Again, if the reason for the papacy of the Roman pontiff depends on this reason, since this reason is very changeable according to fortune, [it follows]{g:sequitur} that the papacy must vary proportionally. For [168] in the time of the apostles and long after, Rome was not the city of Christendom, or, more fully, [it was] the city of the synagogue of Satan,[^13] since the emperor and the greater part of the people were [unbelievers]{g:infidelis}; indeed, as the lord of Armagh declares in book 7, chapter 16 of the *Questions of the Armenians*, the Roman empire is restricted to such a degree that it is less in secular lordship not only than the empire of the Great Khan of Cathay, but now besides than the empire of the Persians, the empire of Uzbek, the empire of the Sultan and the empire of the barbarians;[^14] indeed, according to the report of those who have visited remote countries, there are many cities which surpass Rome. Therefore it is far from catholic for a man to say that that place or people necessitates that its bishop be the best follower of Christ. And therefore the lord of Armagh says below, in chapter 18: "It seems to me," he says, "that the papacy and the Roman pontificate are not so connected that they could not be separated for a reasonable cause."[^15] And in conformity with this the second reason is removed, by which it is feigned that in the head of the world, where there is a more general confluence of people, it[^16] would receive the [doctrine]{g:doctrina} of Christ more effectually, and it would be more aptly exemplified how the Roman princes should be subject to Christ; and also, thirdly, that the fount of the [clergy]{g:clerus} should stand beside the fount of secular lordship, so that the [knighthood]{g:milicia} might strengthen the clergy,[^17] and the clergy counsel the princes and rule according to the gospel of Jesus Christ.

## ¶6 · 168.27–169.31

> [p. 168] Ista autem triplex ficticia non est racio, quia in toto continet oppositum. Nam Nero, Dyoclecianus et genus tyrannorum succedencium fuerunt precipua obstacula, quare non currebat in imperio evangelium et lex Christi, sicut patet ex notis cronicis, ymo Constantinus succedens venenando [p. 169] magis diutine dampnificavit ecclesiam; et iterum clerus ex avaricia nimis dampnificavit imperium, et quod plus est dedit principibus perniciosum exemplum de omni genere peccatorum, et quod est infidelissimum, obmutuit incrassatus corripere vel docere. Nam ante dotacionem Petrus, Clemens, Marcellus et alii sancti Romani episcopi usque ad mortem dilexerunt cesarem et eius populum docendo et corripiendo. Sed postquam dotati sunt, laboraverunt vecordia atque blasfemia, vecordia timendo dicere principibus et populis sua vicia, quin pocius lacte adulacionis mollificarunt et dissolverunt suos dominos, ut dando illis temporalia contra regulam Christi dampnificent se et illos. Et tercio prorumpunt in maiores ingratas blasfemias, dicentes quod non habuerunt dotacionem illam a cesare, sed iure successionis Christi et Petri, qui fuerunt summe taliter dominati. Ideo non docet eos visitare principes et eis impertiri aliquid spiritualis doctrine, sed residere ubicumque voluerint, imperatoresque et reges advocare et abiectissima mundana servicia imperialiter ab eis recipere. Non sic fecit Petrus, ideo indubie dissonat scole Christi, cum post multiplicacionem donorum temporalium satagerent visitando donatores, humilius refundere pociora. Sed quis tam ingratus Christo abbati non foret ingratus proximo? Christus enim ob amorem nostri venit summe inops et egenus, ut dicitur II. Cor. VIII ad finem, et ut nos clerici et specialiter Petri vicarii vivamus similiter.

But this threefold fiction is not reason, because as a whole it contains the opposite. For Nero, Diocletian and the line of tyrants who succeeded them were the chief obstacles to the gospel and the [law of Christ]{g:lex-christi} running in the empire, as is clear from well-known chronicles; indeed Constantine, succeeding, by poisoning [169] harmed the church more lastingly;[^18] and again the clergy out of avarice harmed the empire too much, and, what is more, gave the princes a pernicious example of every kind of sin, and, what is most faithless, grown fat, fell dumb to rebuke or to teach. For before the endowment Peter, Clement, Marcellus and other holy [Roman bishops]{g:romanus-episcopus} loved Caesar and his people even to death, by teaching and rebuking. But after they were endowed they laboured under [faint-heartedness]{g:vecordia} and blasphemy: faint-heartedness, in fearing to tell princes and peoples their vices; nay rather, with the milk of flattery they softened and dissolved their lords, so that by giving them [temporal goods]{g:temporalia} against the rule of Christ they harm both themselves and them. And thirdly they break out into greater, [displeasing]{g:ingratus} blasphemies, saying that they did not have that endowment from Caesar, but by [right]{g:ius} of succession to Christ and Peter, who [they say] exercised lordship in this way in the highest degree. Therefore it does not teach them[^19] to visit princes and to impart to them something of spiritual doctrine, but to reside wherever they will, to summon emperors and kings, and to receive from them, imperially, the most abject worldly [services]{g:servicium}. Peter did not do so; therefore it undoubtedly jars with the school of Christ, since after the multiplication of temporal gifts they ought to have busied themselves, by visiting the donors, in paying back more humbly better things. But who so ungrateful to Christ the abbot would not be ungrateful to his neighbour?[^20] For Christ, for love of us, came supremely poor and needy, as is said in 2 Cor. 8 toward the end,[^21] and so that we clerics, and especially the vicars of Peter, might live likewise.

## Notes

[^1]: **S.** Loserth compares Wyclif's English works, ed. Matthew, p. 471, and Hus (his p. 256), who reuse the etymology (Matthew p. 471, *De papa* ch. 7: the name "is newe foundun" and betokens "wundirful"; the Hus page not checked; Loserth also cites Freeman, *William Rufus* I, 292; LEDGER P08a-1). **R.** A in the margin, at 165.14: *Nota Augustinus mirabiliter contra Wy[clif]*, the annotator's protest against the argument from the letters that follow.
[^2]: **S.** Wyclif's citation omits the Vulgate's *Pater futuri saeculi*, "the Father of the world to come", and reads *Deus fortis* as one title; DR's "God the Mighty" fits it.
[^3]: **S.** Loserth's references (Augustine, *Opera* II, Maurist numbering): Jerome to Augustine, Ep. 112 in Jerome's collection (PL 22, 916; Ep. 75 in Augustine's, PL 33, 251); Augustine to Paulinus, Ep. 31 (al. 32); Alypius and Augustine to Aurelius, *ibid.* p. 87 (Ep. 41); to Generosus, Ep. 53 (al. 165), with its list of Roman bishops down to Anastasius (LEDGER P08a-2, editor). Wyclif's letter numbers are those of his own collection. "Alypius" is the modern form of the print's *Alpius*.
[^4]: **S.** The letters of Count Boniface stand in the appendix of spurious pieces in Augustine's *Opera* II (Loserth: App. p. 1; and Ep. 14, al. 194, App. p. 4; PL 33, 1095 and 1098, spurious Epp. 2 and 14; LEDGER P08a-3). **C.** *Divinas … raciones*: perhaps "the divine accounts" or "ordinances"; we keep the literal "reasons".
[^5]: **T.** Loserth reports a gap in the manuscripts after *Archidiaconus*. **S.** The Archdeacon is presumably Guido de Baysio, archdeacon of Bologna, author of the *Rosarium* on the Decretum, the canonists' usual *Archidiaconus*; the passage has not been traced (LEDGER P08a-4, untraced).
[^6]: **C.** *Non est vis in servacione termini*: the canonists' *vis non est facienda in verbis*, "no stress is to be laid on words". Wyclif lets the word go and fixes the sense.
[^7]: **S.** FitzRalph, *Summa in quaestionibus Armenorum* 7 (chapter 15, Paris 1512, fol. 48r, where Richard sets out these reasons, including the one fount for a "twofold stream of rule" and the martyrs' blood (cf. 168.22–26, 171.24); LEDGER P08a-5). **G.** *Fingit*: "feigns", the verb of *ficticia* "fiction" (166.24).
[^8]: **T.** Loserth prints *quam* for the manuscripts' *quia* (167.8). **C.** *Angulus Europe Roma*: "Rome" is in apposition with "a corner of Europe".
[^9]: **R.** The papal court had resided at Avignon from 1309 to 1377 (Clement V settled there in 1309; Gregory XI entered Rome on 17 January 1377; LEDGER P08a-6). Loserth reports *papa* in the manuscripts for *papas* (167.15).
[^10]: **S.** Gregory, *Homilies on the Gospels* 9.1: "when gifts increase, the accounts (*rationes*) for the gifts also grow" (PL 76, 1106; Gregory goes on: *tanto ergo esse humilior atque ad serviendum promptior quisque debet*, Wyclif's *humilius et promcius ministrandi*; LEDGER P08a-7). **C.** Gregory's *racio* is "account", the reckoning to be rendered; Wyclif plays on it beside *racio* "reason", which we keep throughout.
[^11]: **S.** Wyclif's *Qui non providet* … *negat* departs from the Vulgate's *Si quis … curam non habet, fidem negavit*; the English follows his Latin with DR's wording.
[^12]: **S.** 1 Cor. 4:15, "in Christ Jesus by the gospel I have begotten you."
[^13]: **S.** Apoc. 2:9, "the synagogue of Satan".
[^14]: **S.** FitzRalph, *Summa* 7.16 (Paris, 1511, fol. 48; Loserth after R. L. Poole; seen in the 1512 print, fol. 48r, where the list is spoken by FitzRalph's interlocutor John; LEDGER P08a-8). The same chapter (fol. 48r–v) gives the objector's case for Jerusalem, *in medio terre* (Ps. 73:12) and the city of the martyrs, which Wyclif turns to his own use at 167.1–8 and 172.14 ff. Loserth glosses *Canis* as Khan and *Cathay* as China; B reads *Usboth* for *Usbeck* (Özbeg, khan of the Golden Horde). FitzRalph writes only *imperio soldani*; the Mamluk sultan of Egypt is our presumption (LEDGER P08a-9, untraced).
[^15]: **S.** FitzRalph, *Summa* 7.18 (Paris 1512, fol. 50r: *non tamen sic michi videtur annexi quin ex rationabili causa … valeant separari*; LEDGER P08a-10). **T.** Loserth prints *quin*; the manuscripts read *quando* (168.18).
[^16]: **C.** *Ipse* most likely takes up *populus* in *confluxus populi*, "the people"; it could be the pope himself.
[^17]: **C.** The Latin has *milicia cleri corroboret*, literally "that the knighthood of the clergy might strengthen", with no object; we take *cleri* as the object in sense. A word may have fallen out (e.g. *clerum*).
[^18]: **S.** The legend that at Constantine's donation a voice was heard saying "today poison is poured into the church" (Higden, *Polychronicon* IV.26, Rolls Series V, 130: *Hodie infusum est venenum in ecclesia Dei*; cf. 130.4; LEDGER P08a-11).
[^19]: **C.** The subject of *docet* is not stated: most likely the endowment, or the claim just named; possibly the teaching of their school.
[^20]: **T.** The print reads *Christo abbati*, "Christ the abbot"; Loserth reports *Christi* in the manuscripts, "Christ's abbot". **G.** *Ingratus* here is "ungrateful" toward a person (GLOSSARY §D).
[^21]: **S.** 2 Cor. 8:9, "being rich he became poor for your sakes". Loserth notes that the citation is rather 8:9 than the chapter's end.
