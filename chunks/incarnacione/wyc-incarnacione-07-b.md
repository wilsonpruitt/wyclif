# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 7, part 2: wisdom movable before the incarnation: all times present to God

## ¶1 · 109.21–110.17

> [p. 109] Sed obicitur contra hunc sensum per hoc, quod Christus non est ante incarnacionem; et per consequens scriba huius scripture non intendebat ad litteram mobilitatem Sapiencie incarnate. Hinc dicitur quod, quidquid fuerit de consequente, consequencia non valet; quia, cum apud Deum omnia que fuerunt sunt presencia, certum est quod auctor⁵ huius scripture satis cognovit quomodo⁶ ‘Christus maneat in eternum’ iuxta illam vocem vasis eleccionis sue ad Hebre. 13°. ‘Iesus Christus hodie et cras ipse et in secula.’ Et illud egregie declarat Ieronimus in Epist. De Assumpcione Beate Virginis ad Paulam et Eustochium: ‘Christi quidem incarnacionem, hoc est quod Christus suo tempore incarnatur, cognovit patriarcha Abraham iuxta testimonium Veritatis Ioh. 8°. ‘Abraham pater vester exultavit ut videret diem [p. 110] meum,” id est, tempus incarnacionis et non solum diem eternitatis; “vidit et gavisus est.”’ Cui Augustinus Ome. 43 adicit argumentum. ‘Quando, inquit, Abraham, Gen. 24°., misit servum suum, ut peteret uxorem filio suo Ysaac, hoc eum iuramento obstrinxit, ut fideliter quod iubebatur impleret. Magna enim res agebatur, quando Abrahe semini coniugium querebatur. Sed, ut hoc cognosceret servus quod noverat Abraham, quod nepotes non carnaliter affectabat, nec de suo genere carnale aliquid sapiebat; ait servo quem mittebat: “pone manum sub femore meo et iura per Deum celi.” Quid, inquit, sibi vult “Deus celi” ad femur Abrahe?’ Et respondet. ‘Cum per femur genus notatur, illa iuracione signabatur de genere Abrahe venturum in carne Deum celi. Stulti, inquit, reprehendunt prophetam Abraham tamquam pure puerile gesserit in hoc dicto; sed cum credidit benediccionem sui seminis Messiam credidit de suo semine incarnandum.’

But [it is objected]{g:obicitur-per} against this sense, by appeal to this: that Christ is not before the incarnation; and consequently the writer of this scripture did not intend, to the letter, the [movability]{g:mobilitas} of Wisdom incarnate. Hence the answer is that, whatever may be the case with the [consequent]{g:consequens}, the [inference]{g:consequencia} is not valid; for, since with God all things that have been are present, it is certain that the author of this scripture knew well enough how "Christ abideth for ever",[^1] according to that saying of his vessel of election, Hebrews 13: "Jesus Christ, today and tomorrow: and the same for ever."[^2] And this Jerome notably [makes clear]{g:declarare} in the letter On the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin, to Paula and Eustochium: "The incarnation of Christ indeed, that is, that Christ is incarnate in his time, the patriarch Abraham knew, according to the testimony of the Truth, John 8: 'Abraham your father rejoiced that he might see my [110] day', that is, the time of the incarnation and not only the day of eternity; 'he saw it and was glad.'"[^3] To which Augustine, in Homily 43, adds an argument.[^4] "When," he says, "Abraham, Gen. 24, sent his servant to seek a wife for his son Isaac, he bound him by this oath, that he should faithfully fulfil what he was bidden. For a great thing was being done when a marriage was sought for the seed of Abraham. But so that the servant might know what Abraham knew, that he did not desire grandsons carnally, nor savoured anything carnal concerning his own race, he said to the servant whom he was sending: 'Put thy hand under my thigh, and swear by the God of heaven.'[^5] What," he says, "has 'the God of heaven' to do with Abraham's thigh?" And he answers: "Since by the thigh the race is denoted, by that oath it was signified that the God of heaven would come in the flesh from the race of Abraham. Fools," he says, "reproach the prophet Abraham, as though he had done something merely childish in this saying; but when he believed the blessing of his seed, he believed the Messiah was to be incarnate of his seed."

## ¶2 · 110.18–110.26

> [p. 110] Et idem 2°. declarat Ieronimus de prophetia¹ patriarche Ysaac sencientis Christum odore prophetico, Gen. 27. quando dixit, ‘Ecce odor filii mei sicut odor agri pleni, quem benedixit Dominus.’ Et ita de singulis patriarchis. Cum enim ipsi eadem fide salvati sunt nobiscum, patet quod ipsi crediderunt incarnacionem Domini sicut et nos; et per consequens Dominum suo tempore incarnari. ‘Constat, inquit² Ieronimus, tempus non preiudicasse³ sacramento uniti hominis ac Dei.’

And secondly Jerome makes the same clear from the prophecy of the patriarch Isaac, perceiving Christ by a prophetic scent, Gen. 27, when he said, "Behold, the smell of my son is as the smell of a plentiful field, which the Lord hath blessed." And so of each of the patriarchs. For since they were saved by the same faith with us, it is clear that they believed the incarnation of the Lord as we do; and consequently "the Lord is incarnate in his time". "It is evident," says Jerome, "that time has not prejudiced the [sacrament]{g:sacramentum} of man and God united."[^6]

## ¶3 · 110.27–111.25

> [p. 110] Et patet ad obiectus garulos sophistarum, quibus invehunt contra oraciones ecclesie quibus rogat⁴ Dominum⁴ ‘per⁵ incarnacionem, nativitatem, circumcisionem, baptismum, ieiunium, passionem, mortem, resurreccionem et ascensionem a periculis liberari’; ‘Inanis, inquiunt, est oracio suggesta, cum omnia predicta nec sunt nec possunt esse.’ Sed constat quod unumquodque illorum, cum sit nobis preteritum, et futurum suo tempore patribus veteris testamenti, [quod]⁶ [p. 111] vere est pro suo tempore et per consequens causa misericordiam impetrandi: non quod quidquam scrupulosum¹ in nostra lege celamus,² quod non audeamus disputacioni cuiuscunque philosophantis ostendere. Et illum modum loquendi docet Ieronimus ex illo dicto Iude. ‘Iesus, inquit, populum ex Egipto salvans secundo eos, qui non crediderunt, perdidit:’ et Apostolus 1ª ad Cor. 10°: ‘neque temptemus Christum sicut quidam eorum temptaverunt;’ non quod iam esset Iesus Christus natus ex Maria virgine, sed quia illa illa persona, que tempore suo est Christus, est eterna et sempiterno³ Dei consilio incarnata et⁴ materialis essencia semper presto. Sic ergo⁵ sancti patres, in quocunque tempore fuerunt,⁶ sciverunt vere Christum esse, fuisse, et fore; et⁷ proposiciones formate ab eis fuissent vere— Christus est, fuit, vel erit incarnatus—quamvis tunc non fuisset ita⁸ quod Christus est incarnatus. Nam, ut sepe dixi, sicut proposicio potest esse hic vera cum hoc, quod hic non sit suum primarie significatum, sed satis est quod ipsum sit alicubi⁹: correspondenter proposicio potest esse nunc vera cum hoc, quod nunc non sit suum primarie signatum, sed satis est quod ipsum sit aliquando. Et sic intelligendus est Augustinus cum aliis doctoribus quod eadem est fides patrum tam novi quam veteris testamenti. Nam incarnacio, mors Christi, et adventus ad iudicium sunt fides credita, a qua fide absit falsitas, sive ficticia etc.

And [from this] the answer is clear to the chattering objections of the sophists, with which they inveigh against the prayers of the [church]{g:ecclesia} in which it asks the Lord "by the incarnation, nativity, circumcision, baptism, fasting, passion, death, resurrection and ascension to be freed from dangers":[^7] "Empty," they say, "is the prayer put forward, since all the aforesaid neither are nor can be." But it is evident that each one of these, since it is past for us, and future in its time for the fathers of the old testament, [111] truly is for its time,[^8] and consequently is a cause of [obtaining]{g:impetrare} mercy: not that we hide anything doubtful in our law that we would not dare to show to the disputation of anyone who philosophises. And this mode of speaking Jerome teaches from that saying of Jude: "Jesus," he says, "having saved the people out of Egypt, did afterwards destroy them that believed not"; and the Apostle, 1 Cor. 10: "Neither let us tempt Christ, as some of them tempted";[^9] not that Jesus Christ was then already born of the virgin Mary, but because that [person]{g:persona}, who in his time is Christ, is eternal, and incarnate by the everlasting counsel of God, and the material [essence]{g:essencia} [was] always at hand. So, then, the holy fathers, in whatever time they were, knew truly "Christ is, was, and will be"; and the [propositions]{g:proposicio} formed by them would have been true, "Christ is, was, or will be incarnate", although it was not then so that Christ is incarnate. For, as I have often said, just as a proposition can be true here together with this, that its primary [significate]{g:significatum} is not here, but it is enough that it be somewhere: correspondingly a proposition can be true now together with this, that its primary [thing signified]{g:signatum} is not now, but it is enough that it be at some time.[^10] And so Augustine, with other doctors, is to be understood [when he says] that the faith of the fathers of the new as well as of the old testament is the same.[^11] For the incarnation, the death of Christ, and the coming to judgement are the faith that is believed, from which faith let falsity, or [fiction]{g:ficticia}, be far, etc.

## ¶4 · 111.26–112.19

> [p. 111] Unde Gregorius 23° Moralium ca°. 15¹⁰ exponens illud¹¹ Iob. 33°, ‘semel loquetur Deus, et secundo ad¹² ipsum non repetet,’ ita scribit: ‘In Deo ideo dicere quodlibet tempus audacter, licet quod in eo nullum proprie dici¹³ licet.’ Unde¹⁴ notat¹⁴ causam eius 30°. Moralium ca°. 5° dicens quod Deus preteritorum¹⁵ non reminiscitur, cum ipsa que in semetipsis¹⁶ [p. 112] pretereunt eius¹ intuitu semper presencia assistunt: et exemplificat satis notabiliter 34°. Moralium ca°. 5°. tripliciter: unde 9°. Moralium 25 expresserat illud diffusius exponens illud Iob. 10. ‘Numquid sicut dies hominis, dies tui?’ ‘Deo, inquit, nec transacta pretereunt, nec adhuc ventura, que non apparent, desunt; quia is, qui² semper esse habet cuncta sibi presencia, conspicit.’ Et idem dicit Anselmus Monologion 19°. Ideo, ut sepe dixi, nisi omne tempus preteritum vel futurum fuerit Deo presens, paterentur theologi magnas angustias in exposicione³ scripturarum ut Ps. 21°. tempore David dicit dicta Sapiencia: ‘foderunt⁴ manus meas et pedes meos:’ et sic de multis scripturis, que pueriliter essent sine racione posite⁵ sub ita disparatis temporibus nisi ad imprimendum in⁶ nobis quod immensa Dei eternitas coassistit omni tempori preterito et⁷ futuro. Unde ad istum sensum loquitur sapiencia Ecclesiastici 47°: ‘Christus purgavit⁸ peccata ipsius.’ Et patet quod facta instancia non impugnat sensum datum ad istam scripturam, ‘omnibus mobilior est Sapiencia.’

Hence Gregory, Moralia 23, chapter 15, expounding that [text] of Job 33, "God will speak once, and the second time will not repeat it",[^12] writes thus: "In God, therefore, [it is permitted] to speak boldly of any time, although it is permitted that none be properly said in him."[^13] Hence he notes the cause of this in Moralia 30, chapter 5, saying that God does not remember things past, since the very things that in themselves [112] pass away are always present before his gaze: and he gives examples, quite notably, in Moralia 34, chapter 5, in three ways: so in Moralia 9, [chapter] 25, he had expressed it more diffusely, expounding that [text] of Job 10, "Are thy days as the days of man?": "To God," he says, "neither do things done pass away, nor are things yet to come, which do not appear, lacking; for he who always has being beholds all things as present to himself."[^14] And Anselm says the same, Monologion 19.[^15] Therefore, as I have often said, unless every time past or future were present to God, theologians would suffer great straits in the exposition of the scriptures, as [where], Ps. 21, in the time of David, the said Wisdom says: "They have dug my hands and feet": and so of many scriptures, which would childishly and without reason have been set down under times so disparate, were it not to impress on us that the immense eternity of God is present together with every time, past and future. Hence to this sense wisdom speaks, Ecclesiasticus 47: "Christ took away his sins."[^16] And it is clear that the [objection]{g:instancia} made does not impugn the sense given to this scripture, "wisdom is more active than all".

## ¶5 · 112.20–112.32

> [p. 112] Sunt autem multi alii sensus huic littere adaptati.⁹ Ut hi dicunt quod extensive loquendo de motu Sapiencia increata movetur obiective a cognitis terminantibus eius actum. Hi dicunt cum tot sint motus genera quot et entes,¹⁰ et Deus ad omnem punctum mundi continue adquirit dominia, est in illa manerie motus relacionis ‘summe mobilis.’ Tercii autem dicunt quod in omni motu est dare ordinem prioritatis et posterioritatis¹¹ secundum successivam denominacionem¹² subiecti in materia motus, et omne tale secundum esse intelligibile ordinatum et descriptum¹³ est eternaliter in Sapiencia increata, et secundum illas raciones dicitur figurative ‘summe mobilis.’ Sed prior exposicio, cum sit literalior de virtute sermonis, verior, et fidei conformior, plus placeret.

And there are many other senses adapted to this text. Thus some say that, speaking of motion extensively, uncreated Wisdom is moved objectively by the things known that terminate his act. Others say that, since there are as many kinds of motion as there are beings, and God at every point of the world continually acquires [lordships]{g:dominium}, he is, in that [kind]{g:maneries} of motion, [the motion] of relation, "supremely movable".[^17] And a third [group] say that in every motion [one may posit]{g:est-dare} an order of [priority]{g:prioritas} and posteriority according to the successive [naming]{g:denominacio} of the subject in the matter of the motion, and every such thing is, as to [intelligible being]{g:esse-intelligibile}, ordered and described eternally in uncreated Wisdom; and according to those reasons he is said figuratively [to be] "supremely movable". But the first exposition, since it is more literal, [in the proper sense of the words]{g:de-virtute-sermonis}, truer, and more conformed to the faith, would please more.

## ¶6 · 113.1–113.29

> [p. 113] Unde quamvis auctor scripture intenderit omnes istos sensus, primum tamen principalius, ut sit introitus ad alios consequentes. Et sic Augustinus de virtute sermonis salvat textus scripture plus imbrigabiles ut illud Ioh. 14,¹ ‘Sermonem quem audistis non est meus, sed eius, qui misit me, Patris.’ Dicit enim illud Ome. 76ᵃ. esse verum de virtute sermonis: ‘non, inquit, miremur, non paveamus:² non est minor Patre, vel impar sibi: non enim mentitus est; “qui me non diligit, sermones meos³ non servat:” non est hic sibi contrarius, sed forte non⁴ sine misterio ibi dixit pluraliter, hic singulariter; volens hic intelligere semetipsum, qui, cum sit Sermo vel Verbum Dei Patris non autem Sermo sui ipsius, verum dixit ad litteram.’ Quod si grammaticus ex accusativo casu offenditur, potest dici quod facta construccione recta non sit⁵ anthitesis. Nam qui non diligit Christum, non servat sermones suos, id est,⁶ sentencias vel veritates, quas precepit observari. Sed ut manifestet quid ex hoc sequitur, subiungit ‘et sermonem quem audistis’ (supple⁷ ‘non servat’); per quam copulacionem docemur methaphisicam quod omnes huiusmodi veritates eterne sunt idem essencialiter Verbo Dei. Et 3°, ut doceat quod ille Sermo sit idem essencialiter Deo Patri, licet personaliter⁸ distinguatur, subiungit ‘non est meus sed eius, qui misit me, Patris.’ Licet autem sensus catholicus posset esse quod sermo vocalis non sit Verbi ex se, modo quo Apostolus dicit solum Patrem habere immortalitatem;⁹ ut exponit Augustinus primo De Trinitate et primo contra Maximinianum caᵒ. 10; tamen¹⁰ prior sensus est subtilior et mihi¹¹ carior.

[113] Hence, although the author of scripture intended all these senses, yet [he intended] the first more principally, so that it might be an entrance to the others that follow. And so Augustine, in the proper sense of the words, saves the more [troublesome]{g:imbrigabilis} texts of scripture, as that of John 14, "The word which you have heard is not mine; but the Father's who sent me." For he says in Homily 76 that it is true in the proper sense of the words: "Let us not," he says, "wonder, let us not be afraid: he is not less than the Father, or unequal to himself: for he did not lie: 'He that loveth me not keepeth not my words': he is not here contrary to himself, but perhaps not without a [mystery]{g:misterium} he spoke there in the plural, here in the singular; wishing here to understand himself, who, since he is the [Speech]{g:sermo} or [Word]{g:verbum} of God the Father, but not the Speech of himself, said what is true to the letter."[^18] And if the grammarian is offended by the accusative case, it can be said that, once the construction is made right, there is no [antithesis]{g:anthitesis}.[^19] For he who does not love Christ does not keep his words, that is, the [teachings]{g:sentencia} or truths which he commanded to be observed. Now, to make plain what follows from this, he adds, "and the word which you have heard" (supply "keepeth not"); by which conjunction we are taught the metaphysics that all such eternal truths are essentially the same as the Word of God. And thirdly, to teach that that Speech is essentially the same as God the Father, although personally distinguished, he adds, "is not mine; but the Father's who sent me." And although the catholic sense could be that [the spoken word]{g:sermo-vocalis} is not the Word's of himself, in the way in which the Apostle says that the Father alone has immortality, as Augustine expounds in De Trinitate 1 and in book 1 against Maximinus, chapter 10, yet the first sense is subtler and dearer to me.[^20]
