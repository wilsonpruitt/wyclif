# *De potestate pape*, chapter 12, part 17: the king must rule his clergy first; the clergy's ornaments

## ¶1 · 377.24–378.32

> [p. 377] Et ex istis primo patet quod rex Anglie primo et principaliter daret operam ad regulandum clerum suum et specialiter episcopos ut vivant similius legi Christi; totum enim regnum est unum corpus, quod tueri atque mederi spectat ad regis officium, ut dicit iste sanctus. Ideo oportet se- cundum beatum Crysostomum Omelia XXXI Operis Imperfecti incipere a clero, cum sit pars principalis et stomachus corporis, per quem cibi digestio et sanitas sunt ad cetera membra corporis derivanda. Oportet enim regem ab illis incipere [p. 378] secundum leges ordinis naturalis. Unde quantum ad illas viginti duas condiciones regis, omnes sonant implicite quod rex debet ad regimen cleri sui magis attendere; tunc enim defendit ecclesiam, regnum, viduas et pupillos. Quod si exaltat et fovet indignos episcopos, heresiarcha et autor sui facinoris est censendus. Secundo patet quod sive Romanus pontifex sive quicumque alius ex presumcione infundabili impedierit hoc sacrum regis officium, habet racionem heretici et pessimi Antichristi. Patet ex hoc quod talis est capitalis in heresi talis regis, nam nec Petrus nec Paulus nec eciam dominus Ihesus Christus habet potestatem ad dampnificandum, sed meliorandum ecclesiam; ideo hoc temptans contra reges, qui sunt Dei vicarii, temptat sacrilege contra sanctam ecclesiam et per consequens contra Deum; eciam sic potest sinistre intelligi maledicta glossa ordinaria super XXV questione Sunt quidam, ubi dicit quod papa habet potestatem nedum dispensandi contra Apostolum sed eciam contra Christum, et tunc indubie est Christo contrarius et pessimus Antichristus. Non enim fingi potest quomodo haberet a Deo potenciam ad tollendum vel impediendum potestatem regum, qua edificaretur ecclesia; si enim non liceret regi regere suum clerum, cum ille potest totum residuum regni inficere, ymo residuum populi superare, alias non foret rex regni integri. Et sic, ut dicit Augustinus De Antichristo XX De Civitate Dei, XXIV capitulo, ipse destrueret quartum regnum.

And from these it is clear, first, that the king of England should first and chiefly give his effort to ruling his [clergy]{g:clerus}, and especially the [bishops]{g:episcopus}, so that they live more like the [law of Christ]{g:lex-christi}; for the whole [kingdom]{g:regnum} is one body, which it belongs to the king's office to protect and to heal, as this saint says.[^1] Therefore, according to blessed Chrysostom, Homily 31 of the *Unfinished Work*, he must begin with the clergy, since it is the principal part and the stomach of the body, through which the [digestion]{g:digestio} of food and health are to be passed on to the other [members]{g:membrum} of the body.[^2] For the king must begin with them [378] according to the laws of the natural order. Hence, as to those twenty-two conditions of a king, all of them imply that the king ought to attend more to the rule of his clergy; for then he defends the [church]{g:ecclesia}, the kingdom, widows and orphans. But if he exalts and fosters unworthy bishops, he is to be reckoned a heresiarch and the author of his misdeed. Second, it is clear that whether the [Roman pontiff]{g:romanus-pontifex} or anyone else should, out of presumption [without foundation]{g:infundabilis}, hinder this sacred office of the king, he has the nature of a heretic and of the worst Antichrist. This is clear from this, that such a one is chief in the heresy of such a king;[^3] for neither Peter nor Paul nor even the Lord Jesus Christ has [power]{g:potestas} to damage the church, but to improve it; therefore one who attempts this against kings, who are God's vicars, attempts sacrilegiously against holy church and consequently against God. In this way too the accursed ordinary gloss on [Causa] 25, question [1], *Sunt quidam*, can be understood in a sinister sense, where it says that the [pope]{g:papa} has power to dispense not only against the Apostle but even against Christ;[^4] and then without doubt he is contrary to Christ and the worst Antichrist. For it cannot be imagined how he would have from God [power]{g:potencia} to take away or hinder the power of kings by which the church would be built up; for if it were not lawful for the king to rule his clergy, since it can infect the whole remainder of the kingdom, indeed overpower the remainder of the people, then he would not be king of a whole kingdom. And so, as Augustine says of Antichrist in *City of God* 20, chapter 24, he would destroy the fourth kingdom.[^5]

## ¶2 · 379.1–379.23

> [p. 379] Tercio patet ex verbis sancti doctoris, quod pax regnorum est precipue per cleri maculas perturbata, non propter regis culpam in regendo subiectos; primo enim, ut dicit, pax populorum dissolvitur. Sed defectus illius regiminis a defectu regiminis cleri exoritur; ibidem enim primo pax cum Deo destruitur, et per consequens oportet destrui pacem cum homine, quia Iob IX 4 scribitur: Quis sibi restitit et pacem habuit? Patet igitur quod venenato clero, qui mediaret inter Deum et homines, necesse est pacem in populo perturbari; in cuius signum, ubi clerus insolencius et nequius conversatur, est maior tranquillitatis distancia. Et ut notat quidam intuens successus seculi in diocesi Dunelmiensi, est maior turbacio et minor episcoporum et cleri religio. Ideo gaudent quidam de iusto Dei iudicio, quod ibidem ponuntur pestilencie, fames et unius gentis in alteram insultacio. Surgant itaque et pacificent, qui facto et consensu sextuplici tam cupide et irregulariter ecclesiam Christi turbaverant. Melius enim est pugnare contra hostes extrinsecos quam contra iustos domesticos.

Third, it is clear from the words of the holy doctor that the peace of kingdoms is chiefly disturbed by the stains of the clergy, not on account of the king's fault in ruling his subjects; for first, as he says, the peace of peoples is dissolved.[^6] Now the failure of that rule arises from failure in the rule of the clergy; for there peace with God is first destroyed, and consequently peace with man must be destroyed, for it is written in Job 9:4: "Who hath resisted him, and hath had peace?"[^7] It is clear therefore that, when the clergy that should mediate between God and men is poisoned, peace among the people must be disturbed; in sign of which, where the clergy lives more insolently and more wickedly, there is the greater distance from tranquillity. And, as one notes who watches the course of the world, in the diocese of Durham there is greater disturbance, and less religion in the bishops and clergy.[^8] Therefore some rejoice at the just judgement of God, that pestilences, famine and the assault of one nation upon another are set there. Let them therefore rise up and make peace, who by deed and by sixfold consent have so greedily and irregularly troubled the church of Christ.[^9] For it is better to fight against enemies outside than against the just of one's own household.

## ¶3 · 379.24–380.29

> [p. 379] Sed certum est quod nunquam tolletur regnorum dissensio, antequam rex clerum rexerit in statu quem Christus instituit, ut patet Daniel II Omne enim tale peccatum Deus regi imputat ex consensu. Expergiscat igitur rex prudencia et non permittat maniacos per privilegia consumere bona sua et bona pauperum regni sui, nam reducere ad statum primevum ecclesiam regi principaliter spectat ad suum officium. Non autem potest fundari in lege Domini tale privilegium et specialiter emptum cum gravi consumpcione bonorum pauperum. Ideo bona illa ne sic depopulentur, [p. 380] ad regem pertinet sequestrare. In scriptura siquidem legitur de duplici tali privilegio sacerdotis et cetera. Ieremias enim ex mandato Domini appendit ad collum cathenam ligneam, ut patet Jeremie XXVII. et post fracturam illius cathene per pseudoprophetam Ananiam accepit cathenam ferream, ut patet XXVIII capitulo. Sedechias vero pseudopropheta cesareus libro III Regum XXII et II Paralipomenon XVIII fecisse sibi ornamentum ex parte dyaboli concessum ad fingendum mendacium. Fecit, inquit, sibi Sedechias filius Canaan cornua ferrea, et ait: Hec dicit Dominus Deus: Hiis ventilabis Syriam, donec deleas eam. Et revera sicut contra Micheam erant multi pseudoprophete, false ferentes signa mendacii, sic contra paucos sacerdotes Christi sunt multi cesarei longe falsius ferentes ornamenta cesarea, quibus de victoria contra dyabolum se et suos decipiunt. Cum igitur cessarunt in nobis legalia legis veteris, debemus ad moralia virtutum figurata attendere et videre quid in Christo et suis discipulis invenitur secundum legem suam induere. Nam Psal. CXXXI, 9 per prophetam dicitur: Sacerdotes eius induantur iusticiam. De ornamentis autem corporalibus non legi Christum vel suos apostolos legem particulariter tradidisse, sed sine dissensione vel scandalo nunc unum ornamentum nunc aliud leviter acceptasse, docendo nos ut faciamus similiter.

But it is certain that the dissension of kingdoms will never be taken away before the king has ruled the clergy in the state which Christ instituted, as is clear from Daniel 2.[^10] For God imputes every such sin to the king by [his] consent. Let the king therefore awaken in prudence, and not permit madmen through [privileges]{g:privilegium} to consume his goods and the goods of the poor of his kingdom; for to bring the church back to its first state belongs chiefly to the king, to his office. Now such a privilege cannot be grounded in the law of the Lord, and especially one bought with grave consumption of the goods of the poor. Therefore it pertains [380] to the king to sequester those goods, lest they be so laid waste. For indeed in scripture one reads of a twofold privilege of this kind for a priest, etc. For Jeremias at the command of the Lord hung a wooden chain about his neck, as is clear from Jeremias 27, and after that chain was broken by the false prophet Hananias he received an iron chain, as is clear in chapter 28.[^11] And Sedecias, a caesarean false prophet, [is read] in 3 Kings 22 and 2 Paralipomenon 18 to have made himself an ornament granted on the devil's part for feigning a lie:[^12] "Sedecias", it says, "the son of Chanaana made himself horns of iron, and said: Thus saith the Lord God: With these shalt thou push Syria, till thou destroy it." And truly, as against Micheas there were many false prophets, falsely bearing signs of a lie, so against the few priests of Christ there are many caesareans bearing far more falsely caesarean ornaments, by which they deceive themselves and their own about victory against the devil. Since therefore the [legal precepts]{g:legalia} of the old law have ceased in us, we ought to attend to the moral [meanings] of the virtues figured [in them], and to see what is found in Christ and his disciples to put on according to his law.[^13] For it is said by the prophet in Psalm 131:9: "Let his priests be clothed with justice."[^14] And as to bodily ornaments, I have not read that Christ or his apostles handed down a law in particular, but that without dissension or scandal they lightly accepted at one time one ornament, at another another, teaching us to do likewise.

## ¶4 · 380.30–381.2

> [p. 380] Quantum igitur ad privilegia ornamentorum debet christianus ipsa attendere quasi neutra et nullo modo preponderare illa suis signatis, scilicet virtutibus [p. 381] datis a Domino, licet tamen eis uti pro tempore, ut patet de Apostolo Act. XXI.

As to the privileges of ornaments, therefore, a Christian ought to regard them as [neutral things]{g:neutra} and in no way to weigh them above the things they signify, namely the virtues [381] given by the Lord; yet it is lawful to use them for a time, as is clear of the Apostle in Acts 21.[^15]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** "This saint" is the author of *On the Twelve Degrees of Abuses*, quoted as Augustine at the end of the preceding part; the kingdom as one body is Wyclif's summary of him, not a quotation.
[^2]: **S.** Pseudo-Chrysostom, *Opus imperfectum in Matthaeum*, hom. 31; Loserth gives no reference, to be checked (LEDGER P12q-1).
[^3]: **C.** *Talis est capitalis in heresi talis regis*: we take *talis regis* as the king just described, who exalts unworthy bishops, and *capitalis* as "chief" in that heresy. *Talis legis* ("of such a law") would read more easily, but there is no variant; we translate the print.
[^4]: **S.** Decretum C. 25 q. 1 c. 6 (*Sunt quidam*), with the ordinary gloss; Loserth cites the Venice 1595 edition, I, p. 1347, at the gloss *Papa utrum possit contra Apostolum dispensare* (the modern form is ours; LEDGER P12q-2). Loserth's side-note: "Accursed error in the Gloss."
[^5]: **S.** Augustine, *De civitate Dei* 20.24 as Wyclif cites it (Loserth: Aug. *Opp.* VII, 606; LEDGER P12q-3), on the fourth kingdom of Daniel. The subject of *destrueret* is the one who hinders the king's power, set beside Antichrist.
[^6]: **C.** *Pax populorum dissolvitur* recalls the first of the twelve evils of the unjust king (*pax sepe ipsorum rumpitur*, 376). The argument: the king's failure breaks the peace, but that failure springs from the clergy's.
[^7]: **S.** Job 9:4, DR "who hath resisted him, and hath had peace?"; Wyclif reads *sibi* for the Vulgate *ei*.
[^8]: **R.** Durham, the see on the Scottish border; Loserth compares *De ecclesia* p. 219 (LEDGER P12q-4). The observer is not named.
[^9]: **C.** *Consensu sextuplici*: the sixfold consent, the ways in which one consents to another's sin, which Wyclif sets out elsewhere (to be checked, LEDGER P12q-5). *Irregulariter* here is "against the rule", not the canonical impediment (*irregularitas*).
[^10]: **S.** Dan. 2, the statue of the four kingdoms struck by the stone; Wyclif gives the chapter only.
[^11]: **S.** Jer. 27:2 (DR "Make thee bands, and chains: and thou shalt put them on thy neck") and 28:10–13, where Hananias breaks the yoke and the Lord answers "Thou hast broken chains of wood, and thou shalt make for them chains of iron". Wyclif has one chain, in the singular.
[^12]: **C/S.** *Fecisse* has no governing verb in its own sentence; we supply *legitur* from the sentence before. The quotation is 3 Kings 22:11 (2 Par. 18:10 is nearly the same), in DR; Wyclif adds *Deus* ("the Lord God").
[^13]: **C.** *Moralia virtutum figurata*: we take *moralia* as a noun ("the moral [meanings]"), the virtues being figured by the ornaments of the old law. The manuscripts' margin: *Ornamenta clericorum*.
[^14]: **S.** Ps. 131:9, DR "Let thy priests be clothed with justice"; Wyclif reads *eius* for the Vulgate *tui* (cf. 131:16, *sacerdotes ejus*).
[^15]: **S.** Acts 21:23–26, where Paul is purified in the temple at the counsel of James. The manuscripts' margin: *Dispensacio ad clericatum*.
