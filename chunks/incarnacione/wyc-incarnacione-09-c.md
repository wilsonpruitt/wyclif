# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 9, part 3: a distinction of reason, and in what sense the Word assumed man

## ¶1 · 150.25–151.16

> [p. 150] Hic dicitur sicut⁷ in materia De Trinitate, quod multiplex est distinccio, ut alia essencialis, alia personalis, et alia racionis. Unde distinccio racionis non interimit mutuam predicacionem distinctorum; ut natura specifica hominis, licet a Petro racione distinguatur,⁸ cum ipsa sit communicabilis omni homini, Petrus autem non; et⁹ tamen Petrus est illa natura specifica, et conversim, sed non ex equo. Et natura vel essencia divina racione distinguitur a persona, cum ipsa communicatur tribus personis producentibus vel [p. 151] productis; nulla autem persona sic communicari poterit,¹ sicut nec natura divina adintra producere poterit¹ vel produci. Et tamen non obstante distinccione huius racionis omnis persona divina est natura divina et e contra. Correspondenter cum persona Verbi communicatur² humanitati et divinitati, patet quod est distinccio racionis inter personam illam et humanitatem; cum illa persona sit deitas, illa persona est eterna absolute necessaria equalis simpliciter Deo Patri. Illa quidem³ humanitas non potest esse deitas; sed est necessario temporalis, contingens, et minor simpliciter Deo Patre, licet sit personaliter illud, quod est deitas, absolute necessarium et equale simpliciter Deo Patri. Et patet illa distinccio non tollit predicacionem mutuam forme hominis et persone. Et patet quod assumptum in argumento est verum, sed negatur minor, cum nemo recte dicit oppositum.

Here [the answer is]{g:dicitur}, as in the matter of the Trinity,[^1] that distinction is manifold: one essential, another personal, and another of reason. Hence a [distinction of reason]{g:distinccio-racionis} does not destroy the mutual [predication]{g:predicacio} of the things distinguished; as the [nature of the species]{g:natura-specifica} of man, although it is distinguished from Peter by reason, since it is communicable to every man, while Peter is not, yet Peter is that nature of the species, and conversely, but not on equal terms.[^2] And the divine [nature]{g:natura} or essence is distinguished by reason from a [person]{g:persona}, since it is communicated to the three persons, producing or [151] produced; but no person could be so communicated, just as the divine nature [within]{g:adintra} could not produce or be produced.[^3] And yet, notwithstanding the distinction of this reason, every divine person is the divine nature, and the converse. Correspondingly, since the person of the [Word]{g:verbum} is communicated to [the humanity]{g:humanitas} and to the [divinity]{g:divinitas}, it is clear that there is a distinction of reason between that person and the humanity; since that person is [the Godhead]{g:deitas}, that person is eternal, absolutely necessary, equal [without qualification]{g:simpliciter} to God the Father. That humanity indeed cannot be the Godhead; but it is necessarily temporal, contingent, and less without qualification than God the Father, although it is personally that which is the Godhead, absolutely necessary and equal without qualification to God the Father. And it is clear [that] that distinction does not take away the mutual predication of the form of man and the person. And it is clear that [the assumption]{g:assumptum} in the argument is true, but the minor is denied, since no one rightly says the opposite.[^4]

## ¶2 · 151.17–152.23

> [p. 151] Unde si moderniores admisissent predicacionem secundum essenciam vel personam, licet non formalem, concedendo tamen cum Anselmo in De Incarnacione 7°. quod natura specifica est singulus⁴ hominum, sicut natura divina est singulum suorum suppositorum; et correspondenter quod persona cuiuscumque hominis est tam corpus quam anima, licet ille nature essencialiter distinguantur; et quod inter quamlibet personam hominis et illas naturas sit cum predicacione mutua distinccio racionis: tunc, inquam, saperet illis quod natura divina et natura humana sunt utraque persona Verbi, licet ille nature essencialiter distinguantur, et inter Verbum et utramque naturam sit distinccio racionis; et tunc saperet illis exemplum illud⁵ philosophicum fidei in simbolo Athanasii quod⁶ Augustinus tam crebro recitat; ‘sicut anima racionalis et caro unus est homo, ita Deus et homo unus est Christus.’ Diversio autem⁷ ab istis principiis facit nimirum variacionem maximam consequenter. [p. 152] Ulterius concedendum est de virtute sermonis¹ quod Verbum hominem assumpsit, sicut persepe dicunt Augustinus, Ieronimus, et Anselmus, et antiqui qui recte sapuerunt in logica. Cum enim homo sit concretum humanitatis, sicut Deus deitatis, patet quod potest indifferenter simpliciter supponere pro natura, vel personaliter pro persona; ut sicut Deus communicatur omnibus tribus personis divinis, quia deitas est sic communis, et tamen idem Deus incommunicatus, cum sit persona gignens; sic homo communicatur omni persone hominis,² cum sit forma specifica; et tamen homo non potest sic communicari, cum sit persona hominis; et correspondenter de aliis secundum supposicionem simplicem et personalem equivoce variatis. Unde quandocunque dicitur quod hominem Verbum assumpsit, intelligendum est simpliciter pro natura; ut humanitatem nedum specificam sed singularem assumpsit, quam Anselmus nominat Iesum Christum. Imo si diligenter attendimus quomodo homo quale-quid vel substancialem qualitatem primo significat, ut dicunt Aristoteles et Anselmus, prius significaret talis proposicio—hominem Verbum assumpsit—quod naturam humanam assumpserat⁴ quam quod personam hominis assumpserat.⁴ Ideo est talis proposicio ad sensum primarium concedenda et sensus alius est negandus.

Hence, if [the more modern]{g:moderniores} had admitted [predication by essence]{g:predicacio-secundum-essenciam} or by person, though not formal [predication], granting nevertheless with Anselm, in *De incarnatione* chapter 7, that the nature of the species is each one of men, as the divine nature is each one of its [supposits]{g:suppositum};[^5] and correspondingly that the person of any man is both the body and the soul, although those natures are essentially distinguished; and that between any person of a man and those natures there is, with mutual predication, a distinction of reason: then, I say, it would savour to them that the divine nature and the human nature are each the person of the Word, although those natures are essentially distinguished, and between the Word and each nature there is a distinction of reason; and then that philosophical example of the faith in the creed of Athanasius, which Augustine so often recites, would savour to them: "as the rational soul and flesh is one man, so God and man is one Christ."[^6] Therefore the turning aside from these principles makes, no wonder, the greatest variation in what follows.[^7] [152] Further, it is to be granted, [in the proper sense of the words]{g:de-virtute-sermonis}, that the Word assumed man, as Augustine, Jerome and Anselm very often say, and the ancients who were rightly wise in logic.[^8] For since "man" is the [concrete]{g:concretum} [term] of the humanity, as "God" of the Godhead, it is clear that it can stand indifferently, with simple supposition, for the nature, or with personal supposition for the person; so that, as God is communicated to all three divine persons, because the Godhead is so common, and yet the same God is uncommunicated, since he is the begetting person; so man is communicated to every person of a man, since it is the form of the species; and yet man cannot be so communicated, since it is the person of a man; and correspondingly of other [terms] varied [equivocally]{g:equivoce} according to simple and personal supposition. Hence whenever it is said that the Word assumed man, it is to be understood with simple supposition, for the nature; so that he [assumed]{g:assumere} the humanity [not only]{g:nedum} of the species but singular, which Anselm names Jesus Christ.[^9] Indeed, if we attend diligently to how "man" primarily signifies a what-of-a-kind or substantial quality, as Aristotle and Anselm say, such a [proposition]{g:proposicio} (the Word assumed man) would signify that he had assumed the human nature before [it would signify] that he had assumed the person of a man.[^10] Therefore such a proposition is to be granted in its primary sense, and the other sense is to be denied.

## ¶3 · 152.24–153.9

> [p. 152] Et si queritur⁵ diversitas inter illos terminos humanitas et homo quoad modum significandi cum, licet de se mutuo predicentur, refert tamen multibi⁶ ponere unum vel relicum: dicitur quod more aliorum terminorum quorum unus est concretus aliusque abstractus, concretum supponit indifferenter personaliter pro persona vel simpliciter pro natura; et hinc recipit formalem predicacionem cuiuscumque generis accidentis: abstractum autem abstractive tentum non suscipit formaliter nisi predicacionem per se et predicacionem secundum habitudinem abiciendo omnem formalem predicacionem [p. 153] suppositalem; ut humanitas non est qualis aut quanta accidenter, nec agit, nec patitur, sedet vel ditatur,¹ sed predicaciones respectivas recipit modo quo dictum est de universalibus; ut, si humanitas esset alba, cum humanitas sit esse hominem, tunc esse hominem foret formaliter esse album; et per consequens, ut arguit Aristoteles, homo tunc esset albedo. Et patet quod Verbum solum humanitatem vel naturam humanum assumpsit, non personam hominis; licet secundum alium sensum omnis humanitas sit persona.

And if the difference is asked between those terms "humanity" and "man" as to the mode of signifying, since, although they are predicated of each other, yet it matters in many places to put the one or the other: the answer is that, in the manner of other terms of which one is concrete and the other abstract, the concrete stands indifferently, with personal supposition for the person or with simple supposition for the nature; and hence it receives formal predication of any kind of accident: but the abstract, taken [abstractively]{g:abstractive}, does not formally take up any predication but predication [of itself]{g:per-se} and predication by [respect]{g:habitudo},[^11] rejecting all formal [153] suppositional predication; so that the humanity is not qualified or [quantified]{g:quantus} by accident, nor acts, nor is acted on, sits or is enriched,[^12] but it receives [relational]{g:respectivus} predications in the way that was said of [universals]{g:universale}; so that, if the humanity were white, since the humanity is to be a man, then to be a man would be formally to be white; and consequently, as Aristotle argues, man would then be whiteness.[^13] And it is clear that the Word assumed only the humanity or human nature, not the person of a man; although in another sense every humanity is a person.

## ¶4 · 153.10–154.11

> [p. 153] Verumptamen notandum est quod in materia de incarnacione doctores sancti utuntur isto termino ‘humanitas’ tanquam medio inter abstractum et concretum: ut Augustinus Ome. 19. super Iohannem dicit, quod ‘Maria humanitatem genuit que eciam in cruce passa est’; et Enchiridion 29°; ‘illam, inquit, creaturam quam virgo concepit et peperit, quamvis ad solam personam Filii pertinentem, tota² Trinitas fecit.’ Inseparabilia enim³ sunt opera Trinitatis, et indubie per ‘illam creaturam’ intelligit substanciam humanam, que est humanitas, cum paulo ante dicit utramque substanciam, divinam scilicet⁴ et humanam, esse Filium unicum Dei Patris. Et sic de multis similibus, que omnia cum eis similibus intelligenda sunt, quod persone Verbi Dei insunt huiusmodi predicata⁵ secundum quod⁶ homo; hoc est,⁶ humanitas est medium vel causa, secundum quam predicata huiusmodi insunt; ut non haberet unde temptaretur, gigneretur, vel meritorie pateretur, et⁷ sic de aliis communicacionibus⁷ ydiomatum, nisi assumendo naturam hominis. Nec mirum de ista transsumpcione dispari in illa materia, cum⁸ [solum] Verbum Dei sit homo per accidens prius persona quam homo⁸; cum solum illa personalitas trahit⁹ sibi naturam singularem in suam ypostasim; alie autem persone similis¹⁰ nature sunt cum aliis¹⁰ formis singularibus. [p. 154] Ideo¹ sic cum dico hoc—‘passus¹ est in cruce’—multi fideles sunt indifferentes ad intelligendum² illam passionem secundum deitatem vel humanitatem: multi autem proniores ad intelligendum hoc secundum illam naturam secundum quam habet subsistenciam et personam. Ideo racionabiliter ordinarunt sancti specialiter³ in illa materia ad destruccionem ambiguitatis huiusmodi et certificacionem sentencie pertinentis, quod abstractum⁴ limitaret ad sensum expositum. Quandocunque ergo dicitur quod humanitas Christi suscepit⁵ predicaciones huiusmodi, intelligendum est quod Christus secundum illam sic suscipit.

Nevertheless it is to be noted that in the matter of the incarnation the holy doctors use this term "humanity" as a mean between abstract and concrete: as Augustine says in homily 19 on John, that "Mary bore the humanity, which also suffered on the cross"; and *Enchiridion* 29: "that [creature]{g:creatura}," he says, "which the virgin conceived and bore, although belonging to the person of the Son alone, the whole Trinity made." For the works of the Trinity are inseparable, and undoubtedly by "that creature" he understands the human substance, which is the humanity, since a little before he says that each substance, namely the divine and the human, is the only Son of God the Father.[^14] And so of many like [sayings], all of which, with those like them, are to be understood [to mean] that predicates of this kind are in the person of the Word of God [insofar as]{g:secundum-quod} [he is] man; that is, the humanity is the means or cause according to which predicates of this kind are in [him]; so that he would not have whence to be tempted, to be begotten, or meritoriously to suffer, and so of the other [communications of idioms]{g:communicacio-ydiomatum}, except by assuming the nature of man. Nor is this uneven [transumption]{g:transsumpcio} in that matter a wonder, since only the Word of God is man by accident, being person before man;[^15] since only that personality draws to itself a singular nature into its [hypostasis]{g:ypostasis}; but the other persons of like nature are [persons] with other singular forms. [154] Therefore thus, when I say this, "he suffered on the cross", many of the faithful are indifferent to understanding that passion [as to the Godhead]{g:secundum-deitatem} or as to the humanity; but many are more inclined to understand it as to that nature according to which he has [subsistence]{g:subsistencia} and person.[^16] Therefore [the saints]{g:sancti} reasonably ordained, especially in that matter, for the destruction of ambiguity of this kind and the making certain of the pertinent [teaching]{g:sentencia}, that the abstract should limit to the sense expounded. Whenever, therefore, it is said that the humanity of Christ took up predications of this kind, it is to be understood that Christ as to it so takes them up.[^17]

## ¶5 · 154.12–154.21

> [p. 154] 3° principaliter arguitur deducendo⁶ multiplicia inconveniencia⁶ que secuntur. Nam primo videtur quod nedum idem est assumens et eciam assumptum, sed quod assumens est assumptum, creans est creatum, causa suum causatum; ymo si Dominus Iesus Christus sit assumptus, tunc et Deus assumitur, sicut humanitas⁷ humiliatur:⁷ et per consequens secundum duplicem naturam fuit duplex Christi assumpcio: ymo si nature tam distantes ydemptificari potuerunt, multo evidencius ypostases, que in natura magis conveniunt, tanta ydemptitate uniri potuerunt.⁸

Third, [as a main head]{g:principaliter}, it is argued by deducing many [absurdities]{g:inconveniens} that follow.[^18] For first it seems that not only is the same thing assuming and also assumed, but that the assuming is the assumed, the creating is the [created]{g:creatus}, the cause its caused; indeed, if the Lord Jesus Christ is assumed, then God too is assumed, as the humanity is [humbled]{g:humiliacio}:[^19] and consequently, according to the twofold nature, there was a twofold [assumption]{g:assumpcio} of Christ: indeed, if natures so distant could be [identified]{g:ydemptificare}, much more evidently could hypostases, which agree more in nature, be united by so great an identity.

[^3]: **T.** O omits *sicut … poterit* (151.1–2), by eye-skip from *poterit* to *poterit*; O reads *communicati et* for *communicatur* (151.5) and *quieta* for *quidem* (151.9).

[^4]: **C.** *Assumptum* is the logician's premiss, not the assumption of a nature: the objection's premiss (the Word assumed the humanity, not a person, 150.19–20) is true; the minor that would make it infer a distinction excluding mutual predication is denied.

[^5]: **S/T.** Anselm, *Epistola de incarnatione Verbi* (LEDGER I09c-2, to be checked). *Singulus hominum* "each one of men" (151.20) is the print; A B C read an abbreviated *sing…s*, O *singulis*. *Predicacio secundum personam* is set beside the ruled *predicacio secundum essenciam*.

[^6]: **S/C.** The *Quicumque* (Athanasian Creed), v. 37 (LEDGER I09c-3, to be checked, with Augustine's use of the analogy). *Saperet illis* "it would savour to them", i.e. they would grasp it; Wyclif plays on *sapuerunt in logica* (152.3). A B C omit *illud* (151.29); O reads *et* for *quod* (151.30).

[^7]: **T.** "Therefore" with C (*ergo*) and O (*igitur*), against the print's *autem* (151.32, with A B): agreement across the families. As printed: "And the turning aside …".

[^8]: **S/T.** That the Word assumed man (*hominem*) in Augustine, Jerome and Anselm (LEDGER I09c-4, to be checked). A's text was erased and corrected in the margin to *de vi vocis* "by the force of the words" (152.1).

[^9]: **C/S.** *Simpliciter* here is the supposition term (simple supposition, the term standing for the common nature), set against *personaliter* (personal supposition), not "without qualification". Anselm's naming of the singular humanity "Jesus Christ" (LEDGER I09c-5, to be checked).

[^10]: **S/T.** "Man" signifies *quale quid*, a substance of a kind: Aristotle, *Categories* 5, 3b13–21, and Anselm, *De grammatico* (LEDGER I09c-6, to be checked). *Prius … quam* "before … rather than". O omits *quam … assumpserat* (152.21).

[^11]: **C.** *Habitudo* here in its "respect" sense: predication under a relation, as of a universal. *Per se*: predication of the essence itself. O reads *queretur* (152.24); A B C read *multicubi* for *multibi* "in many places" (152.26), the same word.

[^12]: **T/C.** The categories in turn: quality, quantity, action, being-acted-on, position (*sedet*), having (*ditatur* "is enriched"). C O read *dicatur* "is said" for *ditatur* (153.2); it fits no category in the list, and the print is kept.

[^13]: **S/T.** Aristotle's argument that what is said of the abstract would make man whiteness (LEDGER I09c-7, to be checked). *Naturam humanum* (153.8) is the print's form for *humanam*.

[^14]: **S/T.** Augustine, *Tractatus in Iohannem* 19 (LEDGER I09c-8, to be checked); *Enchiridion* 12.38, with 10.35 for *paulo ante* (LEDGER I09c-9, to be checked). O reads *totum* for *tota* (153.16), *non* for *enim* (153.17), omits *scilicet* (153.20).

[^15]: **T.** "Only", with C O, which read *solum* (153.29); Harris brackets it as corrupted from the next line's *cum solum* and would omit it, an emendation against every manuscript that has the clause (A B omit *cum … homo*, 153.29–30).
