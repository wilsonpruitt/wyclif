# *De eucharistia*, Questio ad fratres: what the white round thing is in its nature

## Headnote

The *Questio ad fratres de sacramento altaris* is a short question put to the friars on the sacrament of the altar, printed by Loserth at the end of the volume (pp. 347–348). It asks what the consecrated host is in its nature, answers in a sentence with the teaching of the *Tractatus de eucharistia* (the host is true bread in its nature and, by the power of the sacramental words, the body of Christ), and then restates the question as one that "the same John" has often put to the friars, with the friars' answers at Oxford named against it. Loserth prints only this first part: his note at 348.5 says that what follows in the manuscript does not belong to the question on the sacrament of the altar, and he gives its opening words (*Personarum accepcio est preponderativa unius persone in amore supra aliam …*). Loserth (Introduction, p. LXV) identifies that continuation as Hus's work, not Wyclif's: the *Tractatus de tribus dubiis factis in Holomucz* of 1412 (incipit *Quia dubitantibus sanum est consulere*). He takes the text from the Prague University manuscript III. G. 11, the codex Shirley had noted (*Catalogue* no. 22), where only the first chapter, "28 lines in all", is on the sacrament; the few notes on it are, he judges, a Bohemian Wycliffite's. He prints no variants for these pages. The reference to the friars' teaching at Oxford places the question in the controversy of Wyclif's last Oxford years, c. 1379–81 [verify]. Scripture chapters are Wyclif's, verse numbers Loserth's.

## ¶1 · 347.3–347.19

> [p. 347] Quid in natura sua est hoc album et rotundum quod consecratur a sacerdote et quod videmus oculo corporali in manibus sacerdotis post consecracionem, quod frangitur, quod teritur dentibus, et numquid est corpus Christi, et numquid est sacramentum corporis Christi. Post publicacionem fidei scripture ex testimonio veritatis et sui Apostoli dicitur quod sacramentum eucaristie est verus panis in natura sua et cum hoc idem panis est virtute verborum sacramentalium corpus Christi, sicut est simul Deus et homo. Et solucio arguciarum in hac parte est satis facilis. Nec sunt obiectus trutannici oppositi digni memoria. Alii autem infideles taciti dicunt quod illa hostia consecrata est signum sensibile. Sed tacent infideliter utrum sit corpus Christi vel in natura sua panis, ut dicit Apostolus et sancti concorditer quousque diabolus est solutus.

What, in its [nature]{g:natura}, is this white and round thing which is consecrated by the priest, and which we see with the bodily eye in the priest's hands after the [consecration]{g:consecracio}, which is broken, which is ground by the teeth; and is it [the body of Christ]{g:corpus-christi}, and is it the [sacrament]{g:sacramentum} of the body of Christ?[^1] After the publishing of the faith of scripture from the testimony of the Truth and of his Apostle, [the answer is]{g:dicitur} that the sacrament of the eucharist is true [bread]{g:panis} in its nature and, with this, the same bread is, by the [power]{g:virtus} of the sacramental words, the body of Christ, just as he is at once God and man.[^2] And the [resolution]{g:solucio} of the [quibbles]{g:argucie} in this matter is easy enough. Nor are the vagabond [objections]{g:obiectus} set against it worthy of remembrance.[^3] And other [unbelievers]{g:infidelis}, keeping silent, say "that consecrated [host]{g:hostia} is a [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} [sign]{g:signum}." But they keep silent, faithlessly, whether it is the body of Christ or in its nature bread, as the Apostle and [the saints]{g:sancti} say with one accord until the devil was loosed.[^4]

## ¶2 · 347.20–348.4

> [p. 347] Questio eiusdem: Sepe quesivit idem Johannes et adhuc queritur a fratribus utrum ista sensibilis hostia consecrata sit in natura sua panis vel unum peius quam panis equinus vel venenum, sicut fratres quidam dixerunt Oxonie, et utrum sit corpus Christi vel unums ibi accidens ignoratum, quia idem Johannes est certus ex fide quod ille panis est realiter corpus [p. 348] Domini Jesu Christi cum Christus qui mentiri non potest dicit: Hoc est corpus meum, non hic est corpus meum, cum scimus ex fide quod ubique est increata trinitas que est melior corpore Salvatoris etc.

The question of the same [man]: The same John has often asked, and it is still asked of the [friars]{g:frater}, whether that perceptible consecrated host is in its nature bread, or a thing worse than horse-bread or poison, as certain friars said at Oxford, and whether it is the body of Christ or some unknown [accident]{g:accidens} there;[^5] for the same John is certain, [as faith teaches]{g:ex-fide}, that that bread is [really]{g:realiter} [the body]{g:corpus-domini} [348] of the Lord Jesus Christ, since Christ, who cannot lie, says, "This is my body", not "here is my body", since we know, as faith teaches, that the uncreated Trinity, which is better than the body of the Saviour, is everywhere, etc.[^6]

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 347.3: What is the white round thing that is consecrated and that we see after consecration?
- 347.6: Is it Christ's body or the sacrament of His body.
- 347.19: This question John (Wyclif) has often asked, and still asks of the friars.
- 348.1: Is it bread and Christ's body or a mere accident?

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Hoc album et rotundum* is neuter, "this white and round thing": the question names the host by its perceptible marks, before saying what it is. The two questions (*numquid … numquid*) are kept apart: whether it is the body of Christ, and whether it is the sacrament of the body.
[^2]: **C/S.** "The Truth" (*veritas*) is Christ (John 14:6), and "his Apostle" Paul, who calls it bread (1 Cor. 10:16, "the bread which we break"). The subject of *sicut est simul Deus et homo* is unexpressed: we take it as Christ, the comparison being with the two natures in one person; it could also be read "as [the bread] is", which would make the bread "God and man", and is unlikely.
[^3]: **C.** *Trutannici*, from *trutannus*, a vagrant or sturdy beggar: the friars' objections are called "vagabond", a jibe at the mendicants. *Oppositi* agrees with *obiectus*, "set against [it]".
[^4]: **C/S.** The proposition the "unbelievers" say is given as theirs, not asserted. "Until the devil was loosed" alludes to Apoc. 20:7 ("Satan shall be loosed out of his prison"); the Latin names no date for it.
[^5]: **C/R.** The friars' saying at Oxford (the host "worse than horse-bread or poison") is reported as theirs, not identified (LEDGER Kquest-1). *Unums* is a misprint in Loserth's text (so on the plate); we read *unum*, "some".
[^6]: **C/S.** Matt. 26:26, "This is my body." *Hoc* is the neuter pronoun of the words of institution; *hic* is taken as the adverb "here": the argument is that a mere presence "here" says nothing peculiar to the sacrament, since the uncreated Trinity is everywhere. (*Hic* could also be the masculine pronoun, "this [one]"; the clause about the Trinity's being everywhere fixes the adverb.)
