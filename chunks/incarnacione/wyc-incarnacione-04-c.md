# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 4, part 3: the body and the soul as Christ's parts in the three days

## ¶1 · 57.7–58.12

> [p. 57] Item pari³ evidencia, qua Christus habet corpus mortuum tanquam partem suam pro illo triduo, habet quamlibet partem illius corporis partem suam, ut latus, caput,⁴ et pedes,⁴ sicut scriptura sacra patenter asserit. Sed concesso consequente, cum non possunt poni nisi partes quantitive Christi, cum sint partes quantitative corporis, sequitur quod sunt partes quantitative Christi. Et cum omnis pars quantitativa rei componit ut sic quantitative⁵ omne totum cuius est pars, sequitur quod dicte partes quantitative componunt Christum quantitative,⁶ et per consequens sic componunt,⁷ ut sit maius⁸ sua parte quantitativa. Sequitur quod Christus sit⁹ pro triduo mole magnus, ut puta septipedalis, in tumulo, cum ibi habeat partes extra partes: et cum nichil sit quantum nisi substancia materialis, sequitur quod Christus fuit pro dicto triduo substancia corporea; et per hoc est verisimile¹⁰ Doctorem debere concedere, cum dicit quod Christus potest denominari pro illo triduo homo mortuus a tota natura, cui discrete copulatur. Et abreviator suus Cowtonus¹¹ dicit quod fuit pro illo triduo homo mortuus¹² sed¹³ non homo. Sed videtur michi quod si quis est homo mortuus,¹² tunc est animal et corpus mortuum¹⁴ et sic corpus, quamvis enim mortuum¹⁴ per se circumlocutum cum verbo equivaleat verbo ampliativo. Ita¹⁵ quod non sequitur—ista bestia nunc est mortua; ergo illa nunc est—Tamen si ista bestia nunc sit¹⁶ bestia mortua, ista nunc est bestia, que moritur [p. 58] aut¹ moriebatur. Si ergo res sit mortua secundum partem quantitativam ut² manum aridam³ vivente residuo, quanto magis erit mortua per totum, si quelibet pars quantitativa sui sit mortua? Patet ergo quod si Christus unquam⁴ fuit mortuus,⁵ hoc fuit in triduo, et indubie tunc fuit persona mortua: et cum oportet dare⁶ naturam unitam, secundum quam passus est mortem, que natura subiectet mortem; patet quod tunc fuit corpus sibi unitum sicut subtiliter⁷ dicit Doctor. Non enim fuisset mortuus, si pro primo instanti non-actuacionis corporis post vitam Christi tam corpus quam anima adnichilata⁸ fuerunt; quia tunc omnis mors esset adnichilacio.⁸

Again, by the equal [evidence]{g:evidencia} by which Christ has the dead body as his part for those [three days]{g:triduum}, he has any part of that body as his part, as the side, the head, and the feet, as holy scripture plainly asserts.[^1] Now, the consequent granted, since they cannot be posited as anything but quantitative parts of Christ, since they are [quantitative parts]{g:pars-quantitativa} of the body, [it follows]{g:sequitur} that they are quantitative parts of Christ. And since every quantitative part of a thing, as such, composes quantitatively every whole of which it is a part, it follows that the said quantitative parts compose Christ quantitatively, and in consequence so compose [him] that he is greater than his quantitative part. It follows that Christ is for the three days great in bulk, say seven feet long, in the tomb, since there he has parts outside parts: and since nothing is [quantified]{g:quantus} but a material substance, it follows that Christ was for the said three days a corporeal substance; and by this it is likely that the Doctor must grant [it], since he says that Christ can be named for those three days "a dead man" from the whole [nature]{g:natura} to which he is discretely joined. And his abbreviator Cowton says that he was for those three days a dead man but not a man.[^2] But it seems to me that if anyone is a dead man, then he is an animal and a dead body, and so a body; for although "dead", circumlocuted by itself with a verb, is equivalent to an [ampliative]{g:ampliativus} verb, so that it does not follow, "this beast is now dead; therefore it now is", yet if this beast now is a dead beast, this now is a beast which is dying [58] or was dying.[^3] If, therefore, a thing is dead as to a quantitative part or a withered hand, the rest living, how much more will it be dead throughout, if every quantitative part of it is dead?[^4] It is clear, therefore, that if Christ was ever dead, this was in the three days, and without doubt he was then a dead [person]{g:persona}: and since one must posit a united nature as to which he suffered death, a nature that is the subject of death, it is clear that the body was then united to him, as the Doctor subtly says. For he would not have been dead if, for the first instant of the body's non-actuation after Christ's life, both body and soul had been annihilated; for then every death would be annihilation.[^5]

## ¶2 · 58.13–59.20

> [p. 58] Item omnis anima, in quantum huiusmodi,⁹ animat, ut patet 2° De Anima. Si ergo ille spiritus creatus fuit pro illo triduo anima Christi, tunc animavit; et, cum non animavit tunc corpus, relinquitur¹⁰ quod tunc animavit Christum¹¹; et per consequens ipse fuit tunc animatus ad sensum expositum. Et cum¹² non secundum naturam divinam pocius quam¹³ in mundi principio: ergo servata est unio nature create precedens animam, que non potest fingi nisi humanitas. Non enim sortiretur¹⁴ animacionem cum illo creato spiritu, nisi ypostatice uniretur; sicut habet denominacionem humanitatis propter ypostaticam unionem. Confirmatur. Si Christus uniretur ypostatice primo illi spiritui separato tunc esset aliquid quod prius non fuerat, cum fieret alicuius speciei in genere substancie, cum aliter non esset unio personalis. sed modo unitus est eadem unione qua tunc uniebatur¹⁵: ergo manet Christus illud pro triduo. Minor patet ex hoc, quod alterius racionis fuit unio, qua¹⁶ Christus uniebatur spiritui suo pro triduo, quam¹⁷ unio non ypostatica¹⁸ solum per graciam: eo quod aliter non denominaretur Christus tunc habere animam, descendere ad¹⁹ infernum, [p. 59] et ministrare Patri ut sibi subditus, pocius quam Spiritus Sanctus dicitur minor Patre secundum columbam; quod est contra Augustinum primo Contra Simplicianum. Sicut ergo Christus fuisset tunc spiritus create speciei, ita est modo propter consimilem unionem: et cum ille spiritus non esse¹ potest nisi humane speciei, quia tunc posset transire de specie ad speciem, et sic species solum per accidens sibi inexistens foret sibi non species: nam² corrupto omni corpore et servatis spiritibus illis, qui quondam erant anime, forent ipsi individua racionalis nature; quia per se denominati agere acciones personales; et per consequens individua speciei non alterius quam humane, ut olim fuerant: ergo relinquitur quod forent homines: haberent enim unam³ naturam communem, in qua convenirent pari evidencia qua⁴ alia supposita speciei, et talis communis natura, cum sit prima quiditas⁵ illorum individuorum, foret illis species specialissima. Et illud facit michi evidenciam quod humanitas servatur in anima; cum individuum non potest exuere formam specificam, et olim fuit in specie humana non solum indirecte,⁶ sed habuit propriam quiditatem.

Again, every soul, [insofar as]{g:in-quantum} it is such, [animates]{g:animare}, as is clear from *De anima* II.[^6] If, therefore, that created spirit was for those three days the soul of Christ, then it animated; and, since it did not then animate the body, it remains that it then animated Christ; and in consequence he was then animated in the sense set out. And since [he was] not [animated] as to the divine nature, any more than in the beginning of the world, therefore the union of a [created]{g:creatus} nature preceding the soul was preserved, which cannot be feigned to be anything but [the humanity]{g:humanitas}.[^7] For he would not obtain animation with that created spirit unless he were [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} united [to it]; as he has the naming of humanity because of the hypostatic [union]{g:unio}. This is confirmed. If Christ were hypostatically united first to that separated spirit, then he would be something that he had not been before, since he would become [a thing] of some species in the genus of substance, since otherwise it would not be a personal union. Now he is at present united by the same union by which he was then united: therefore Christ remains that [thing] for the three days.[^8] The minor is clear from this, that the union by which Christ was united to his spirit for the three days was of another kind than a non-hypostatic union by grace alone: because otherwise Christ would not be named as then having a soul, descending into [59] hell, and ministering to the Father as subject to him, any more than the Holy Spirit is said to be less than the Father as to the dove; which is against Augustine, in the first [book] *Against Simplicianus*.[^9] As, therefore, Christ would then have been a spirit of a created species, so he is now, because of a like union: and since that spirit cannot be of any but the human species (for then it could pass from species to species, and so a species existing in it only by accident would be no species for it: for, every body being corrupted and those spirits which were once souls being preserved, they would be [individuals]{g:individuum} of a rational nature, because they are named by themselves as performing personal actions; and in consequence individuals of no species other than the human, as they had once been: therefore it remains that they would be men: for they would have one common nature, in which they would agree by equal evidence as other [supposits]{g:suppositum} of a species, and such a common nature, since it is the first [quiddity]{g:quiditas} of those individuals, would be for them the [most special species]{g:species-specialissima}).[^10] And this gives me evidence that the humanity is preserved in the soul: since an individual cannot put off its specific form, and it was once in the human species not only indirectly, but had its own quiddity.

## ¶3 · 59.21–59.30

> [p. 59] Propter tales evidencias declinant moderniores loquentes⁷ superficialiter more⁷ legis ab istis principiis dicentes quod Christus non habuit in dicto triduo corpus vel animam; et sic nec fuit mortuus nec mortem passus, cum nulla fuit mors Christi; nec iacuit sepultus, nec descendit ad inferos; et per consequens, cum tunc non fuit unio ypostatica Christi ad naturam creatam, Christus non liberavit in persona propria patres de limbo, sicut nec Pater; cum tunc fuit secundum⁸ omnem naturam⁸ tunc habitam equalis⁹ Patri et non minister.

Because of such evidences [the more modern speakers]{g:moderniores-loquentes}, speaking superficially after the manner of the law, turn aside from these principles, saying that Christ did not have in the said three days a body or a soul; and so he was neither dead nor suffered death, since there was no death of Christ; nor did he lie buried, nor descend into hell; and in consequence, since there was then no hypostatic union of Christ to a created nature, Christ did not free the fathers from [limbo]{g:limbus} in his own person, any more than the Father did; since he was then, as to every nature then had, equal to the Father and not a minister.[^11]

## ¶4 · 59.31–60.21

> [p. 59] Nec intelligende sunt dicte¹⁰ locuciones¹⁰ synecdochice,¹¹ cum Christus tunc non habuit partem: et non est synecdochica¹² [p. 60] locucio tocius per partem nisi pro mensura pro qua¹ est pars propter inconsequencia,² que secuntur. Sed hos deliros a scriptura a fide et a sanctis doctoribus vocat Magister Sentenciarum Distinccione 21' hostes veritatis.' Nec inveni in scriptis³ alicuius doctoris probabilis istam⁴ sentenciam; sed fingunt eam recentissimi correctores. Unde doctor Bonaventura⁵ putat super Distinccione 21. 3ⁱⁱ Sentenciarum quod sit de substancia fidei credere quod tam corpus quam anima fuerunt in triduo partes Christi; et allegat⁶ ad hoc tam Augustinum quam Iohannem Damascenum dicentes⁷:' Anathema sit, qui dicit Verbum deposuisse quod semel assumpsit.' Unde consequenter declarando quod triplex⁸ est unio, scilicet accio agentis, passio uniti vel unitorum, et 3°. unitorum relacio, dicit quod eadem⁹ unione activa continue unitum est⁹ Verbum carni et anime sicut eadem tencione tenemus equum et frenum. Unde et sanctus Thomas super Dᵉ. 21 dicit quod deitas fuit coniuncta in dicto triduo tam corpori quam anime; non quod Verbum fuit tunc corpus vel anima, sed quod tunc fuit corpus Christi [corpus]¹⁰ et anima eius anima, et solum¹¹ talis fuit unio ypostatica post et ante.

Nor are the said locutions to be understood synecdochically, since Christ then had no part: and there is no [synecdochic locution]{g:synecdochica-locucio} of the whole through the part [60] except for the measure [of time] for which it is a part, because of the inconsistencies that follow.[^12] But these men, raving away from scripture, from faith, and from the holy doctors, [the Master]{g:magister} of the *Sentences*, Distinction 21, calls "enemies of the truth."[^13] Nor have I found this [teaching]{g:sentencia} in the writings of any [reputable doctor]{g:probabiles-doctores}; but the most recent correctors feign it.[^14] Hence the doctor Bonaventure holds, on Distinction 21 of the third [book] of the *Sentences*, that it is of the substance of faith to believe that both the body and the soul were in the three days parts of Christ; and he [alleges]{g:allegare} for this both Augustine and John Damascene, who say: "Anathema be he who says that [the Word]{g:verbum} laid down what he once assumed."[^15] Hence, consequently [making clear]{g:declarare} that union is threefold, namely the action of the agent, the [being-acted-on]{g:passio} of the united [thing] or [things], and third the relation of the united things, he says that by the same active union the Word is continuously united to the flesh and the soul, as by the same pull we hold the horse and the bridle.[^16] Hence also Saint Thomas, on Distinction 21, says that [the Godhead]{g:deitas} was conjoined in the said three days both to the body and to the soul; not that the Word was then body or soul, but that the body was then Christ's, and his soul [his] soul; and such alone was the hypostatic union after and before.[^17]

## ¶5 · 60.22–60.30

> [p. 60] Arguitur¹² autem de racione supposita fide catholica ad probandum propositum. Nam, supposito quod essemus in triduo, patet quod corpus Christi iacet in sepulcro et anima descendit ad inferos: nam hoc iacet in sepulcro et hoc est corpus Christi post et ante hoc triduum; ut patet ampliantibus presens tempus: ergo corpus Christi iacet in sepulcro. Et idem est argumentum de anima. Ex quo videtur quod¹³ dicte partes sunt partes Verbi in triduo, quia¹⁴ Verbum habet in triduo illas partes.

And it is argued from reason, the catholic faith being supposed, to prove what is proposed. For, supposing that we were in the three days, it is clear that the body of Christ lies in the sepulchre and the soul descends into hell: for this lies in the sepulchre, and this is the body of Christ after and before this three days, as is clear to those who [ampliate]{g:ampliacio} the present tense: therefore the body of Christ lies in the sepulchre. And the same is the argument for the soul. From which it seems that the said parts are parts of the Word in the three days, because the Word has those parts in the three days.[^18]

## ¶6 · 60.31–61.20

> [p. 60] Sed quia non omnes conveniunt in illo principio, ideo [p. 61] arguitur 3° sic. Eo ipso quod persona manet¹ per tempus et² habet per idem tempus omnes³ subiectas partes³ sibi proprie servatas, que erunt postmodum⁴ partes⁵ sue, habet pro illo tempore partes illas: sed sic fuit de corpore et anima Christi pro dicto triduo: ergo tunc fuerunt⁶ partes sue. Maior patet eo quod aliter manus arida vel membrum mortuum non esset pars persone: quod est contra scripturam et communem modum loquendi. Imo, ut patet in De Anima, partes extreme⁷ longe cicius moriuntur ipso corde: et sic in⁸ media vita est dare aliquas partes mortuas mixtas cum vivis.⁹ Ad cuius conformitatem locuntur Morales, quod¹⁰ membrum corporis Christi mistici¹¹ per privacionem gracie mortuum, non exinde desinit esse membrum, cum tunc omnis¹² expers gracie foret ut sic¹³ infidelis. Et confirmacio illius est, quod tunc continuacio non est de essencia substancie materialis. Ex quo videtur quod manus abscisa ab homine supervivente remanet quodammodo manus sua. Et per idem, cum persona Filii Dei mansit per totum triduum habens a proprietate¹⁴ et specialiter idem corpus aliter quam alia, videtur quod¹⁵ tunc fuerat corpus suum.

But because not all agree on that principle, therefore [61] it is argued, third, thus. By the very fact that a person remains through a time and has through the same time all the subject parts properly preserved for him that will afterwards be his parts, he has those parts for that time: now so it was with the body and soul of Christ for the said three days: therefore they were then his parts.[^19] The major is clear from this, that otherwise a withered hand or a dead member would not be a part of the person: which is against scripture and the common manner of speaking. Indeed, as is clear in *De anima*, the extreme parts die far sooner than the heart itself: and so in the middle of life [one may posit]{g:est-dare} some dead parts mixed with living ones.[^20] In conformity with this the moralists say that a [member]{g:membrum} of [the mystical body of Christ]{g:corpus-christi-misticum}, dead by privation of grace, does not thereby cease to be a member, since then everyone without grace would as such be an [unbeliever]{g:infidelis}.[^21] And the confirmation of this is that then [continuity]{g:continuacio} is not of the essence of material substance. From which it seems that a hand cut off from a man who survives remains in a certain way his hand. And by the same token, since the person of the Son of God remained through the whole three days having, by ownership and specially, the same body otherwise than the others, it seems that it was then his body.[^22]

[^2]: **S.** *Cowtonus* (so O; A *colton*, B *carton*, C *cantori*): perhaps Robert Cowton OFM, *Sentences* commentator, whom Wyclif calls the Doctor's (Scotus's) abbreviator; the identification is not Harris's: he found *no certain trace* of him and guessed John Colton, archbishop of Armagh. Cowton's Book III is unedited, and the place is untraced (LEDGER I04c-1). O omits 57.25–26 *sed ... mortuus* (eye-skip on *mortuus*) and 57.27–28 *et ... mortuum*; at 57.16 A B C omit the second *quantitative*, O reads *componit* without *ut*, B *magis* (O *mag°*) for *maius*.
[^3]: **C.** 57.27–58.1: "dead", put by itself with the verb (*est mortuus*), works as an ampliative verb, extending the subject to what was; so "this beast is dead" does not yield "this beast is", but "this beast is a dead beast" makes it a beast that is dying or was dying. 57.29 *Ita*: O *ampleatiō ï*. 58.1 *aut*, the print (O): A B C *ac*, "and"; the print is rendered.
[^4]: **T.** 58.2 *aut manum aridam*, with A B C O (*aut codd. omn.*, Harris's foot), against the print's *ut*, Harris's emendation: "as to a quantitative part or a withered hand". The manuscripts' reading construes. O *aridum*.

[^7]: **C.** 58.18–19 *Et cum non secundum naturam divinam pocius quam in mundi principio*: elliptical; "[he was] not [animated]" is supplied: as to the divine nature he is no more animated now than at the world's beginning. O C omit *cum*; A C *quoniam* for *quam*.
[^8]: **T.** 58.28 *uniebatur*, the print (O): A B C *uniretur*, "by which he would then be united", keeping the hypothetical. The print, setting the present union beside that of the three days as the minor's proof does (58.30), is rendered. *Modo* "at present" is kept apart from the "now" of the minor premiss.
[^9]: **S.** Wyclif's *Contra Simplicianum* I: the *Ad Simplicianum* never mentions the dove. The place is Augustine, *Contra Maximinum* I.19, PL 42, 756: the Spirit took no creature *ad unitatem personae suae*, and though shown *per columbae speciem*, *nunquam dictus est minor Patre* (from *Collatio cum Maximino* 14, PL 42, 723). Wyclif's title is perhaps a slip for *Contra Maximinum* (*verified*, LEDGER I04c-4). 58.32 A B C *in infernum*.
[^10]: **C.** 59.5–17: the *cum* clause (*et cum ille spiritus non esse potest nisi humane speciei*) is proved by the *quia* and *nam* clauses, set off here in parentheses; the period has no main clause of its own, and its conclusion is drawn in the next sentence (*Et illud facit michi evidenciam*). 59.16 *quiditas*: B *deitas*, C *quantitas*.
[^11]: **C.** 59.22 *superficialiter more legis*: "after the manner of the law", reading words by their surface as the lawyers do; O *superficienter nostre legis*, "superficially [in the matter] of our law". 59.28–29 *secundum omnem naturam*: O *seme̅ m̃n̄*, which Harris queries as *semen et naturam*; C *essencialis* for *equalis*.

[^13]: **S.** The Lombard, *Sent.* III d. 21 (Wyclif's reference), "enemies of the truth": d. 21 c. 2, PL 192, 801, *Sed quis, nisi hostis veritatis, dicat animam a Verbo depositam?* (*verified*, LEDGER I04c-5). [^14]: **C.** *Sentencia* is the CONTEXTUAL row: "teaching", the position held. 60.5 A B C *illam scienciam*, "that knowledge"; O *scripturis* for *scriptis*. *Correctores*: those who "correct" the received teaching.
[^15]: **S.** Bonaventure, *In III Sent.* d. 21 a. 1 q. 1 (Quaracchi III, 437), sed contra 1: *Augustinus et Damascenus: Anathema sit qui dicit, Verbum deposuisse quod semel assumsit* (*verified*, LEDGER I04c-6). *De substancia fidei* is Wyclif's gloss: Bonaventure argues that neither the soul (q. 1) nor the flesh (q. 2) was separated. The words are neither father's. Quaracchi gives Augustine *sententialiter*, *In Ioh.* 47.10 (PL 35, 1738: *A Verbo autem animam separatam esse non dico*), and calls the axiom one ascribed to Gregory of Nyssa or to the Damascene (*De fide* III.27, IV.1). Harris's margin names Augustine and John of Damascus. *Deposuisse* "laid down" is kept apart from *dimittere* "lay aside". O omits *dicentes*.
[^16]: **G.** 60.13 *passio uniti*: *passio* is here the passive correlate of *accio*, "the being-acted-on", not the attribute of the *Posterior Analytics*. The threefold union (*actio unientis … passio unibilis … relatio unitorum*) and the horse and the bridle (*si teneam equum per frenum … non tamen duplici tentione teneo utrumque, sed unica*) are Bonaventure's, d. 21 a. 1 q. 3 and ad 3 (Quaracchi III, 440–441) (*verified*, LEDGER I04c-6). 60.14–15 *unione activa*: A has *activa* barely legible in an erasure, B *anima quo*, C *activa conclusio*; B *duplex* for *triplex*.
[^17]: **T.** 60.19 *[corpus]*: supplied by Harris against every manuscript (*corpus in codd. omnibus omissum restitui*). The manuscripts' text construes, *quod tunc fuit corpus Christi et anima eius anima*, "that the body was then Christ's, and his soul [his] soul", and the English follows it. Thomas Aquinas, *Scriptum super Sent.* III d. 21 q. 1 a. 1 qc. 1–2: neither the body nor the soul was separated from the Godhead in death, but *non potest dici quod filius Dei sit anima* (qc. 2 ad 3), *neutrum illorum de Christo praedicatur* (ad 6) (*verified*, LEDGER I04c-8).
[^18]: **C.** 60.26–27 *ut patet ampliantibus presens tempus*: for those who extend the present tense to what was and will be, "this is the body of Christ" holds of the corpse before and after. 60.22 O *aliter* for *Arguitur*; 60.29 A B C *et quod* for *quia*, "and that the Word has".
[^19]: **T.** 61.1 *manet*: O *nmqȝ*, which Harris queries as *naturam habet*. 61.2 *subiectas partes*: O *substancias*; A B C *ut* for *et*.
[^20]: **S.** The withered hand: Matt. 12:10. The extremities dying before the heart: Wyclif cites *De anima*, and Harris's Index II lists the place under Aristotle.
