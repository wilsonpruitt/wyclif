# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 6, part 5: that Christ was and was not: the last objections, and contradiction among the natures

## ¶1 · 100.19–101.30

> [p. 100] Quod si 2° obicitur quod ‘Christus’ dicens duas naturas dicit principaliter ac simpliciter naturam increatam, cum analogum¹⁰ per se sumptum secundum famosius¹¹ debet intelligi; dicitur, quod si illa evidencia procederet, negandum¹² esset omne predicabile de¹³ Christo humanitus,¹³ et per consequens totum misterium et fides incarnacionis. Ideo, cum scriptura et sancti doctores concedunt simpliciter utramque maneriem predicatorum de Christo, qui nos sumus ut audeamus¹⁴ tam consolatoriam fidem¹⁵ infringere? Unde Augustinus 2° De Visitacione Infirmorum: ‘Cum, inquit, credatur scripture sacre testimoniis sunt alia de Deo Filio nobis¹⁶¹⁷ propinqua et contigua, et¹⁶ ex ipsa propinquitate sui et contiguitate nobis familiaria, et ex ipsa familiaritate nobis delectabilia. Delectabile quippe est homini et salutare [p. 101] morienti loqui et saciari de humanitate Christi. Quod enim “Verbum caro factum est et habitavit in nobis,” hominis est; quod homo Deus factus est, hominis est: utrumque ergo hoc ineffabile sacramentum hominis est. Quoniam ergo infirmus hominis intellectus, ex sui ipsius humanitate ponderosa ebetatus, ‘videns nunc per speculum in enigmate,’ non potest Dei divinitatem, ut dignum est, comprehendere; ad illam, que sua est, Christi humanitatem oculum suum intellectualem reflectat, in illa delectetur, ex illa sacietur, ad illam faciem suam tanquam parietem cum Ezechia convertat.’ Et sequitur consilium bonum morbis pestilencialibus fatigatis. ‘De homine, inquit, tuo puro fac tibi participem,¹ quia prius fecit Deus de homine tuo puro sibi participem.¹ Ex divinitate processit salvacio et ex humanitate redempcio: utrumque tamen ex utroque:² sed,³ quod unum est michi quadam cognacione et secreta quadam affeccione [et] conglutinio quodam,³ proprio iure consanguinitatis vendico, tucius et iocundius loquor ad meum Iesum quam ad aliquem sanctorum spirituum⁴: plus debet Christus michi⁵ quam cuilibet celestium spirituum⁶ quia, quod tu es, fieri dignatus est Deus, non factus est angelus. Nollem, inquit, habere locum angeli, si possem habere locum debitum homini.’ Nec dubito quin omnes logici nesciunt offendendo impingere in illam sentenciam de vi vocis, quia cum Christus sit frater noster, ut patet Ioh. 20. ‘vade ad fratres meos et dic eis etc.’; et ad Hebre. 2° ‘non confunditur eos fratres vocare dicens: “nunciabo nomen tuum fratribus meis”’: consequens est quod sit eadem communis humanitas, que est quilibet frater suus; et per consequens debet lege proximitatis diligere fratres suos.

And if, secondly, [it is objected]{g:obicitur} that "Christ", which [signifies]{g:dicere} two [natures]{g:natura}, signifies principally and [without qualification]{g:simpliciter} the uncreated nature, since an analogous term taken [of itself]{g:per-se} ought to be understood according to its [best-known]{g:famosior} [sense]: the answer is that if that [evidence]{g:evidencia} held good, everything predicable of Christ [humanly]{g:humanitus} would have to be denied, and consequently the whole [mystery]{g:misterium} and faith of the incarnation. Therefore, since scripture and the holy doctors grant without qualification both [kinds]{g:maneries} of predicates of Christ, who are we, that we should dare to break so consoling a faith? Hence Augustine, in the second [book] On Visiting the Sick: "Since," he says, "it is believed on the testimonies of holy scripture, there are other things about God the Son that are near and adjoining to us, and from that very nearness and adjacency of theirs familiar to us, and from that very familiarity delightful to us.[^1] For it is delightful to a man, and wholesome to one [101] dying, to speak of the [humanity]{g:humanitas} of Christ and be filled with it. For that 'the [Word]{g:verbum} was made flesh and dwelt among us' is man's; that man was made God is man's: both, then, this ineffable [sacrament]{g:sacramentum} is man's.[^2] Since, then, man's weak understanding, dulled by its own heavy humanity, 'seeing now through a glass in a dark manner',[^3] cannot comprehend the [divinity]{g:divinitas} of God as is fitting, let it turn back its intellectual eye to that humanity of Christ which is its own, let it delight in it, be filled from it, let it turn its face to it as to the wall, with Ezechias." And there follows good counsel for those worn out by pestilential diseases. "Of your own pure man," he says, "make for yourself a sharing, for God first made, of your own pure man, a sharing for himself.[^4] From the divinity proceeded salvation and from the humanity redemption: yet each from both: but what is mine (mine because [it is] from me) by a certain thought and kinship, and a certain secret affection and a certain gluing-together, I claim for myself by the proper right of consanguinity;[^5] more safely and more joyfully I speak to my Jesus than to any of the holy spirits: Christ owes more to me than to any of the heavenly spirits, because God deigned to be made what you are; he was not made an angel. I would not," he says, "have the place of an angel, if I could have the place due to a man." Nor do I doubt that all the logicians, stumbling, know not how to dash against that [teaching]{g:sentencia} [by the force of the words]{g:de-vi-vocis},[^6] because, since Christ is our brother, as is clear from John 20, "go to my brethren and say to them, etc.";[^7] and from Hebrews 2, "he is not ashamed to call them brethren, saying: 'I will declare thy name to my brethren'": it is [consequent]{g:consequens} that it is the same common humanity which is each brother of his; and consequently he ought to love his brothers by the [law of neighbourhood]{g:lex-proximitatis}.

## ¶2 · 101.31–102.4

> [p. 101] Sed 3° obicitur per hoc quod hereticum est credere Iesum Christum non fuisse ante concepcionem ex virgine, sicut⁷ patet de Heresi 44ᵃ. superius recitata. Ymo cum contradictoria [p. 102] non verificantur de eodem, et Christus Deus est eternaliter post et ante; videtur quod, concesso¹ Christum semper fuisse, negandum est, ut contradictorium, eundem Christum aliquando non fuisse.

But thirdly [it is objected, by appeal to]{g:obicitur-per} this: that it is heretical to believe "Jesus Christ was not before the conception from the virgin", as is clear from the forty-fourth heresy, recited above.[^8] Indeed, since contradictories [102] are not [true of]{g:verificari} the same thing, and Christ is God eternally, after and before, it seems that, "Christ always was" being granted, "the same Christ at some time was not" must be denied, as its contradictory.

## ¶3 · 102.5–103.3

> [p. 102] Hic dico quod, si essem pure gentilis philosophus indifferens omni secte coram capciosissimis logicis intendentibus² michi³ concludere; dum tamen admitterem gracia argumenti istam philosophicam⁴ supposicionem et necessariam fidei Christiane, quod eadem persona Verbi est trium naturarum quelibet; ego concederem conclusionem tanquam⁵ sine repugnancia⁵ consequentem. Pro cuius declaracione primo⁶ suppono quod in equivocis non sit contradiccio: 2° quod summa equivocacio sit inter deitatem et naturam creatam, cum in nullo universali univoco conveniunt: 3° quod persona Verbi sit corpus, anima, et deitas singillatim. Quibus suppositis patet ex posicione⁷ tam quod iste Christus fuit in mundi principio. Affirmativa patet ex hoc quod illa deitas tunc fuit et ipsa est Christus: negativa autem patet ex hoc quod illa humanitas tunc non fuit, et ipsa est Christus. Sicut ergo non est contradiccio quod homo currit et homo non currit, cum multa sunt quorum⁸ quodlibet est homo, pro quorum⁸ uno verificatur affirmativa, et pro alio negativa; sic in proposito, cum multa sunt,⁹ quorum quodlibet est Christus. Et hinc dicit beatus Ambrosius 3° libro De Spiritu Sancto quoad¹⁰ logicam subtiliter¹¹ et profunde. ‘Generalis, inquit, est fides ista¹² quod Christus est Dei filius¹² et¹³ natus ex virgine, quem quasi gigantem propheta describit, eo quod biformis gemine nature unus consors divinitatis et corporis. Idem ergo paciebatur et non paciebatur, moriebatur et non moriebatur, sepeliebatur¹⁴ et non sepeliebatur,¹⁴ resurgebat et non resurgebat; resurgebat secundum carnem, que mortua [p. 103] fuerat, non secundum Verbum, quod apud Deum semper manebat.’ Et quando ‘sapiebam ut¹ parvulus,’ putabam¹ istum sanctum multum ignarum logice.

Here I say that, if I were purely a pagan philosopher, indifferent to every sect, before the most captious logicians intent on driving me to a conclusion;[^9] provided only that I admitted, for the sake of argument, this philosophical [supposition]{g:supposicio}, and one necessary to the Christian faith, that the same [person]{g:persona} of the Word is each of three natures, I would grant the [conclusion]{g:conclusio} as following without [incompatibility]{g:repugnancia}.[^10] For [making]{g:declarare} this clear I suppose, first, that in equivocals there is no contradiction; secondly, that the highest [equivocation]{g:equivocacio} is between the [Godhead]{g:deitas} and created nature, since they [agree]{g:convenire} in no [univocal]{g:univocus} [universal]{g:universale}; thirdly, that the person of the Word is body, soul, and Godhead, each singly. These being supposed, it is clear [expositorily]{g:expositorie}[^11] as much that this Christ was at the beginning of the world [as that he was not].[^12] The affirmative is clear from this, that that Godhead was then, and it is Christ; and the negative is clear from this, that that humanity was not then, and it is Christ. As, then, there is no contradiction in "a man runs" and "a man does not run", since there are many things each of which is a man, for one of which the affirmative is true and for another the negative, so in the case at hand, since there are many things each of which is Christ. And hence blessed Ambrose speaks in the third book On the Holy Spirit, subtly and profoundly as to logic: "This," he says, "is the general faith, that Christ is the Son of God and born of the virgin, whom the prophet describes as a giant,[^13] because, two-formed, of a twin nature, he is one sharer of divinity and of body. The same one, then, suffered and did not suffer, died and did not die, was buried and was not buried, rose and did not rise; he rose as to the flesh, which [103] had been dead, not as to the Word, which always remained with God."[^14] And when "I understood as a child", I thought this saint very ignorant of logic.

## ¶4 · 103.4–103.19

> [p. 103] Et patet quomodo intelligende sunt negative doctorum in materia de Incarnacione. Unde Ambrosius primo De Trinitate ca° 6 dicit quod Christus non est creatura: et tamen constat ipsum² expressissime dicere quod Christus suscepit predicaciones humanas univoce cum aliis, in tantum quod dicit eum ad litteram ‘profecisse sapiencia et etate,’ et ignorasse multa; ut probat, libro 3°· De Spiritu Sancto, auctoritate scripture. Et sic intelligendum est dictum Augustini in sermone De Fide: ‘si quis, inquit, dixerit Dei Filium passum, anathema sit.’ Exponit enim³ seipsum ibidem, quod hoc intelligendum est secundum divinitatem, cum affirmativa sit de substancia fidei, scilicet, quod Dei Filius passus est, ut sepe meminit Augustinus. Et ita concordanda sunt alia dicta doctorum, non concedendo contradictoria, sed veritates nedum simul esse veras, sed a quocunque fideli catholice concedendas.⁴

And it is clear how the negatives of the doctors in the matter of the incarnation are to be understood. Hence Ambrose, in the first [book] On the Trinity, c. 6, says that "Christ is not a [creature]{g:creatura}";[^15] and yet it is evident that he says most expressly that Christ [took up]{g:suscipere} human [predications]{g:predicacio} [univocally]{g:univoce} with others, so much so that he says he literally "advanced in wisdom and age", and was ignorant of many things; as he proves, in book 3 On the Holy Spirit, by the authority of scripture.[^16] And so is to be understood the saying of Augustine in the sermon On the Faith: "If anyone," he says, "says the Son of God suffered, let him be anathema." For he expounds himself in the same place: that this is to be understood as to the divinity, since the affirmative is of the substance of the faith, namely "the Son of God suffered", as Augustine often recalls.[^17] And so the other sayings of the doctors are to be brought into agreement: not by granting contradictories, but [by granting] that truths are [not only]{g:nedum} true together, but to be granted catholicly by any of the faithful whatever.

## ¶5 · 103.20–103.28

> [p. 103] Quod si queritur quomodo dabimus contradiccionem in talibus cum negacio preposita toti non sufficit; dicitur⁵ iuxta regulam Aristotelis primo Elenchorum ca° 4°, quod enunciando contradictoria de aliquo attendendum est quod illud sit idem ultimum singulare⁶ et secundum idem simpliciter et in eodem tempore; ut, quod idem Christus in numero secundum⁷ eandem naturam in numero⁷ sit simul⁸ vivus et mortuus, passus et non passus. Et ita de aliis oppositis est repugnancia.

And if it is asked how we shall assign a contradiction in such cases, since [a negation placed before the whole]{g:negacio-preposita-toti} does not suffice, the answer is, following the rule of Aristotle in the first [book] of the Elenchi, c. 4, that in enunciating contradictories of something one must attend that it be the same last singular, and in the same respect without qualification, and at the same time:[^18] as, that the same Christ in number, as to the same nature in number, be at once living and dead, suffering and not suffering.[^19] And so of the other opposites there is incompatibility.

## ¶6 · 103.29–104.13

> [p. 103] Unde concedentes quod Christus eciam⁹ secundum altissimam naturam in eo non habuit existenciam ante conceptum ex virgine senserunt,¹⁰ ut Augustinus recitat, nimis heretice. Non autem illi, qui concedunt ipsum non preextitisse subintelligendo secundum humanitatem ad sensum expositum. [p. 104] Contradiccio enim non est nominis tantum, sed et¹ rei et nominis. Et indubie causa quare scola discessit ab illa logica fuit inanis² apparencia² sophistica, qua mavult vitare apparenciam redargucionis vel puerilis inconsequencie, quam pausare³ in sensu reali scripture. Prima⁴ ad Cor. 4° scribitur: ‘nos stulti propter Christum.’ Nam apostoli, predicantes Deum⁵ crucifixum et mortuum, et⁶ simul parem Deo et minorem Deo, reputati sunt nimirum stulti tam a philosophis quam a plebeis. Sed⁷ ‘siquis videtur inter vos sapiens esse in hoc seculo, sic stultus fiat ut sit sapiens’ Deo:’ ut precipit Apostolus prima ad Cor. 3°·, quia indubie gentiles philosophi ex ignorancia logice et methaphisice reputant sensum illum stulticiam esse.

Hence those who granted that Christ, even as to the highest nature in him, did not have existence before the conception from the virgin, thought, as Augustine recounts, [exceedingly]{g:nimis} heretically.[^20] But not those who grant "he did not pre-exist", [understanding as implied]{g:subintelligere} "as to the humanity", in the sense expounded. [104] For contradiction is not of the name only, but of the thing and the name.[^21] And without doubt the cause why the school departed from that logic was an empty sophistical appearance, by which it would rather avoid the appearance of [refutation]{g:redarguere} or of a childish [inconsistency]{g:inconsequens} than rest in the real sense of scripture. In the first [epistle] to the Corinthians, 4, it is written: "We are fools for Christ's sake." For the apostles, preaching God crucified and dead, and at once equal to God and less than God, were, no wonder, reputed fools both by the philosophers and by the common people. But "if any man among you seem to be wise in this world, let him so become a fool, that he may be wise" to God, as the Apostle commands in the first [epistle] to the Corinthians, 3;[^22] for without doubt the pagan philosophers, from ignorance of logic and metaphysics, reckon that sense to be folly.

## ¶7 · 104.14–105.9

> [p. 104] Et si queratur, utrum concedi debet quod Deus aliquando non fuit, sicut generabatur temporaliter vel incepit;⁸ videtur michi quod licet illud sane posset intelligi, tamen iste sensus non ministratur ex terminis. Ideo ego concedo negativas solum cum subiectis signantibus geminas substancias in Filio, ut Messias ille homo Iesus aliquando non fuit: quia in illis intelligo naturam que incepit⁹ esse et aliquando non fuit. Non¹⁰ sic autem in nominibus appropriate competentibus deitati. Conceditur tamen quod Deus fuit natus de virgine, passus et mortuus. Sed illud probabitur resolvendo predicacionem ad hominem sic formatum. Et ex hoc non sequitur quod Deus aliquando non fuit, sed¹¹ quod secundum hominem aliquando non fuit. Nec est incepcio vel generacio Deo conveniens, Deum esse et ipsum aliquando non fuisse: sed sic ex esse aliquando¹² humanitus et ex negacione, que est,¹³ antea humanitus non fuisse, cum instanti et aliis principiantibus illum motum. Et patet quod non sequitur—Christus aliquando non fuit; et ipse est Deus: ergo Deus¹⁴ aliquando non fuit—propter extraneacionem [p. 105] supposicionis. Unde si concedimus quod Deus genuit Deum et Deus non genuit Deum, licet tantum sit unus Deus propter distinccionem personarum; quanto magis concederemus quod Christus est factus et Christus non est factus, licet tantum unus sit Christus: cum sicut Deus est tres persone, sic Christus est tres nature, longe nobis manifestius disparate.¹ Et patet exemplariter quomodo respondebitur ad² questiones et communes² obiectus in ista materia seminatos.

And if it is asked whether "God at some time was not" ought to be granted, as he was begotten in time, or began:[^23] it seems to me that, though that could be soundly understood, yet that sense is not supplied by the terms. Therefore I grant the negatives only with subjects that signify the twin substances in the Son, as "the Messiah, that man Jesus, at some time was not": for in those I understand the nature that began and at some time was not.[^24] But not so in names that belong appropriately to the Godhead. Yet it is granted that "God was born of the virgin, suffered and died". But that will be proved by resolving the predication to the man so formed.[^25] And from this it does not follow that "God at some time was not", but that "as to the man, he at some time was not". Nor is the [beginning]{g:incepcio} or generation that befits God this, that God is and that he at some time was not; but thus: from being, at some time, humanly, and from the negation, which is, not to have been humanly before, together with the instant and the other things that are the [principle]{g:principiare} of that motion.[^26] And it is clear that it does not follow, "Christ at some time was not; and he is God: therefore God at some time was not",[^27] because of the [estrangement]{g:extraneare} [105] of supposition. Hence if, because of the distinction of the persons, we grant that "God begot God and God did not beget God", though there is only one God, how much more should we grant that "Christ is made and Christ is not made", though there is only one Christ: since as God is three persons, so Christ is three natures, far more manifestly disparate to us.[^28] And it is clear by example how the questions and the common [objections]{g:objectum} sown in this matter will be answered.

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Cum … credatur … sunt alia*: the period does not construe as printed (*cum* with no verb of its own, and *alia* "other things" with nothing before it to be other than); nothing is lost, though. In the source, pseudo-Augustine, *De visitatione infirmorum* II.2 (PL 40, 1152–1153), *Enimvero credatur sacrae Scripturae testimoniis* is a sentence of its own, and *Sunt et alia de Deo Filio quasi nobis propinqua* sets these "other things" against the mystery of the Trinity in the sentence before, which is to be believed rather than expounded. Wyclif's *cum* joins the two. The English renders the words as they stand (*verified*, LEDGER I06e-1).
[^2]: **G.** *Sacramentum* here is the Augustinian "mystery" of the incarnation. **C.** *Quod homo Deus factus est*: "that man was made God", in the order of the Latin; "that God was made man" (*homo* predicate) is possible, the word order favours the first.
[^3]: **S.** 1 Cor. 13:12, Vulgate *videmus*, made a participle in the sentence. "As to the wall … with Ezechias": Isa. 38:2.
[^4]: **T.** "A sharing": *participium* (twice), with A B C and the source (*De visit. inf.* II.2, *quoddam participium … sibi participium*), against the print's *participem* "a sharer" with O (101.12, 101.13). **C.** *De homine tuo puro*: "your own pure man", the unmixed human nature that is the reader's own and Christ's.
[^5]: **T.** "What is mine (mine because [it is] from me) by a certain thought and kinship": A B C *quod meum est meum quod ex me michi quadam cogitacione et cognacione*, with the source (*De visit. inf.* II.2: *quia meum est meum, quia et ex me mihi quadam cogitatione et cognatione, et secreta quadam affectione conglutino*), against the print's *quod unum est michi quadam cognacione* (101.15–17); O reads *quod unum est michi quadam cognacione et cognacione*. [^6]: **C.** *Nesciunt offendendo impingere*: "stumbling, know not how to dash against", that is, no logician can overturn the teaching by the force of the words, however he stumbles at it; *offendendo* may also be "by taking offence". *De vi vocis* may also go with *sentenciam*, "that teaching [taken] by the force of the words", which the *quia* clause (Christ our brother, one common humanity) then proves.

[^8]: **S.** Augustine, *De haeresibus* 44 (the Paulianists, who held that Christ did not exist before Mary), PL 42, 34 (*verified*); "recited above" refers to 80.22, where the Paulianists are heresy 44 (Harris, Note on 101.33; LEDGER I06e-2). **T.** *Sicut*: A B C *et sic*.
[^9]: **C.** *Intendentibus michi concludere*: "intent on driving me to a conclusion", *michi* as the one argued against; "intent on inferring to me" is possible.

[^12]: **T.** *Tam* has no *quam*, and the next sentences prove an affirmative and a negative; a clause *quam quod non fuit* (ending like the one before it) seems lost. Supplied in brackets (our emendation).
