# *De ecclesia*, chapter 16, part 5: the case against appropriation; the sixth argument: the captive knight; Ambrose on the church's gold

## ¶1 · 372.2–375.2

> [p. 372] Primo per hoc quod ecclesia cleri vixit sic exproprietarie ex ordinacione Christi et apostolorum usque ad dotacionem cesaream; sed et usque hodie in plus religiosis clericis remanet illa vita, et illam conversacionem declaravi superius esse plus autenticam, plus meritoriam et securam. Secundo ex hoc quod stat minando destruccionem regni superhabundare multitudinem clericorum, ut docet Apostolus I, Cor. XII°, in exemplo de corpore humano, quod foret nimis monstruosum, si totum, medietas vel tercia foret oculus. Cum ergo omne utile ad regimen rei publice secundum Augustinum, II° De Doctrina Christiana, in fine, sit reperibile in scriptura, videtur quod in scriptura fundabilis sit regula secundum quam in populo proporcionaliter sit de clero, nec inveniet homo regulam reccius directivam quam ministrando clero secundum formam quam ecclesia primitiva instituit, et limitando eis officium quod tunc gessit. Ergo hoc foret conveniens racioni. Si enim in veteri testamento, quando sacerdotes et Levite onerati erant importabiliter cum disposicionibus bestiarum ad victimas, limitati sunt ad istam porcionem triplicem (ut dixi superius XIII° cap.), multo magis in tempore legis gracie, quando clerus alleviatus ab oneribus legis debet esse pauperior, et populus ad relevandum est prompcior. Tercio arguitur ex precavicione inconvenientis multiplicis quod taliter tolleretur; primo, inquam, non aspiraret tanta multitudo ad sacerdocium plus propter affeccionem ad seculum quam ad sacerdotale ministerium. Secundo non foret tanta mercacio symoniaca nec tanta contencio et vulpina confederacio sicut modo, et ista conturbant regna plurimum. Tercio brachium seculare non tantum necessitaretur pugnare [p. 373] pro bonis fortune ut modo, tum quia ex uno latere universitas temporalis dominii saciaret, tum eciam quia clerus vivens apostolice eorum tyrannidem mitigaret. Quarto quia seculares sic magis disponerentur ad magis ordinate imperciendum elemosinas magis dignis et plus paterent discolorum latebre que hodie in vestimentis ovium sunt occulte. Quinto quia elemosine limitate clero ad distribuendum pauperibus forent in manu layca equius distribute. Notet, inquam, speculator primo bona regni devoluta ad manum mortuam, notet secundo subtraccionem partis eorum proporcionalem numero et vite procuratorum pauperum et notet tercio quomodo residuum est expensum. Dicitur enim quod fratres peregrini et alii egentes tantam elemosinam recipiunt de exteris ut de illis. Sexto quia secularibus occupantibus illa bona iam incarcerata tamquam suis (ut convenit) foret terra magis fructifera, plus habundans bestiis et aliis bonis fortune et ad defensionem regni ac augmentum exercitus christiani amplius populosa. Sic enim contigit ante dictam dotacionem, et racio probabilis sumpta ex facto cum eius circumstanciis contestatur. Deus enim non fundit aquam pluvie differenter per gurgites sed guttatim et roride facit ad nutrimentum terre nascencium pluviam distillare, docens nos per hoc indubie omnia genera bonorum nostrorum sine personarum accepcione prudenter dispergere, et sic (si non fallor) ista expropriacio excitaret devocionem populi, prepararet ad elemosinas perpetuas cleri Christi, et per consequens cederet in relevaminis pauperum notabile incrementum. Unde quod ecclesie et redditus regni sic sunt appropriata collegiis, error appropriacionis ecclesiarum abbathiis illud fecit; ex hinc enim tollebantur rectores et eorum clerici qui augerent [p. 374] universitates et clerimoniam regni nostri, eo quod vel divise sunt ecclesie vel cognatis et familiaribus patronorum clericorum infideliter distribute, sic quod nisi Deus reliquisset nobis semen in fundacione collegiorum ex licencia regum Anglie quoad clerimoniam quasi Sodoma fuissemus; melius tamen foret quod quilibet collegiatus aptus ad regimen animarum haberet ecclesiam que iam appropriatur monasterio cui pastum spiritualem et sacramenta debite ministraret. Tunc enim prosperaretur regnum in corporalibus et spiritualibus et cresceret universitas nostra tam numero quam virtute; in cuius signum ante catheclismum appropriacionis huiusmodi erant in Oxonia sexaginta milia scolarium, ubi hodie non sunt tria. Universitas eciam plus crescit hodie per presbiteros mobiliter conducticios, quam crescit per perpetuas cantarias, nec oporteret habere tot privilegia a rege nisi quia ob defectum sacerdotum et supplantacionem privilegiorum laycorum per clericos layci eis propter sensacionem iniurie oppido sunt infesti. Olim autem non fuit sentencia ista heretica ante dotacionem ecclesie, quando in ea floruerunt catholice veritates. Ymmo (si non fallor) sonaret ad emendacionem ecclesie et ad stabilimentum regni et ad profectum cuiuscunque membri ecclesie tam vivi quam mortui quod proviso cuilibet clerico secundum ordinem suum de necessario [p. 375] nutrimento et tegumento ista conclusio sit paulative et provide practizata.

First, by this: that the [church]{g:ecclesia} of the [clergy]{g:clerus} lived thus [without property]{g:exproprietarie} by the [ordinance]{g:ordinacio} of Christ and the apostles up to the caesarean [endowment]{g:dotacio}; and even to this day that life remains in the more religious clerics, and I declared above that this [manner of life]{g:conversacio} is more [authentic]{g:autenticus}, more meritorious and more secure. Second, from this: that it is the case, to the threat of the destruction of the [kingdom]{g:regnum}, that the multitude of clerics superabounds, as the Apostle teaches, 1 Corinthians 12, in the example of the human body, that it would be too monstrous if the whole, the half or the third were an eye.[^1] Since, therefore, everything useful for the government of the commonwealth is, according to Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* II, at the end, to be found in scripture, it seems that a rule can be founded in scripture according to which there should be clergy in proportion among the people; nor will a man find a rule more rightly directive than by ministering to the clergy according to the form which the primitive church instituted, and by [assigning]{g:limitare} them the office which it then bore.[^2] Therefore this would be agreeable to reason. For if in the old testament, when the [priests]{g:sacerdos} and Levites were burdened unbearably with the handling of beasts for victims, they were limited to that threefold portion (as I said above, chapter 13), much more in the time of the law of grace, when the clergy, relieved of the burdens of the law, ought to be poorer, and the people is readier to relieve [them].[^3] Third, it is argued from the forestalling of manifold [troubles]{g:inconveniens}, which would in this way be removed. First, I say, so great a multitude would not aspire to the priesthood more from affection for the world than for the priestly ministry. Second, there would not be so much simoniac traffic, nor so much contention and fox-like alliance, as now; and these disturb kingdoms very greatly. Third, the [secular arm]{g:brachium-seculare} would not be so much necessitated to fight [373] for the goods of fortune as now, both because on the one side the totality of temporal [lordship]{g:dominium} would satisfy [it], and also because a clergy living apostolically would mitigate their tyranny.[^4] Fourth, because [seculars]{g:secularis} would thus be more disposed to bestow [alms]{g:elemosina} in a more ordered way on the more worthy, and the hiding-places of the [froward]{g:discoli}, which today are hidden "in the clothing of sheep", would lie more open. Fifth, because the alms assigned to the clergy for distribution to the poor would be distributed more equitably in a lay hand. Let the [watchman]{g:speculator} note, I say, first, the goods of the kingdom devolved to [the dead hand]{g:manus-mortua}; let him note, second, the withdrawal of a part of them proportional to the number and the life of the [proctors]{g:procurator} of the poor; and let him note, third, how the residue is spent. For it is said that the pilgrim friars and other needy people receive as much alms from outsiders as from them.[^5] Sixth, because, if seculars occupied those goods now imprisoned as their own (as is fitting), the land would be more fruitful, more abounding in beasts and other goods of fortune, and more populous for the defence of the kingdom and the increase of the christian army. For so it happened before the said endowment, and a probable reason, taken from the fact with its circumstances, bears witness to it. For God does not pour out the rain-water unevenly in torrents, but makes the rain distil drop by drop and as dew for the nourishment of what springs from the earth, teaching us by this beyond doubt to disperse all kinds of our goods prudently, without respect of persons; and so (if I am not mistaken) this [renunciation of property]{g:expropriacio} would rouse the devotion of the people, would prepare [it] for perpetual alms to the clergy of Christ, and consequently would turn to a notable increase in the relief of the poor.[^6] Hence, that the churches and [revenues]{g:reditus} of the kingdom are thus appropriated to colleges, the error of the [appropriation]{g:appropriacio} of churches to abbeys has done it; for by this the rectors and their clerics were taken away who would increase [374] the universities and the learning of our kingdom, because the churches were either divided or faithlessly distributed to the kinsmen and household of clerical [patrons]{g:patronus}; so that, unless God had left us seed in the foundation of colleges by licence of the kings of England, we should have been as Sodom as to learning. Yet it would be better that every collegiate apt for the rule of souls should have a church which is now appropriated to a monastery, and should duly minister to it spiritual food and the sacraments.[^7] For then the kingdom would prosper in bodily and spiritual things, and our university would grow both in number and in virtue; in sign of which, before the cataclysm of such appropriation there were sixty thousand scholars in Oxford, where today there are not three.[^8] The university also grows more today through [presbyters]{g:presbiter} hired at will than it grows through perpetual chantries; nor would it need to have so many [privileges]{g:privilegium} from the king, were it not that, because of the default of priests and the supplanting of the privileges of laymen by clerics, laymen are exceedingly hostile to them through their feeling of the injury. And formerly, before the endowment of the church, when catholic truths flourished in it, this [teaching]{g:sentencia} was not heretical. Indeed (if I am not mistaken) it would make for the amendment of the church, and for the establishment of the kingdom, and for the profit of every [member]{g:membrum} of the church, living as well as dead, that, provision being made for every cleric according to his order of necessary [375] nourishment and covering, this conclusion should be [put into practice]{g:practizare} gradually and providently.[^9]

## ¶2 · 375.3–375.20

> [p. 375] Sexto arguitur, sumendo quod principes et domini temporales licite et absque peccato vendunt terras et tenementa sua viris ecclesiasticis et suis successoribus in perpetuum, ergo a forciori conferrent eis temporalia in perpetuum; et assumptum probatur, ponendo quod aliquis dominus secularis sit constitutus in extrema necessitate, sicut sunt milites nostri capti ab hostibus in partibus transmarinis, nec habeat dictus dominus unde se ipsum redimat nisi alienando in perpetuum suum manerium, et cum hoc suppono quod nullus sit qui velit emere dictum manerium nisi collegium nostrum vel aliud ei simile, tunc datum collegium tenetur relevare dictum militem in extrema necessitate positum, et hoc sub pena peccati mortalis; sed non potest ipsum relevare nisi emat ab eo manerium, ergo tenetur in casu sub pena peccati mortalis emere a dicto milite pro perpetuo suum manerium.

Sixth, it is argued, assuming that princes and [temporal lords]{g:temporales-domini} lawfully and without sin sell their lands and tenements to churchmen and their successors in perpetuity: therefore, with stronger reason, they would confer [temporal goods]{g:temporalia} on them in perpetuity. And [the assumption]{g:assumptum} is proved by supposing that some secular lord is set in extreme necessity, as are our [knights]{g:milites} captured by enemies in parts overseas, and that the said lord has nothing with which to redeem himself except by alienating his manor in perpetuity; and with this I suppose that there is no one who will buy the said manor except our college or another like it: then the given college is bound to relieve the said knight, placed in extreme necessity, and this under pain of mortal sin; now it cannot relieve him unless it buys the manor from him; therefore in the case it is bound, under pain of mortal sin, to buy his manor from the said knight in perpetuity.[^10]

## ¶3 · 375.21–375.34

> [p. 375] Iste casus foret michi nimis difficilis, nec video colorem quomodo foret possibilis nisi forte dictum collegium fuerit per dominum huius seculi vinculis insolubilibus mammone iniquitatis adeo cathenatum quod ex induracione necessario dampnabili non possit dictum militem misericorditer relevare, sed necesse habeat eum de hereditate paterna dampnabiliter spoliare. Sed nec casum illum libenter admitterem nec aliud relevamen militis omnino excluderem; tale enim collegium si foret esset summe dyabolicum, quia (ut innuitur) non posset per elemosinam sed non nisi per sacrilegium dictum militem relevare, et revera Deus non posset salvare tale collegium de sua potencia absoluta.

This case would be too difficult for me, nor do I see any [colour]{g:color} how it would be possible, unless perhaps the said college had been so chained by the lord of this world with the indissoluble bonds of the mammon of iniquity that, from a hardening necessarily damnable, it could not mercifully relieve the said knight, but must needs damnably despoil him of his paternal inheritance. But I would neither willingly admit that case nor altogether exclude other relief for the knight; for such a college, if it existed, would be supremely diabolical, because (as is implied) it could not relieve the said knight by alms but only by sacrilege; and truly God could not save such a college by his absolute power.[^11]

## ¶4 · 376.1–376.25

> [p. 376] Debet ergo miles ire ad dominum terre et narrare sibi condiciones talis collegii, et totum regnum teneretur collegium tale venenosum expellere et cum bonis que talis legio occuparet, si fuerit elemosina, militem relevare. Et patet quod hoc fieri posset legaliter ex dicto beati Ambrosii posito in decretis XIIª, q. IIª, Aurum. Aurum, inquit, habet ecclesia non ut servet sed ut eroget et subveniat in necessitatibus. Quid est opus custodire quod nihil adiuvat? An ignoramus quantum auri atque argenti de templo domini Assirii sustulerunt? Nonne melius conflat sacerdos propter alimoniam pauperum, si aliqua subsidia desunt, quam si sacrilegus contaminat et asportat hostis? Nonne dicturus est dominus: Cur passus es tot inopes fame mori? Et certe habebas aurum, unde ministrasses alimoniam. Cur tot captivi in commercium ducti nec redempti ab hoste occisi sunt? Melius fuerat ut vasa vivencium servares quam metallorum; hiis non posset responsum deferri. Quid enim diceres: Timui ne templo Dei ornatus deesset? Aurum, inquit, sacramenta non querunt, neque auro placent que auro non emuntur. Ornatus sacrorum est redempcio captivorum, et vere sunt illa vasa preciosa que redimunt animas a morte. Ille verus est thesaurus domini qui operatur quod sanguis eius operatus est.

The knight ought therefore to go to the lord of the land and tell him the conditions of such a college; and the whole kingdom would be bound to expel such a poisonous college and, with the goods which such a legion would occupy, if they are alms, to relieve the knight.[^12] And it is clear that this could be done lawfully, from the saying of blessed Ambrose placed in the decrees, [Causa] 12, q. 2, *Aurum*: "The church has gold," he says, "not to keep but to lay out, and to bring help in necessities. What need is there to guard what is of no help? Are we ignorant how much gold and silver the Assyrians carried off from the temple of the Lord? Does not a priest do better to melt it down for the sustenance of the poor, if other supplies are lacking, than that a sacrilegious enemy should defile and carry it off? Will not the Lord say: Why did you suffer so many needy to die of hunger? And certainly you had gold, with which you might have ministered sustenance. Why were so many captives led away into trade and, not redeemed, slain by the enemy? It had been better that you kept the vessels of the living than those of metals. To these things no answer could be brought. For what would you say: I feared lest the temple of God should lack its adornment? The sacraments," he says, "do not seek gold, nor do things please by gold which are not bought with gold. The adornment of sacred things is the redemption of captives; and truly those are precious vessels which redeem souls from death. That is the true treasure of the Lord which works what his blood has worked."[^13]

## Notes

[^1]: **C.** *Stat minando destruccionem regni superhabundare multitudinem clericorum*: the ablative gerund is taken as "to the threat of", i.e. the superabundance threatens the kingdom; the construal is uncertain. **S.** 1 Corinthians 12:17 (DR "If the whole body were the eye, where would be the hearing?"); the half and the third are Wyclif's.

[^2]: **S.** Augustine, *De doctrina christiana* 2.42.63, that whatever is useful elsewhere is found in scripture (the wording matches; *regimen rei publice* is Wyclif's gloss; LEDGER E16e-1); A in the margin at 372.13: *Augustinus*. **T.** At 372.19 B reads *limitando eque*. **C.** *Limitando eis officium*: "assigning" (an office), but *limitati sunt* below is "limited", to a portion (GLOSSARY).

[^3]: **S.** The threefold portion of the priests and Levites (tithes, first-fruits and offerings; cf. Numbers 18) is the subject of chapter 13 (so A, corrected in the margin from *XXII*). **T.** At 372.26 B reads *populus* (OCR *populua*).

[^4]: **T.** At 372.27 B reads *Tercio confirmatur ex*. **C.** *Inconveniens* (CONTEXTUAL) here leans to its other sense: troubles in affairs, not an absurdity in argument. *Universitas temporalis dominii*: the whole of temporal lordship, once in lay hands, would satisfy the secular arm, which would no longer need to fight for church goods.

[^5]: **S.** Matthew 7:15 (DR "who come to you in the clothing of sheep"). **C.** *Proporcionalem numero et vite procuratorum pauperum*: the part withdrawn for the poor should answer to the number and the manner of life of those who administer it. *Fratres peregrini*: taken of the wandering friars. *De illis*: from the possessioners themselves. **R.** A in the margin at 373.23: *Nota*.

[^6]: **C.** *Differenter*: "unevenly" (in different measures to different places), against *guttatim*; the word is doubtful in context. *Sine personarum accepcione*: the scriptural phrase "without respect of persons" (Romans 2:11). *Prepararet*: the object, "[it]", is the people.

[^7]: **C.** *Clerimonia*: learning, the lettered state of the clergy (cf. Middle English "clergy" in that sense). *Patronorum clericorum*: taken as "clerical patrons" (religious houses and prelates holding advowsons); it could be "the kinsmen and household clerics of the patrons". **S.** Isaiah 1:9 (DR "Except the Lord of hosts had left us seed, we had been as Sodom"); cf. Romans 9:29. **R.** B in the margin at 374.4: *Approbat ut videtur collegium universitatum*. **T.** At 374.6–7 B reads *quod quibus collegiatus*; at 374.8 *que tam appropriatur*.

[^8]: **T.** At 374.14 B reads *sexaginta sex millia*. **R.** A in the margin: *Sexaginta millia scolarium fuerunt Oxonie, vide supra*. **S.** Loserth compares Richard FitzRalph, *Defensio curatorum*: thirty thousand students at Oxford in his time, not six thousand now (Brown, *Fasciculus* II, 473; Goldast, *Monarchia* II) (LEDGER E16e-2). **C.** *Non sunt tria*: "not three", presumably three thousand; the print gives the bare numeral.

[^9]: **C.** *Mobiliter conducticios*: hired priests removable at will, against the perpetual chantry priest. **T.** At 374.23 A and A₁ lack the words from *et* to *ecclesie*, apparently *et ad stabilimentum … membri ecclesie*. **S.** *Nutrimento et tegumento* recalls 1 Timothy 6:8 (DR "having food and wherewith to be covered").

[^10]: **R.** A in the margin at 375.3: *Sexta racio*; at 375.9: *Casus*. **T.** At 375.5 B reads *et per tenementa*. **C.** *Datum collegium*: the college posited in the case. *Sed non potest* brings the minor premiss.

[^11]: **C.** *Colorem* (GLOSSARY *color*): a plausible ground, not a pretext. *Dominum huius seculi*: the devil (cf. 2 Corinthians 4:4, DR "the god of this world"); *mammone iniquitatis*: Luke 16:9 (DR "the mammon of iniquity"). *De sua potencia absoluta*: God's absolute power, as against his ordained power. **R.** A in the margin at 375.21: *Responsio*.

[^12]: **C.** *Legio*: the print's word, "legion", apparently a play on *collegium* and on the "Legion" of Mark 5:9; taken as it stands. *Si fuerit elemosina*: if the goods are alms (and so the poor's).

[^13]: **S.** Ambrose, *De officiis* 2.28 (Loserth: "lib. II, De Officiis, c. XXVIII"), as received in Gratian, *Decretum* C.12 q.2 c.70 (Loserth: "Decreti sec. pars, causa XII, q. II, cap. LXX"; the modern form is ours) (LEDGER E16e-3). **R.** A in the margin at 376.5: *Ambrosius*; at 376.7: *Aurum habet ecclesia*; B in the margin at 376.16: *Pro redempcione captivorum exponenda sunt vasa ecclesie*. **T.** At 376.15 B reads *aurum aurum*; at 376.17 A reads *Melius fuerant*.
