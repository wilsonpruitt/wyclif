# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 10, part 4: the moderns' gloss on the saints, and Christ and his humanity the same in all their parts

## ¶1 · 171.14–173.7

> [p. 171] Quoad 2ᵈᵃᵐ³ patet cuilibet volenti advertere, quod glosa est nimis extranea preter mentem sanctorum; ut quando Augustinus, Gregorius, Ieronimus, Anselmus, et alii dicunt, quod utraque substancia, scilicet divina et humana, est Christus, dicit glosa ipsos per hoc intendere, quod altera non est Christus, sed natura eius, nec conveniens secum⁴ in essencia nec persona. Pro certo inter omnes glosas, quas unquam audiveram est hec inculcior, glosare dicta sanctorum directe per sua opposita. Si,⁵ inquam, ista glosa sufficeret, quis formidaret auctoritatem sibi oppositam? Sed⁶ ut pallient⁶ glosam suam, dimittunt verbaliter negativam⁷ dicentes quod Augustinus intendit utramque naturam esse naturam Christi. Quando autem queritur utrum Augustinus intendit utramque naturam esse Christum, dicunt quod non, sed utramque esse naturam Christi. Et quando ulterius opponitur, si⁸ intenderit naturam humanam esse Christum, sicut intendit naturam divinam esse Christum, dicunt quod non: et sic⁹ finaliter incidunt in glosam yronicam supradictam. Sed quam sinistrum est dicere quod tam subtiles logici et [p. 172] philosophi tam pii et prudentes theologi in precipua materia fidei, capciosissimis hereticis circumsepti, dicerent tam crebro et patule quod utraque natura Christi est Deus, quia persona Christi, si finaliter intenderent quod altera est Deus, et¹ altera non est Deus.¹ Et nimis leve² verbum est dicere Augustinum intendere,³ quando dicit³ ‘aliud Deus aliud homo sed non alius, quia non alia persona,’⁴ quod est⁴ quod ipse per hoc intendit, quod non natura alterius persone sit Christi deitas sive humanitas: tum quia glosa illa seipsam interimit falsitate, cum natura divina sit natura alterius persone quam Filii, quia tam Patris quam Doni⁵; tum eciam quia verba essent nimis impertinencia suis sentenciis. Nam Augustinus persepe asserit, quod anima sit Christus et⁶ caro Christus, sicut deitas Christus et humanitas Christus,⁶ utrumque aliud a reliquo sed non alius⁷; ut patet super Ioh. Ome. 47. 78 et sepe alibi ut patet superius. Et ad hunc sensum ut verba sonant ac raciones concordant, exponunt eum Anselmus, Hugo de Sancto Victore, et alii. Unde De Incarnacione ca° 6° in fine sic scribit Anselmus; ‘suscepit Verbum hominem in unitatem persone, ut due nature, scilicet⁸ divina et humana, sint una persona.’ Et hoc dicit Augustinus in Dialogo ad Felicianum; ‘aliud, inquit, Dei Filius, aliud hominis filius, sed non alius:’ et primo De Trinitate 9° ca°; ‘forma Dei⁹ formam⁹ hominis accepit, utraque¹⁰ Deus utraque homo’; et sic de quotlibet similibus, ex quibus patet quod sensus datus Augustini primo De Trinitate¹¹ 16° sit nimis sophisticus,¹¹ quando dicit, ‘talis fuit illa suscepcio, que hominem faceret Deum et Deum hominem’; hoc est, ut inquiunt, ‘illa suscepcio fecit quod homo est Deus, et e contra.’ Quis dubitat eciam laicus¹² de conversione illorum? Non ergo oportuit Augustinum [p. 173] exponere quod idem sit—Deum esse hominem—et—hominem esse Deum. Intenditur¹ ergo, ut alibi sepe dicit, quod natura illa sit² Deus et Deus sit illa natura ypostatice assumendo.³ Et sic favorabilius et coloracius sunt moderni glosandi, qui dicunt quod humanitas non est Deus. Intendunt enim quod Deus nec per se nec formaliter nec secundum essenciam deitatis sit humanitas.

As to the second [reply], it is clear to anyone willing to pay attention that the [gloss]{g:glosa} is [exceedingly]{g:nimis} far-fetched, beyond the mind of the [saints]{g:sancti}:[^1] as when Augustine, Gregory, Jerome, Anselm and others say that each substance, namely the divine and the human, is Christ, the gloss says that by this they mean that the one is not Christ but his [nature]{g:natura}, agreeing with him neither in [essence]{g:essencia} nor in [person]{g:persona}. Certainly, among all the glosses I had ever heard, this is the crudest: to [gloss]{g:glosare} the sayings of the saints directly by their opposites. If, I say, this gloss sufficed, who would fear an authority opposed to himself? But to cloak their gloss they let go the negative [proposition] in words, saying that Augustine means "each nature is the nature of Christ". And when it is asked whether Augustine means "each nature is Christ", they say no, but "each is the nature of Christ". And when it is further objected, whether he meant "the human nature is Christ" as he means "the divine nature is Christ", they say no: and so in the end they fall into the ironical gloss mentioned above.[^2] But how perverse it is to say that such subtle logicians and [172] philosophers, such pious and prudent theologians, in a chief matter of faith, hemmed in by most captious heretics, would say so often and so openly that each nature of Christ is God, because [each is] the person of Christ, if in the end they meant that the one is God and the other is not God. And it is an exceedingly light word to say that Augustine means, when he says "one thing God, another thing man, but not another one, because not another person", that is, that he means by this that the [Godhead]{g:deitas} or the [humanity]{g:humanitas} of Christ is not the nature of another person:[^3] first, because that gloss destroys itself by its falsity, since the divine nature is the nature of another person than the Son, since [it is the nature] of the Father as well as of the Gift; and also because the words would be exceedingly [irrelevant]{g:impertinens} to his [teachings]{g:sentencia}.[^4] For Augustine very often asserts that the soul is Christ and the flesh Christ, as the Godhead [is] Christ and the humanity Christ, each [another thing]{g:aliud} than the other but not another one; as is clear on John, homilies 47 and 78, and often elsewhere, as is clear above. And in this sense, as the words sound and the arguments agree, Anselm, Hugh of St Victor and others expound him. Hence in *De incarnatione*, c. 6, at the end, Anselm writes thus: "The [Word]{g:verbum} took up man into unity of person, so that two natures, namely the divine and the human, are one person."[^5] And Augustine says this in the *Dialogue to Felician*: "Another thing," he says, "the Son of God, another thing the son of man, but not another one"; and in *De Trinitate* book 1, c. 9: "the form of God took the form of man, each God, each man"; and so of any number of like [sayings], from which it is clear that the sense given of Augustine in *De Trinitate* book 1, c. 16, is exceedingly sophistical, when he says, "such was that [taking-up]{g:suscipere}, as would make man God and God man"; that is, as they say, "that taking-up brought it about that 'man is God', and conversely".[^6] Who, even a layman, doubts the [conversion]{g:conversio} of these? So there was no need for Augustine [173] to explain that it is the same, "God is man" and "man is God". What is meant, then, as he often says elsewhere, is that that nature is God and God is that nature, by [assuming]{g:assumere} [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice}. And so [the moderns]{g:moderni} are to be glossed more favourably and [more colourably]{g:coloracior}, who say "the humanity is not God". For they mean that God is neither [of himself]{g:per-se} nor formally nor according to the essence of the Godhead the humanity.[^7]

## ¶2 · 173.8–173.20

> [p. 173] Et si queratur qualis predicacio sit⁴ ista—Deus est homo sive humanitas—, dicitur quod equivocando in nomine accidentis ut⁵ supra; hoc⁵ est, per accidens; sicut et hec— Petrus est bipedalis—secundum⁶ negantes quantitates distingui est predicacio secundum essenciam; non quod deitas sit natura humana, sed quod persona deitatis sit illa essencia. Est eciam in parte predicacio causalis et formalis, cum humanitas causata a Deo sit ‘forma servi,⁷ secundum quam Deus est servus obediens Trinitati. Et patet quod humanitas cum sit forma temporalis distinguitur a Verbo, licet sit ypostatice ipsum Verbum. Nec sequitur per locum a simili quod substancia sit sua quantitas; cum non possit esse personalis unio substancie ad accidens inherens formaliter.

And if it is asked what kind of [predication]{g:predicacio} this is, "God is man" or "[God is the] humanity", the answer is that [it is so] by equivocating on the name of accident, as above; that is, [by accident]{g:per-accidens}; as this also, "Peter is two feet [tall]", according to those who deny that quantities are distinct, is a [predication by essence]{g:predicacio-secundum-essenciam}: not that the Godhead is the human nature, but that the person of the Godhead is that essence.[^8] It is also in part a causal and formal predication, since the humanity caused by God is "the form of a servant", according to which God is a servant obedient to the Trinity.[^9] And it is clear that the humanity, since it is a temporal form, is distinguished from the Word, although it is hypostatically the Word himself. Nor does it [follow]{g:sequitur} by the topic from likeness that a substance is its quantity, since there cannot be a personal [union]{g:unio} of a substance to an [accident]{g:accidens} inhering formally.[^10]

## ¶3 · 173.21–174.17

> [p. 173] Item si sint aliqua duo, quorum omnes partes sint idem inter se, et⁸ ipsa sunt idem⁹ inter se: sed Christus et eius humanitas sunt duo distincta quorum omnes partes sunt idem inter se: ergo⁹ et ipsa tota eisdem¹⁰ adequale composita. Minor¹¹ patet ex hoc quod quelibet pars corporis Christi sicut et totum ipsum corpus et anima est pars humanitatis Christi et e contra; cum Christus non habet partem¹² qualitativam aut quantitativam, nisi secundum humanitatem eo quod secundum deitatem sit indivisibilis omnino. Et maior patet eis qui ponunt omne totum compositum ex partibus esse insimul¹³ illas partes; hoc autem [p. 174] hic supponitur. Et patet cum ista multitudo parcium sit Christus et ista eadem multitudo sit ista humanitas, quod Christus et ista humanitas sunt idem alicui tercio; et per consequens inter se: et cum nulla ydemptitas sit pertinens nisi fuerit personalis, sequitur quod Christus et illa humanitas sunt eadem¹ persona.¹ Et istam sentenciam videtur sapere prima conclusio 5ᵗⁱ² Elementorum Euclidis: ‘si fuerint quotlibet quantitates aliquarum totidem eque multiplices, ut eque maiores aut eque minores, aut singule singulis equales, quemadmodum una³ illarum³ ad sui comparem; necesse est totum aggregatum ex his singulariter⁴ se habere.’ Quod eque evidenter videtur de ydemptitate tocius ad totum, presupposita ydemptitate numerali⁵ cuiuscunque partis ad partem sibi correlatam,⁶ sicut de⁷ paritate proporcionum, sicut patet† applicati.⁸ Nam si sint aliqua duo, et quelibet pars unius ydemptificata fuerit parti alterius et e contra; tunc unum⁹ ydemptificatur¹⁰ primo alteri.

Again, if there are any two things all of whose parts are the same as one another, they too are the same as one another: now Christ and his humanity are two distinct things all of whose parts are the same as one another: therefore the wholes too, composed of the same [parts] in equal measure, [are the same].[^11] The minor is clear from this, that every part of Christ's body, as also the whole body itself and the soul, is a part of Christ's humanity, and conversely; since Christ has no [qualitative]{g:pars-qualitativa} or [quantitative part]{g:pars-quantitativa} except [as to the humanity]{g:secundum-humanitatem}, because [as to the Godhead]{g:secundum-deitatem} he is altogether indivisible. And the major is clear to those who hold that every whole composed of parts is those parts together; and this [174] is supposed here. And it is clear, since this multitude of parts is Christ and this same multitude is this humanity, that Christ and this humanity are the same as some third [thing], and consequently as one another: and since no identity is to the point unless it is personal, it follows that Christ and that humanity are the same person. And this teaching seems to savour of the first conclusion of the fifth [book] of Euclid's *Elements*: "If there are any number of quantities equally multiple of as many [others], as equally greater or equally less, or each equal to each, as one of them [is] to its counterpart; the whole aggregate of them must stand as they do singly."[^12] Which seems equally evident of the identity of whole to whole, presupposing the [numerical identity]{g:ydemptitas-numeralis} of each part to the part correlated with it, as [it is] of the parity of proportions, as is clear † applied.[^13] For if there are any two things, and every part of the one has been [identified]{g:ydemptificare} with a part of the other and conversely, then the one is identified primarily with the other.

## ¶4 · 174.18–174.29

> [p. 174] Pono ergo quod Verbum incipiat a pedibus Petri usque ad caput assumendo ypostatice quamlibet partem quantitivam aut qualitativam Petri, sic quod primo in fine hore tam corpus quam anima sit ypostatice assumpta: et patet, posito quod Verbum non aliunde habuerit partem aliquam, nec assumptum dimiserit ante finem, quod quelibet pars Petri fiet idem parti Verbi; et quelibet pars Verbi fiet¹¹ pars Petri,¹¹ posito quod in hora alia dimittatur. Nec fingendum quod ydemptificabuntur totali ydemptificacione, nisi Christus et Petrus vel eius natura. Si ergo quamlibet¹² partem humanitatis Christi fecit esse partem sui, tunc se totum fecit totam illam naturam, ut loquitur Augustinus.

I posit, then, that the Word begins from Peter's feet up to the head, assuming hypostatically every quantitative or qualitative part of Peter, so that only at the end of the hour are both body and soul hypostatically assumed: and it is clear, supposing that the Word had no part from elsewhere and did not [lay aside]{g:dimittere} what he had assumed before the end, that every part of Peter will become the same as a part of the Word; and every part of the Word will become a part of Peter, supposing that it is laid aside in another hour. Nor is it to be feigned that [any] will be identified by a total identification except Christ and Peter, or his nature.[^14] If, then, he made every part of Christ's humanity to be a part of himself, then he made the whole of himself the whole of that nature, as Augustine speaks.[^15]

## ¶5 · 174.30–175.16

> [p. 174] Et potest confirmari tripliciter.¹³ Primo per hoc quod Christus non est¹⁴ infinitum bonus homo, sed tantum [p. 175] finite bonus ut ·homo; licet sit infinitum bonus Deus, ut hic supponitur: sed omnis bonitas humana, quam habet, resultat precise¹ ex eisdem principiis ex quibus resultat bonitas humanitatis: ergo precise¹ eadem est Christi secundum quod homo, que est bonitas sue humanitatis, cum bonitas et entitas convertuntur: sequitur quod eadem sit entitas Christi, que et sue humanitatis. Sed et hoc² sonat vocabulum et omnia que movent concedere Christum esse creaturam. Non enim est dare, que creatura esset Christus, nisi foret illa humanitas: bonitates enim non possunt dici incommunicantes; et, si in aliquo communicant, communicant³ secundum⁴ totum; nec est inconveniens sed necessarium, quod Christus habeat quiditatem creatam, cum sit univoce homo cum aliis habens denominaciones accidentales creatas, quod sonat in amplius imperfectum: aliter enim non, in quantum homo, foret aliquid.

And it can be confirmed in three ways. First by this, that Christ is not an infinitely good man but only [175] finitely good as man, although he is infinitely good God, as is supposed here: now all the human goodness that he has results precisely from the same principles from which the goodness of the humanity results: therefore precisely the same [goodness] is Christ's [insofar as]{g:secundum-quod} [he is] man as is the goodness of his humanity; since goodness and entity [are convertible]{g:converti}, it follows that the same entity is Christ's as [is] his humanity's.[^16] But this too is what the word sounds, and all the things that move [men] to grant "Christ is a [creature]{g:creatura}". For [one may posit]{g:est-dare} no creature that Christ would be, unless it were that humanity: for goodnesses cannot be called things [that do not communicate]{g:incommunicantes}; and if they communicate in anything, they communicate as to the whole.[^17] Nor is it an [absurdity]{g:inconveniens}, but necessary, that Christ have a created [quiddity]{g:quiditas}, since he is [univocally]{g:univoce} a man with others, having created accidental [namings]{g:denominacio}, which sounds toward what is more imperfect still: for otherwise he would not, [insofar as]{g:in-quantum} [he is] man, be anything.[^18]

## Notes

[^1]: **T/C.** "The second [reply]": A B C read *tercium* "the third" (171.14); Harris notes at the foot that the second reply of 167.20 is in hand, the moderns' gloss that Augustine and the others do not mean "the assumed nature is the Word" but "the nature of the Word". O is with the print, and A B C, one family, do not outweigh it. *Extranea* "far-fetched": foreign to the saints' mind.

[^2]: **C/T.** The negative [proposition] the moderns let go in words is "the human nature is not Christ"; when pressed, they come back to it, which is the gloss "by the opposite". A B C omit *secum* (171.19); C reads *sed* for *Si* (171.22), *negacionem* for *negativam* (171.24); O *sed plus pallientes* "but cloaking it further" (171.23–24), *sed* for *si* (171.29), *quia sit* for *et sic* (171.31).

[^3]: **S/C/T.** Augustine's saying (LEDGER I10d-1, to be checked): *aliud* and *alius*, natures against persons. "That is" renders *quod est*, which A B C omit (172.7); the clause after it gives the moderns' reading of the saying. O omits *et altera non est Deus* (172.4–5) and *leve* (172.5); B reads *intenderet aliud* for *intendere, quando dicit* (172.6).

[^4]: **C/T/G.** "The Gift" (*Donum*): the Holy Spirit. The gloss is false because the divine nature is also the Father's and the Spirit's, and so is the nature of a person other than the Son. A reads *dei*, struck through, B *dōi* (172.11). *Sentenciis*: "teachings", the positions Augustine teaches.

[^5]: **S/T.** Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 47 and 78 (LEDGER I10d-2, to be checked). Anselm, *De incarnatione Verbi*, c. 6 in Wyclif's numbering (Schmitt c. 10; PL 158, c. V), at the end (LEDGER I10d-3, to be checked). Hugh of St Victor is named without a place. B omits *et caro … humanitas Christus* (172.13–14); C omits *alius* (172.15); O omits *scilicet* (172.21).

[^6]: **S/T.** The *Dialogue to Felician* is the *De unitate Trinitatis contra Felicianum* transmitted among Augustine's works (LEDGER I10d-4, to be checked). *De Trinitate* I, Wyclif's c. 9 and c. 16 (LEDGER I10d-5, I10d-6, to be checked: whether Augustine reads *formam servi* where Wyclif has *formam hominis*). [^7]: **C/T.** "By assuming": the gerund, the Word assuming the nature. The moderns' "the humanity is not God", taken of predication of itself, formally and by essence, is a sense Wyclif can grant; so they are glossed "more colourably" than they gloss the saints. A B C read *intendunt* "they mean" for *Intenditur* (173.2); O *fit* for *sit* (173.3); A *assumenda*, corrected from *assumendo* (173.4).

[^8]: **C/T.** The predication is "by accident" only by an equivocation on the name: the humanity is no accident inhering in God. "Those who deny that quantities are distinct" from their subjects: Wyclif's own view (cf. 171.11–13). A B read *septipedalis* "seven feet", O *bipedalitas*, for *bipedalis* (173.11); the print is C's. B reads *accidentis hic*, without *ut supra* (173.10); O *est* for *sit* (173.8).

[^9]: **S/T.** Phil. 2:7 ("taking the form of a servant") and 2:8 ("becoming obedient"). Harris opens the quotation at *forma* and does not close it; the bound is set after *servi*. O omits *servi* (173.15).

[^10]: **C.** The topic from likeness would argue: as the Word is the humanity, so a substance is its quantity. Wyclif blocks it: the union is personal, and there is no personal union of a substance with an accident that inheres in it.

[^11]: **C/T.** The conclusion is elliptical; "[are the same]" is supplied from the major. *Adequale* (173.24) has no variant and is taken as an adverb, "in equal measure", the parts answering one to one; doubtful. [^17]: **C/T.** "The word" (*vocabulum*): probably the name "creature" itself. A B read *sed ex hoc*, C *secundo*, for *Sed et hoc* (175.7). A B C read *communicat semel se secundum totum* (175.12).

[^18]: **C.** *Inconveniens* here is "an absurdity" in argument. *Quod sonat in amplius imperfectum*: created accidental namings import more imperfection than a created quiddity, so the lesser is granted a fortiori; the construal is doubtful.
