# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 3, part 1: that Christ was a man in the three days

## ¶1 · 28.0–28.0

<!-- the editor's bracketed argument at the chapter head: a Latin summary, then his own English
     summary in the same bracket (see the volume CARD for what is translated) -->

> [p. 28] [Ostendit quod Christus fuit homo in triduo, et obicit multipliciter et dissolvit. Christ’s humanity was not suspended during the three days’ entombment.]

[It shows that Christ was a man in [the three days]{g:triduum}, and raises objections in many ways and resolves them.]

## ¶2 · 28.17–29.6

> [p. 28] Ex⁸ istis plane consequitur quod Christus fuit verus homo in triduo inter eius mortem et resurreccionem. Probatur sic. Cuiuslibet⁹ hominis tam corpus quam anima est inseparabiliter idem homo: sed tam corpus Christi quam eius anima per dictum triduum manet: ergo Christus fuit per dictum triduum verus homo. Maior patet ex dictis. Nam quomodocunque scriptura vel alii doctores catholici hoc intellexerunt sive figurative sive aliter, suppono me intelligere; sic tamen quod maneat verum de virtute¹⁰ sermonis: et sic oportet me concedere aliqua¹¹ que secuntur. Nam licet illud argumentum det michi plenam fidem ad conclusionem propositam, verumtamen pro intellectu scripture et veritatum annexarum huic [p. 29] materie cum dictis doctorum oportet illam materiam ulterius triplici medio¹ dilatare. Veritas enim testatur omnimode sibi ipsi, nec aliquid ex veritate consequens offenderet pias aures. Quicquid ergo ex hoc principio perfectus² philosophus sciret deducere concedi debet tanquam catholicum, et oppositum abici³ tanquam dissonum fidei Christiane.

From these things it plainly follows that Christ was a true man in the three days between his death and resurrection. It is proved thus. The body as well as the soul of any man whatever is inseparably the same man; now both the body of Christ and his soul remain through the said three days; therefore Christ was a true man through the said three days. The major is clear from what has been said. For however scripture or other catholic doctors understood this, whether figuratively or otherwise, I suppose that I understand it; yet in such a way that it remains true [in the proper sense of the words]{g:de-virtute-sermonis}:[^1] and so I must grant some things that follow. For although that argument gives me full faith in the proposed [conclusion]{g:conclusio}, nevertheless, for the understanding of scripture and of the truths annexed to this [29] matter together with the sayings of the doctors, I must extend that matter further by a threefold [middle term]{g:media}.[^2] For truth bears witness to itself in every way, nor would anything that follows from truth offend pious ears. Whatever therefore a perfect philosopher would know how to deduce from this principle ought to be granted as catholic, and the opposite rejected as discordant with the Christian faith.

## ¶3 · 29.7–29.24

> [p. 29] Ut primo sequitur ex eius opposito quod Christus non descendisset ad inferos, nec iacuisset sepultus in sepulcro, sicut tamen⁴ superius allegatur ex fide. Si enim descendit ad inferos, vel secundum deitatem vel secundum humanitatem. Non secundum⁵ deitatem, cum non secundum illam sit mobilis; et si secundum humanitatem, tunc illa fuit servata⁶ pro tunc descendens ad inferos. Nec sufficit hoc, nisi illa pro tunc fuerit sibi ypostatice copulata: quia persone non est attribuenda pars⁷ denominacionis⁸ pro tempore pro quo ipsa non est pars persone: cum tunc omne corpus racione materie foret perpetue,⁹ et omnis homo racione anime eque post mortem viveret sicut ante. Quod negant illi, quibus non sapit, quod Christus vixit humanitus in dicto triduo: unde dicunt quod est impossibile de vi vocis quod Christus fuit mortuus aut sepultus vel quod descendit ad inferos: sed sic intelligitur: corpus, quod quondam fuit Christi, sepultum est, et anima, que quondam fuit Christi, descendit ad inferos, sed non Christus,¹⁰ cum tunc non fuit ita quod Christus est.¹⁰

As, first, [it follows]{g:sequitur} from its opposite that Christ would not have descended into hell, nor lain buried in the sepulchre, as is nevertheless [alleged]{g:allegare} above from faith.[^3] For if he descended into hell, [he did so] either [as to the Godhead]{g:secundum-deitatem} or as to [the humanity]{g:humanitas}. Not as to the Godhead, since as to that he is not movable; and if as to the humanity, then that [humanity] was preserved, at that time descending into hell.[^4] Nor does this suffice, unless it was at that time joined to him [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice}: because a part is not to be attributed to the [person]{g:persona} in [naming]{g:denominacio}[^5] for a time for which it is not a part of the person: since then every body would be perpetual by reason of its matter, and every man by reason of his soul would live after death just as before. This is denied by those to whom it does not make sense[^6] that Christ lived [humanly]{g:humanitus} in the said three days: hence they say that "Christ was dead," or "buried," or "descended into hell" is impossible [by the force of the words]{g:de-vi-vocis}; but it is understood thus: the body that was once Christ's was buried, and the soul that was once Christ's descended into hell, but not Christ, since then it was not so that Christ is.

## ¶4 · 29.25–30.22

> [p. 29] Sed revera ista ficticia superaddit perfidiam Iudeorum. Nam turba Iudaica Ioh. 12. dixit Iesu: ‘nos audivimus ex lege quod Christus manet in eternum’: quia Iesaie¹¹ 9° scribitur: ‘super solium David et super regnum eius sedebit, ut confirmet illud et corroboret a modo¹² et usque in sempiternum,’ id est, a tempore quo ‘puer natus est nobis.’ Probatur sic. Iesus natus est rex humanitus, ut patet Ioh. 18¹³ ‘Tu dicis quia rex sum ego: nam ego¹⁴ in hoc natus sum.’ Cum ergo per mortem non fuit intercisio regni sui, cum fuit spirituale [p. 30] a corpore independens; ergo mansit perpetuo. Confirmatur: quia Daniel 7 scribitur de humanitate Christi: ‘potestas eius eterna que non auferetur,¹ et regnum eius quod non corrumpetur.’ Non ergo potest ille Dominus eciam² secundum humanitatem perdere regnum suum: quia Michee 4^to. dicitur, ‘regnabit super eos ex hoc³ nunc et usque in seculum.’ Et patet⁴ ex hoc quod non est⁵ alia regnacio hominis interioris⁵ in Christo nisi que est⁶ regnacio Christi Dei: sed anima regnavit a principio sue creacionis continue: ergo Christus. Non enim dictus rex Israel secundum humanitatem ut corporaliter regnet sed spiritualiter super illos: ut exponit Augustinus Ome. 50⁷ exponens illud⁷ Ioh. 12 ‘Benedictus qui venit in nomine Domini, rex Israel.’ ‘Non enim,⁸ inquit Augustinus, rex Israel fuit Christus ad exigendum tributum vel ferro exercitum armandum hostesque⁹ visibiliter debellandos; sed rex Israel, quod mentes regat, quod in eternum consulat, quod in regnum celorum credentes sperantes amantesque perducat.’ Cum ergo illa¹⁰ potestas spiritualis, existens subiective in anima independenter¹¹ a corpore, non fuit spoliata ab anima Christi in morte; videtur quod eciam¹² in morte fuit humanitas vel spiritus¹³ Christi rex Israel¹⁴ sicut ante.

But in truth this [fiction]{g:ficticia} outdoes the faithlessness of the Jews.[^7] For the Jewish crowd, John 12, said to Jesus: "We have heard out of the law that Christ abideth for ever": because in Isaiah 9 it is written: "he shall sit upon the throne of David, and upon his kingdom; to establish it and strengthen it from henceforth and for ever,"[^8] that is, from the time at which "a child is born to us." It is proved thus. Jesus was born a king humanly, as is clear from John 18: "Thou sayest that I am a king: for I was born for this."[^9] Since therefore through death there was no interruption of his [kingdom]{g:regnum}, since it was spiritual, [30] independent of the body, therefore it remained perpetually. This is confirmed: for in Daniel 7 it is written of the humanity of Christ: "his [power]{g:potestas} is eternal, which shall not be taken away: and his kingdom that shall not be destroyed." Therefore that Lord cannot, even as to the humanity, lose his kingdom: for in Micah 4 it is said, "he will reign over them from this time now and for ever."[^10] And it is clear from this that there is no other [reigning]{g:regnacio} of the inner man in Christ but that which is the reigning of Christ God: now the soul reigned continuously from the beginning of its creation: therefore Christ [reigned]. For he is not called king of Israel as to the humanity so that he reigns bodily, but [so that he reigns] spiritually over them: as Augustine expounds in Homily 50,[^11] expounding that [text] of John 12, "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord, the king of Israel." "For Christ," says Augustine, "was not king of Israel for exacting tribute, or arming an army with the sword and subduing enemies visibly; but king of Israel because he rules minds, because he counsels for eternity, because he leads into the kingdom of heaven those who believe, hope and love." Since therefore that spiritual power, existing in the soul as in its [subject]{g:subiectum}[^12] independently of the body, was not stripped from the soul of Christ in death, it seems that even in death the humanity or spirit of Christ was king of Israel as before.

## ¶5 · 30.23–31.1

> [p. 30] Unde et illud Psi. 23 ‘attollite portas etc.’¹⁵ exponitur a beato Augustino et Ieronimo de anima Christi descendente ad inferos: ac si angeli preconizantes¹⁶ Christi¹⁷ descensum¹⁸ ad inferos dicerent principibus tenebrarum; ‘O principes mundanorum! attollite¹⁹ portas vestras, et introibit rex glorie cum captivitate ducta ab inferis, portas aperiens²⁰ eternales.’ Et sic in scripturis dirigitur liberacio patrum ab inferis ad personam Christi, quod ipse humanitus immediate ipsam perficeret.²¹ Ut Luce 1°. ‘Illuminare his²² qui in tenebris et in umbra mortis sedent.’ Quod,²³ cum sequitur de [p. 31] virtute sermonis ex dicto principio, est catholice concedendum.

Hence also that [verse] of Psalm 23, "Lift up your gates etc.," is expounded by blessed Augustine and Jerome of the soul of Christ descending into hell:[^13] as if the angels, heralding Christ's descent into hell, said to the princes of darkness: "O princes of worldly men! lift up your gates, and the king of glory shall enter in with the captivity led out of hell, opening the eternal gates." And so in the scriptures the delivering of the fathers from hell is directed to the person of Christ, [meaning] that he himself, humanly and [without intermediary]{g:immediate}, would accomplish it.[^14] As in Luke 1: "To enlighten them that sit in darkness and in the shadow of death." Which, since it follows [31] in the proper sense of the words from the said principle, is to be granted catholicly.

## ¶6 · 31.3–32.2

> [p. 31] Item nemo sapiens et plene potens incipit opus laudabile in propria persona perficere; et, dum perfecerit¹ in propria persona rudimenta² operis,³ committit suo subdito operis complementum. Cum ergo edificacio ecclesie et liberacio patrum⁴ ab inferis sit opus summe laudabile; sequitur quod Christus in propria persona non perficiet imperfectam disposicionem, et committet spiritui creato ypostatice separato belli victoriam, que est operis complementum. Indignius namque⁵ est temptari a dyabolo, pati, et corporaliter disponere ad edificacionem ecclesie, quam⁶ potenter spoliare Tartara⁷ a dyabolo devicto. Si ergo Christus in propria persona passus fuerat et temptatus, quia in carne et⁸ anima ypostatice copulata: per idem in propria persona spoliavit infernum⁹ extrahendo patriarchas ab inferis, specialiter cum non reliquit unionem anime vel corporis quod assumpsit: dicente Augustino Ome. 47. ‘Si enim dixerimus quod Verbum Dei posuit animam suam et iterum sumpsit eam, metuendum est, ne subintret¹⁰ prava cogitacio et dicatur :—Ergo¹¹ aliquando anima illa separata est a Verbo, et aliquando Verbum illud, ex quo suscepit illam animam¹² fuit sine anima.—Video enim fuisse sine anima humana Verbum cum “in principio erat Verbum;”¹³ sed ex quo “Verbum¹³ caro factum est et habitavit in nobis;” et susceptus est¹⁴ a Verbo totus homo, id est,¹⁵ anima et caro. Quid fecit passio, quid fecit mors, nisi corpus ab anima separavit, animam vero a Verbo non separavit?’ Et sequitur. ‘Fidelis latronis animam¹⁶ non deserebat? et deserebat¹⁷ suam? Absit.’ Et quia minus intelligens potest¹⁸ ex dictis intelligere quod Verbum solum per graciam affuit illi anime et non hypostatica¹⁹ unione [p. 32] subdit¹ differenciam: ‘latronis vero¹ animam custodivit, suam vero inseparabiliter habuit.’²

Again, no one who is wise and fully able begins to accomplish a praiseworthy work in his own person and, when he has accomplished the rudiments of the work in his own person, commits the completion of the work to his subordinate. Since therefore the building of the [church]{g:ecclesia} and the delivering of the fathers from hell is a supremely praiseworthy work, it follows that Christ will not accomplish the imperfect disposing [of it] in his own person and commit to a [created]{g:creatus} spirit, hypostatically separated, the victory of the battle, which is the completion of the work. For it is more unworthy to be tempted by the devil, to suffer, and bodily to dispose [things] for the building of the church, than mightily to [despoil]{g:spoliare} Tartarus, the devil being vanquished.[^15] If therefore Christ had suffered and been tempted in his own person, because [he did so] in flesh and soul hypostatically joined, by the same token in his own person he despoiled hell by drawing the patriarchs out of hell, especially since he did not relinquish the [union]{g:unio} of the soul or of the body that he [assumed]{g:assumere}: as Augustine says in Homily 47:[^16] "For if we say that [the Word]{g:verbum} of God laid down his soul and took it again, it is to be feared lest a wicked thought creep in and it be said: Then at some time that soul was separated from the Word, and at some time that Word, after he took up that soul, was without a soul. For I see that the Word was without a human soul when 'in the beginning was the Word'; but from the time that 'the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us,' and the whole man was taken up by the Word, that is, soul and flesh, what did the passion do, what did death do, but separate the body from the soul? but the soul it did not separate from the Word." And it follows: "He did not forsake the soul of the faithful thief: and did he forsake his own? Far be it." And because one of less understanding can understand from what has been said that the Word was present to that soul only by grace and not by hypostatic union, [32] he adds the difference: "the thief's soul he guarded, but his own he held inseparably."

## ¶7 · 32.3–32.14

> [p. 32] Et potest illud confirmari ex hoc, quod aliter patres in limbo³ tantum tenerentur creature, que non est Deus, spoliando³ eos de manu dyaboli, sicut Christo secundum humanitatem: quod est impossibile. Cum ergo persone debetur accio, anima fuit tunc anima persone agentis.⁴ Unde sicut anima Christi non fuit⁵ spoliata gracia per mortem, et per consequens non⁶ gracia unionis; sic non fuit spoliata potestate spirituali regia, caractere,⁷ vel sacerdocio, quod est qualitas indelebilis secundum doctores. Unde sanctus Thomas concedit⁸ quod Christus fuit sacerdos secundum animam in triduo iuxta textum Apostoli ad Hebre 8. ‘Iesus autem eo quod maneat⁹ in eternum sempiternum habet sacerdocium.’

And this can be confirmed from this, that otherwise the fathers in [limbo]{g:limbus} would be beholden only to a [creature]{g:creatura}, which is not God, for [despoiling]{g:spoliacio} them out of the hand of the devil, as [they are beholden] to Christ as to the humanity: which is impossible.[^17] Since therefore action is owed to the person, the soul was then the soul of the person acting.[^18] Hence, just as the soul of Christ was not stripped of grace by death, and consequently not of [the grace of union]{g:gracia-unionis}, so it was not stripped of the spiritual royal power, the [character]{g:caracter}, or the priesthood, which is an indelible quality according to the doctors. Hence saint Thomas grants that Christ was a priest as to the soul in the three days, according to the text of the Apostle to the Hebrews 8: "But Jesus, for that he continueth for ever, hath an everlasting priesthood."[^19]

## ¶8 · 32.15–32.24

> [p. 32] Et revera non sapit¹⁰ quod illa anima sit rex et sacerdos, nisi sit persona; quia actus huius officii sint signantissime personales. Et Doctor Subtilis vere dicit quod Christus mansit Christus in illo triduo. Sicut ergo nichil quod non est Deus sufficit genus humanum redimere, sic nichil quod non est Deus suffecit tam potenter de manu inimici patres eripere. Sed si hoc posset¹¹ in virtute Dei creatura ab eo ypostatice separata; per idem posset passio vel meritum cuiuscunque hominis a Deo personaliter seiuncti auctoritate Dei pro quocunque peccato hominis facere recompensam.

And in truth it makes no sense that that soul is king and priest unless it is a person; because the acts of this office are most markedly personal. And the Subtle Doctor truly says that Christ remained Christ in those three days.[^20] Just as therefore nothing that is not God suffices to redeem the human race, so nothing that is not God sufficed so mightily to snatch the fathers out of the hand of the enemy. But if a creature hypostatically separated from him could do this by the [power]{g:virtus} of God, by the same token the passion or merit of any man whatever, personally disjoined from God, could by the [authority]{g:auctoritas} of God make recompense for any sin of man whatever.

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** 28.25 *de virtute sermonis*: A B read *de vi sermonis*, C *de Christo sermonis*. The print's formula is kept apart in this edition from *de vi vocis* "by the force of the words" (29.20 and below).
[^2]: **T.** 29.2 *triplici medio*: so A B C O (*3ci*); Harris asks whether *modo* should be read for *medio* ("in a threefold way"). The print is rendered, *medium* as the middle term of an argument.
[^3]: **C.** 29.9 *allegatur ex fide*: the verb governs *ex*, so "from faith", not the formula "as faith teaches".
[^4]: **T.** 29.13 *servata*: A B C *incarnata* ("then it was incarnate at that time, descending into hell"). *Descendens* is in apposition: the humanity, kept in being, is what descended.
[^5]: **T.** 29.15 *pars denominacionis*: all the manuscripts read *partis*, against the print's *pars* (Harris's), and O *denominandum* for *denominacionis*. The English keeps the print: with *partis* the gerundive *attribuenda* has no subject, so the manuscripts' text will not construe unless *denominacionis* is also changed (to *denominacio*, "the naming of a part"), which no manuscript gives; the sense is the same either way. Literally "a part of denomination": the person is not named from a part (body or soul) for a time when it is not his part.
[^6]: **C.** 29.19 *quibus non sapit*: literally "to whom it does not savour"; so at 32.15 *non sapit*.
[^7]: **C.** 29.25 *superaddit perfidiam Iudeorum*: literally "adds over and above to the faithlessness of the Jews". The Jews at least held that "Christ abideth for ever" (John 12:34).
[^8]: **S.** Isa. 9:7, without the Vulgate's *in iudicio et iustitia* ("with judgment and with justice"). *A modo* is O's (*a m°*); A B C *año*. "A child is born to us" is Isa. 9:6.
[^9]: **S.** John 18:37: the Vulgate has no *nam* (DR "Thou sayest that I am a king. For this was I born").
[^10]: **S.** Dan. 7:14: the Vulgate repeats *potestas* (DR "his power is an everlasting power that shall not be taken away"). Mic. 4:7: the Vulgate has *regnabit Dominus super eos in monte Sion ex hoc nunc et usque in aeternum* (DR "the Lord will reign over them in Mount Sion, from this time now and forever").
[^11]: **S.** Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 51.4, on John 12:13 (Wyclif's "Homily 50"), PL 35, 1765 (*verified*, LEDGER I03a-1); Wyclif's number is one below the received one. Augustine has *exercitum ferro armandum* for Wyclif's *ferro exercitum armandum*.
[^12]: **C.** 30.19 *existens subiective in anima*: the soul is the subject in which the power inheres, so the power does not depend on the body.
[^13]: **S.** Ps. 23:7 (Hebrew 24:7). Augustine's *Enarratio* on Ps. 23 takes the verse otherwise (v. 7 of renouncing the world, v. 9 of the ascension); the descent reading is perhaps from the *Sermo* 377 printed among his doubtful works, In Ascensione Domini, PL 39, 1672, where the gates are opened twice, *portae inferorum et coeli*, and *Magnus ergo iste rex gloriae, etiam apud inferos* (Wyclif names no work; LEDGER I03a-2). The Jerome is the pseudo-Jerome *Breviarium in Psalmos* on Ps. 23, PL 26, 887B–889A: *Vox angelorum bonorum, ad angelos malos*, and *Vox angelorum, qui cum Christo ad infernum descenderunt, ad subjectos diaboli dicunt* (*verified*, LEDGER I03a-3); at 887D it has *cum spoliato Tartaro, reduxit secum multitudinem captivorum*, cf. 31.12 *spoliare Tartara*. The angels' cry is a paraphrase built on the verse, not its text.
[^14]: **C.** 30.30 *immediate*: without an intermediary, not "at once": the work is the person's own, not a created agent's (the argument of the next paragraph). Luke 1:79 follows.
[^15]: **C.** 31.12 *spoliare Tartara a dyabolo devicto*: taken as an ablative absolute; it may also be "to wrest Tartarus from the vanquished devil". A B C read *creaturam* for *Tartara*.
[^16]: **S.** Augustine, *In Ioh. tract.* 47.10, on John 10:18 ("I have power to lay it down: and I have power to take it up again"), PL 35, 1738 (*verified*, LEDGER I03a-4); the last clause is Wyclif's paraphrase of *illius ut Dominus custodivit, suam vero inseparabiliter habuit*. Within it, John 1:1 and 1:14.
[^17]: **T.** 32 *tantum tenerentur creature ... spoliando*: Harris marks the place *locus corruptus* and queries *tenerentur, tantum creatura ... spoliante* ("would be beholden, a creature only ... despoiling them"). The print is rendered; the sense is the same.
[^18]: **T.** 32.7 *anima persone agentis*: A B C *persona agens* ("the soul was then the person acting"), which the next paragraph's argument (*nisi sit persona*) treats as absurd.
[^19]: **S.** Saint Thomas, *Scriptum super Sententiis* III d. 22 q. 1 a. 1 ad 4: *illud sacerdotium est etiam animae Christi separatae a corpore* (*verified*, LEDGER I03a-5). In the same article Thomas holds that Christ was not a man in the three days. Thomas concludes against Wyclif's thesis; Wyclif takes only a concession from him. The text is Heb. 7:24 (Wyclif "8"); the Vulgate has *hic* for *Iesus* (DR "But this"). *Maneat* is the Vulgate's; A B C *manet*.
[^20]: **S.** The Subtle Doctor is Duns Scotus; *Ordinatio* III d. 22 q. un. Scotus holds that Christ was not a man in the three days, but to the argument *Christus in triduo fuit Christus: igitur … homo* he answers *nego consequentiam*, letting the antecedent stand (*verified*, LEDGER I03a-6). Scotus too concludes against Wyclif's thesis; Wyclif takes only the concession.
