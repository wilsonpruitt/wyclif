## Doubtful, left as they are

- **09-g ¶1, p. 215**: *maximus hereticus … sit papa, sed et rudissimus laycus vel femella* is rendered "and even the rudest layman". This is an intensive *sed et* ("nay, even"), so none of the classes fits cleanly, and "but even" would read as a contrast. The English "and even" carries it. (Compare *sed precipue*, *De ecclesia* 01-d.)
- **09-h ¶2, p. 221**: *Locus autem sanctus videtur michi esse status sanctus* is rendered "Now". It is either (a), the step that applies the Matthew 24 text to the papacy, or (c), a gloss on the quotation. "Now" is defensible.
- **10-b ¶1, p. 223**: *A tempore autem, quo dimisso papali officio mundo intenderant* is rendered "And from the time". It is either (b), the later worldly popes set against the early popes under whom the church grew, or (c), the next point of the reply. Both readings stand.
- **10-b ¶1, p. 226**: *Sed cum regnum Anglie non subiacet Romano imperio, videtur quod non iure … cesaris* is rendered "But since". It could be (a), the minor premiss against "he must claim it by Caesar's right", but the conclusion sits inside the sentence and the *sed* overturns that claim. "But" is defensible.
- **10-b ¶1, p. 228**: *Obediendo autem homini secundum racionem falsam … est utrumque peccatum dampnabile* is rendered "And to obey". It is either (b), the false obedience set against the two right ways of obeying just described ("But to obey"), or (c), the next case. This one is a real toss-up.
- **10-c ¶2, p. 231**: *rex autem et regnum ex scitu pape perante decreverant* is rendered "And the king and the kingdom". It is (c), background to the narrative, or possibly (b), set against the king's request. "And" stands.
- **09-h ¶1, p. 221** (changed above): *Christus autem* could also be read as (c), "And Christ". "But" was chosen because the contrast between *reptans* and *ascendit/ascendamus* is explicit.
