## Doubtful, left as they are

- **04-e, p. 99**: *Levis **autem** fundacio valet ad hoc quod Christus dedit singulariter illud officium suis apostolis*, rendered "And a light foundation serves for this". Read as (c): it carries on from the church's reasonable ordinance to the ground that is enough to support it, and the next clause (the apostles stood as a type of priests only) backs that ground. A contrast reading (b, "But the foundation for this is light") is possible if *levis* is taken as a criticism of the usual argument. Left.
- **04-e, p. 99**: *non oportuit inmiscere aquam **sed** simul benedicere aquam et vinum*, rendered "there was no need to mingle water in, but only to bless water and wine together". *Non … sed* is correctly "but"; the added "only" has no word behind it in the Latin (*simul* is already "together"). It does not affect the connective. Left.
- **04-e, p. 102**: *Utrum **autem** Greci vere conficiunt … est dubium apud multos*, rendered "But whether the Greeks truly consecrate". This moves from the fitness of our use to the doubtful validity of theirs. That could be (c) "And whether", but the shift from "ours is more fitting" to "theirs is in doubt" makes (b) defensible. Left.
- **04-g, p. 107**: *Ego **autem** primum abnuens tanquam inutile*, rendered "But I, refusing the first as useless". Wyclif sets his own choice against the "masterly work" of reconciling the two teachings: (b). "And I, for my part" (c) would also be defensible. Left.
- **04-e, p. 101 / 04-g, p. 108**: ***Sed** redeundo*, rendered "But, to return". This is a resumptive turn back to the main question after a digression, not a premiss, so it is not (a). "But" works as the transition, as with *Sed pro declaracione* in wave 8. Left.
