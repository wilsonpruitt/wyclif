# *De eucharistia*, chapter 7, part 5: whether the body of Christ is seen with the bodily eye; bodily, imaginary and intellectual vision

## ¶1 · 205.1–205.5

> [p. 205] Ideo oportet primo sopire istam stulticiam qua creditur quod omne ens in quantum adquirit sibi accidens movetur. Oportet enim omnem motum subiectari in substancia mota per acquisicionem vel deperdicionem false quantitatis aut qualitatis.

Therefore one must first lay to rest this folly by which it is believed that every [being]{g:ens}, insofar as it acquires an [accident]{g:accidens} for itself, is moved. For every motion must have its subject in the [substance]{g:substancia} moved, through the acquisition or loss of false [quantity]{g:quantitas} or quality.[^1]

## ¶2 · 205.6–205.10

> [p. 205] Secundo oportet distinguere inter denominaciones respectivas et subiectivas, inter denominacionem efficientem et formalem ut albedinem que denominat formaliter Petrum album denominat efficienter Paulum quantumcunque distantem esse Petro similem.

Secondly one must distinguish between relational and subjective [namings]{g:denominacio}, between efficient and formal naming: as the whiteness which formally names Peter white efficiently names Paul, however distant, to be like Peter.

## ¶3 · 205.11–205.20

> [p. 205] Et tercio oportet notare divisiones et descripciones predictas ad tollendum sophismata et errores. Ad tantum enim desipiunt quidam magni, quod credunt accidens denominare subiectum quantumcunque extrinsecum sine aliqua denominacione accidentis eciam respectivi in illo sic denominato, ut exemplificat de visione, de tempore, de motu. Sed illi parum ponderant visionem passivam que est videri predicamentum ubi et quando causatum a tempore cum denominacione formali et effectuali.

And thirdly one must note the divisions and [definitions]{g:descripcio} given above, [to dispose of]{g:tollere} [sophisms]{g:sophisma} and errors. For certain great men are so far out of their wits that they believe that an accident names a subject however extrinsic, without any naming by an accident, even a [relational]{g:respectivus} one, in the thing so named, as he exemplifies of vision, of time, of motion. But they weigh too little passive vision, which is "being seen", the [category]{g:predicamentum} *where* and *when* caused by time, with formal and effectual naming.[^2]

## ¶4 · 205.21–206.2

> [p. 205] Sed redeundo replicatur (ut tactum est) quod homo videndo quodcunque videt Deum oculo corporali, quia omne sensibile vel insensibile ipsum significat. Et confirmatur ex testimonio scripture multiplicis, nam Genes. XXXII, 30 dicitur quod Jacob vidit Dominum facie ad faciem; et Ysaie VI, 1 dicit quod vidit Dominum sedentem super solium excelsum et elevatum. Et Joh. VIIIᵛᵒ, 56 dicit Christus quod Abraham pater Iudeorum exultavit, ut videret diem Messie, vidit et gavisus est; et sic de multis similibus. Si ergo homo, potest tractare et tangere corpus Christi in hostia, [p. 206] multo magis videre potes ipsum ibidem, cum visus sit sensus subtilissimus et supremus.

But, to return, [it is rejoined]{g:replicatur} (as was touched on) that a man, in seeing anything whatever, sees God with the bodily eye, because everything [perceptible]{g:sensibilis} or [imperceptible]{g:insensibilis} signifies him. And this is confirmed from the testimony of manifold scripture: for in Gen. 32:30 it is said that Jacob "saw the Lord face to face"; and Isaiah 6:1 says that he "saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high and elevated". And in John 8:56 Christ says that "Abraham, the father of the Jews, rejoiced that he might see the day of the Messiah: he saw it and was glad"; and so of many like [texts].[^3] If therefore, O man, one can handle and touch [the body of Christ]{g:corpus-christi} in the [host]{g:hostia}, [206] much more can you see it there, since sight is the subtlest and highest sense.[^4]

## ¶5 · 206.3–207.10

> [p. 206] Hic oportet cognoscere equivocaciones pro veritatis scripture misteriis cognoscendis. Est enim visio corporalis, visio ymaginaria et visio intellectualis. Visio corporalis est qua vigil sentit visibile secundum figuram forme visibilis in organo sensus visus. Unde ut loquar clarius in ista materia exprimo quod nemo videt ibidem corpus Christi oculo corporali, quod debet fidelibus esse certum. Unde Doctor Sollempnis primo quodlibeto, questione VIᵗᵃ, dubitando si fidelis videt corpus Christi in hostia oculo corporali declarat quod non, eciam quod oculus Christi in hostia non videt se ibidem propter limitacionis carenciam tam in visibili quam in specie visibilis quam in virtute visiva. Et idem dicit lex ecclesie satis sepe, ut III Decretalium, De Celebracione missarum, capitulo Cum Marthe dicitur quod forma visibilis panis et vini est sacramentum sed non res sacramenti; et aliud est quod creditur quam cernitur, quia bene corpus Christi creditur et cernitur eius species que est solummodo sacramentum. Unde mirantur multi qua fronte infundabiliter quis audet dicere quod videt corpus Christi in hostia oculo corporali. Non enim sequitur: Petrus intellectu vel fide videt Deum in hostia per corporalem oculum, quia per eius ministerium, ergo videt Deum ibidem oculo corporali. Sic enim arguunt sophiste quod homo videt per talum, per digitos et [p. 207] cetera organa inpertinencia visui Deum et omne sensibile vel insensibile, quia illa sunt in casu instrumenta movencia, ut mente concipiat noscibile quod absconditum est ab omni materiali veritate; sed vident quod ista est argucia deridenda, cum sic dicto: Petrus videt hoc oculo corporali, notatur quod oculus corporalis informatur virtute visiva apprehendente hoc per eius speciem naturalem, quomodo solum color, lux vel eius subiectum aut horum privacio videtur, sicut patet philosopho noscenti limites visionis.

Here one must know the [equivocations]{g:equivocacio}, in order to know the [mysteries]{g:misterium} of the truth of scripture. For there is [bodily vision]{g:visio-corporalis}, imaginary vision and intellectual vision. Bodily vision is that by which one awake [perceives]{g:sentire} a visible thing according to the figure of the visible form in the organ of the sense of sight. Hence, to speak more clearly in this matter, I state that no one sees the body of Christ there with the bodily eye, which ought to be certain to the faithful. Hence the Solemn Doctor, *Quodlibet* I, question 6, raising the doubt whether a believer sees the body of Christ in the host with the bodily eye, [makes clear]{g:declarare} that he does not; even that the eye of Christ in the host does not see itself there, for lack of limitation, as much in the visible thing as in the species of the visible as in the visual [power]{g:virtus}.[^5] And the law of the [church]{g:ecclesia} says the same often enough: as in *Decretals* III, *De celebratione missarum*, the chapter *Cum Marthe*, it is said that "the visible form of [bread]{g:panis} and [wine]{g:vinum} is the [sacrament]{g:sacramentum} but not the [reality of the sacrament]{g:res-sacramenti}; and what is believed is other than what is seen, for the body of Christ is indeed believed, and its [species]{g:species}, which is only the sacrament, is seen".[^6] Hence many wonder with what face anyone dares [without foundation]{g:infundabiliter} to say that he sees the body of Christ in the host with the bodily eye. For [it does not follow]{g:sequitur}: "Peter by understanding or by faith sees God in the host through the bodily eye, because through its ministry; therefore he sees God there with the bodily eye." For so the [sophists]{g:sophista} argue that a man sees God and everything perceptible or imperceptible through the ankle, through the fingers and [207] the other organs [irrelevant]{g:impertinens} to sight, because these are, in the case [put], moving instruments, so that he conceives in mind the knowable which is hidden from all material truth. But they see that this is a [quibble]{g:argucia} to be laughed at, since, when it is said thus, "Peter sees this with the bodily eye", it is signified that the bodily eye is [informed]{g:informare} by the visual power apprehending this through its natural species, in the way in which only colour, light, or its subject, or the privation of these, is seen, as is clear to the philosopher who knows the [limits]{g:limites} of vision.[^7]

## ¶6 · 207.11–207.18

> [p. 207] Secunda maneries visionis vocatur visio ymaginaria que est visio qua secundum virtutem ymaginativam est visibile formaliter apprehensum; et differt a visione priori in tribus: Primo in hoc quod omnis visio corporalis sive per rectam intuicionem sive per reflexionem sive per refraccionem fit per limitacionem speciei presentis visibilis in organo sensus visus; non sic autem visio ymaginaria, cum sepe fit in obiecti absencia.

The second [kind]{g:maneries} of vision is called imaginary vision, which is the vision by which the visible is formally apprehended according to the imaginative power; and it differs from the former vision in three ways. First in this, that every bodily vision, whether by direct intuition or by reflection or by refraction, comes about through the limitation of the species of the present visible thing in the organ of the sense of sight; but not so imaginary vision, since it often comes about in the absence of the object.

## ¶7 · 207.20–207.28

> [p. 207] Secunda differencia est quod visio corporalis solum inest videnti dum vigilat, sed visio ymaginaria inest tam dormienti quam vigili, nam in sompnis fiunt quecunque sompnia in visione ymaginaria, sicut multe prophecie inperfecte fiunt in vigili per misterium visionis ymaginarie, ut patet in Daniele qui tam vigil quam dormiens vidit visione ymaginaria sompnium Nabuchodonosor et per hoc vidit prophetice ultra regem interpretacionem sompnii, ut patet Daniel. IIᵒ.

The second [difference]{g:differencia} is that bodily vision is in the one seeing only while he is awake, but imaginary vision is in one asleep as much as in one awake: for in sleep all dreams whatever come about in imaginary vision, just as many imperfect prophecies come about in one awake through the mystery of imaginary vision, as is clear in Daniel, who, as much awake as asleep, saw by imaginary vision the dream of Nebuchadnezzar, and by this saw prophetically, beyond the king, the interpretation of the dream, as is clear in Dan. 2.[^8]

## ¶8 · 207.29–208.28

> [p. 207] Tercio differunt in hoc quod visio ymaginaria est tam quoad obiectum quam tempus visione corporali extensior. Nemo enim videt corporaliter nisi colorem aut lucem presentem vel subiectum aut eorum privacionem, [p. 208] sed homo videt ymaginarie illa, que non habent existenciam, ut montem aureum et multa ymaginabilia que nunquam existunt. Sepe eciam videt homo ymaginarie pro dando signo temporis illa que tunc non existunt sed post vel ante; primo modo legitur Act. Xᵒ, 11 12, quomodo Petrus vidit in mentis excessu celum apertum et descendens vas quoddam velud lintheum quatuor iniciis submitti de celo in terram in quo erant omnia quadrupedia et serpencia terre et volatilia celi; que scriptura est verissima ad sensum positum. Secundo modo loquitur scriptura Act. IX, 11 quod Ananias ex mandato Domini alloqueretur Paulum: Vade, inquit, in vicum qui vocatur Rectus et quere in domo Jude Saulum nomine Tharsensem. Ecce enim orat. Et vidit virum Ananiam nomine introeuntem et inponentem sibi manus ut visum recipiat. Ecce quod Paulus vidit ymaginarie futurum factum Ananie, ut videtur probabiliter ex textu. Et ista visione vidit Heliseus Gesi valde distantem localiter, quomodo recepit simoniace a Naaman munera propter graciam spiritualem quam Deus contulit Heliseo (ut patet IVᵒ Reg. Vᵒ). Et ita videtur quod triplex sit visio ymaginaria, sicut triplex est visio corporalis, scilicet visio ymaginabilis non existentis preteriti vel futuri, visio ymaginabilis non existentis sed visui possibilis, et visio cuiuscunque estimabilis sicut apprehensio estimative fantastice vel memorative organice.

Thirdly they differ in this, that imaginary vision is more extensive than bodily vision as much as to object as to time. For no one sees [bodily]{g:corporaliter} anything but present colour or light, or the subject, or their privation; [208] but a man sees imaginarily those things which have no existence, such as a golden mountain and many imaginable things which never exist. Often, too, a man sees imaginarily, for the giving of a sign of time, those things which do not then exist but [exist] afterwards or before.[^9] Of the first mode we read in Acts 10:11–12 how Peter "saw" in an ecstasy of mind "the heaven opened and a certain vessel descending, as it were a linen sheet let down by the four corners from heaven to the earth: wherein were all manner of four-footed beasts and creeping things of the earth and fowls of the air"; which scripture is most true in the sense laid down.[^10] Of the second mode scripture speaks in Acts 9:11, of how Ananias at the Lord's command was to speak to Paul: "Go," he says, "into the street that is called Strait and seek in the house of Judas, one named Saul of Tarsus. For behold he prayeth. And he saw a man named Ananias coming in and putting his hands upon him, that he might receive his sight."[^11] See: Paul saw imaginarily the future deed of Ananias, as appears [with probability]{g:probabiliter} from the text. And by this vision Elisha saw Gehazi, far distant [in place]{g:localiter}, how he received gifts simoniacally from Naaman, on account of the spiritual grace which God conferred on Elisha (as is clear in 4 Kings 5).[^12] And so it seems that imaginary vision is threefold, as bodily vision is threefold: namely, vision of an imaginable thing not existing, past or future; vision of an imaginable thing not existing but possible to sight; and vision of any estimable thing whatever, such as the apprehension of the estimative, the [phantastic]{g:fantasticus} or the memorative organic [power].[^13]

## ¶9 · 208.29–209.3

> [p. 208] Sed tercia est visio intellectualis, qua primo intellectu videtur visibile; et est in beatis pura sine visione inferiori corporali vel tardante ymaginaria. In nobis [p. 209] autem sunt iste tres visionum species comitantes, ut simul video hiis tribus virtutibus sacramentum sensibile triplici visione.

But the third is intellectual vision, by which the visible is seen first by the understanding; and in the blessed it is pure, without lower bodily vision or a retarding imaginary [vision].[^14] But in us [209] these three species of vision accompany one another, as I see at once with these three powers the perceptible sacrament by a threefold vision.

## ¶10 · 209.4–209.23

> [p. 209] Est autem triplex visio intellectualis, scilicet intuitiva, abstractiva et enigmatica. Prima visione vident Deum beati in patria, sicut vident angelos et sensibilia, et cum actus specificantur ab obiectis, ponitur quod iste sunt tres species intuicionum, sicut aliter intuetur beatus sensibile et aliter viator. Intuicio autem intellectualis habet hanc racionem quod per se causatur ex capacitate intellectus et presencia rei vise, ut posito visibili cum sua applicacione que exprimitur per presenciam et sufficienti capacitate intellectus consequitur intuicio. Sed sic non est de visione abstractiva, quia ad illam requiritur discursus racionis, sicut philosophi naturaliter noverunt Deum ex effectibus (ut dicit Apostolus Rom. Iᵒ). Et illam dicunt doctores requiri ad actuale meritum viantis, et hanc neminem posse mereri in primo instanti in quo incipit preter Christum. Oportet enim tale meritum procedere a voluntate libere mota per racionem ad meritorie operandum quod requirit tempus in quolibet alio preter Christum.

And intellectual vision is threefold, namely intuitive, [abstractive]{g:abstractive} and enigmatic. By the first vision the blessed see God in [the homeland]{g:patria}, as they see angels and perceptible things; and since acts are specified by their objects, it is posited that these are three species of intuitions, just as the blessed man intuits a perceptible thing in one way and the [wayfarer]{g:viator} in another. And intellectual intuition has this account: that it is caused [of itself]{g:per-se} by the capacity of the intellect and the presence of the thing seen, so that, when the visible is posited with its application, which is expressed by "presence", and a sufficient capacity of the intellect, intuition follows. But it is not so with abstractive vision, for to it the discourse of reason is required, as the philosophers by nature knew God from his effects (as the Apostle says, Rom. 1[:20]).[^15] And this, the doctors say, is required for the actual merit of [one on the road]{g:viantes}, and that no one except Christ can merit this in the first instant in which he begins.[^16] For such merit must proceed from a will freely moved by reason to work meritoriously, which requires time in anyone else whatever except Christ.

## Loserth's side-notes (PD, verbatim; not our translation)

- 205.6: 2. We must distinguish between subjective and respective naming.
- 205.11: 3. We must keep these distinctions in mind to avoid error.
- 205.21: Our adversary's reply: Scripture shews that we see God in the host.
- 206.3: Three kinds of seeing: bodily, imaginative, intellectual.
- 206.11: I. Bodily. We cannot by bodily sight see the body of Christ.
- 206.20: We see the host with the eye; we see God there by faith; it does not follow that we see God with the eye.
- 207.11: 2. Imaginitive. This differs from bodily sight,
- 207.14: a) In applying to absent things;
- 207.20: 6) in acting during sleep;
- 207.29: c) in applying to things that do not exist,
- 208.3: or to things, past or future.
- 208.29: 3. Intellectual vision.
- 209.4: Three kinds of intellectual vision: a) Intuitive, as the blessed see God.
- 209.14: b) Abstractive, the result of reasoning; as the philosophers by nature know God.

## Notes

[^1]: **T.** *False quantitatis aut qualitatis* (205.5) stands on the plate and no manuscript is reported against it; "false" does not give a clear sense here and is translated as printed (a possible corruption, unresolved).

[^2]: **C.** "As he exemplifies": the verb is singular, one of the *quidam magni*, unnamed. The last sentence is elliptical: passive vision is "being seen", and the categories *where* and *when* are caused by time, both with formal and effectual naming; a word may be lost. D reads *videre*, C *predicatum*.

[^3]: **S.** Gen. 32:30 Vulgate: Jacob says *Vidi Deum facie ad faciem*; Wyclif has *Dominum*. John 8:56 Vulgate *Abraham pater vester … diem meum*; Wyclif's *pater Iudeorum … diem Messie* is translated. Isa. 6:1 agrees with the Vulgate.

[^4]: **C.** *Si ergo homo, potest … videre potes*: the print's comma makes *homo* a vocative, but the verb is third person (*potest*) and then second (*potes*). Translated as it stands.

[^5]: **S.** The Solemn Doctor is Henry of Ghent (Loserth, who cites Woodford against Wyclif in *Fasc. rer. expet.* fol. 97, and *De ecclesia* p. 317); *Quodl.* I q. 6 as Wyclif cites it (LEDGER K07e-1). *Species* here is the likeness of the visible thing in the medium and the eye, not the sacramental species.

[^6]: **S.** *Cum Marthe*: X 3.41.6 (Loserth: *Decr. Gregor.* lib. III, tit. XLI, cap. VI; the modern form is ours) (LEDGER K07e-2). Quotation bounds follow Loserth's italics. ABDE lack *De celebracione missarum*.

[^7]: **C.** *Per eius ministerium*: through the eye's service. The sophists' "Peter sees God through the bodily eye" names an instrument, not the manner of seeing; the reply turns on what *videt oculo corporali* means.

[^8]: **S.** Dan. 2:19, 2:28–45: the mystery shown to Daniel in a vision by night. DE read *vigile*; D *ministerium* for *misterium* ("through the ministry of imaginary vision").

[^9]: **C.** "The first mode" is the vision of what has no existence (the golden mountain); "the second mode" the vision of what exists before or after.

[^10]: **S.** Acts 10:10–12; Wyclif omits *magnum* after *linteum*. "In an ecstasy of mind" is 10:10.

[^11]: **C/S.** The words are the Lord's to Ananias (Acts 9:11–12), which Wyclif frames as the command by which Ananias was to address Paul; *inquit* has the Lord as its subject.

[^12]: **S.** 4 Kings 5:20–27 (2 Kings 5 in modern numbering): Elisha, "Was not my heart present …?" (5:26).

[^13]: **C.** *Estimative fantastice vel memorative organice*: genitives with *virtutis* understood, the three inner senses; "phantastic" renders *fantastice* as the faculty of *fantasia*, not "imaginary".

[^14]: **C.** *Primo intellectu*: "first" may go with the understanding ("by the first [power], the understanding") or be an adverb, "primarily". D *tarditate* for *tardante*.

[^15]: **S.** Rom. 1:20, "the invisible things of him … are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made" (Loserth's margin: Rom. I, 19, 20).

[^16]: **C.** *Illam … hanc*: *illam* is the abstractive vision; *hanc* may be the same or the intuitive vision as merit's reward. That Christ merited from the first instant of his conception is Thomas, *ST* III q. 34 a. 3 (*in primo instanti suae conceptionis Christus meruerit*, by the movement of his free will); that no one else can, since merit needs time, is Wyclif's report of the doctors (LEDGER K07e-3).
