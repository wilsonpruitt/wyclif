# *De benedicta incarnacione*, chapter 12, part 3: every animal assumed, the continuous giant, and Christ as his own parts

## ¶1 · 209.27–211.11

> [p. 209] Item posito quod quodlibet suppositum generis animalis servatis⁵ omnibus positivis sit Verbo ypostatice copulatum; patet quod non exinde periret genus, species, vel differencia animalis; et per consequens non omne animal foret Christus; ex quo per locum a sufficienti similitudine sequitur quod Verbum assumendo multas humanitates non foret solummodo unus homo. Assumptum patet eo quod Verbum non⁶ necessitatur ex unione ypostatica corrumpere aliquod positivum; [p. 210] et, cum genus, species, et differencia sint plurimum positiva, patet quod non ex assumpcione oportet illas exstinguere; et cum non sit genus in actu nisi habeat species et illa supposita, que specifice¹ distinguantur, patet ex casu possibili illis quos alloquor² de facto posito; consequens est quod sint multa individua³ distincta specifice; non ergo omne individuum³ de genere vivencium foret Christus; maiorem enim distinccionem oportet esse individuis quam in formis communibus, cum omnia inferiora in suis communioribus uniuntur. Nec potest dici quod manet genus animalis cum suis divisivis differenciis sicut prius, cum indifferenter omnis homo sit asinus, leo, capra, et sic de aliis, si Christus non sit multa animalia racione multarum animalitatum ypostatice coniunctarum. Ubi ergo foret genus distinctum a specie vel individuo secundum communioritatem, si idem individuum cuiuslibet generis animalis sit commune cuilibet animali? Ubi eciam tunc⁴ foret differencia inter racionale et irracionale, rudibile et irrudibile⁵ et ita de ceteris,⁶ si omne animal racionale sit animal irracionale, rudibile sit irrudibile, et ita de⁶ omnibus aliis⁷ differenciis assignandis? Omnes ergo termini per se in predicamento substancie nec predicarentur in quid, nec de rebus distinctis specifice cum omne animal sit omne animal, nec forma aliqua est sufficiens medium distinguendi unum suppositum vel naturaliter⁸ ab altero eo quod species permixte de se mutuo predicantur; et cum⁹ tam species differant⁹ quam individua speciei, videtur quod ydemptificatis speciebus individua ydemptificari poterunt. Nec valet dicere quod ex assumpcione huiusmodi species corrumpuntur, quia omnis species, que prius fuit, habet omnem singularem essenciam, quam prius habuit; et Verbum nedum est suppositum hominis sed bovis, leonis, et cuiuslibet animalis. Cum ergo sufficit ad existenciam [p. 211] speciei quod unum suppositum illius nature¹ existat,² sequitur quod manet omnis species, que perante. Non enim est hic fallacia accidentis—quicquid fuerit in principio huius³ hore est modo; species leonis est⁴ aliquid quod fuit in principio⁵ illius hore; ergo omnis talis species est modo—. Et cum⁶ nulla possunt nunc plus differre et nunc minus, manencia omnino immota; sequitur quod, sicut nature per ante differebant specifice, sic et modo; et per consequens modo est species. Confirmatur ex hoc quod omnis species est natura,⁷ omnis⁷ natura animalis, que prius fuit, modo est; ergo⁸ omnis species, que prius fuit, modo est.⁸

Again, supposing that every [supposit]{g:suppositum} of the genus animal, with all [its] positive [features] preserved, is joined [hypostatically]{g:ypostatice} to the [Word]{g:verbum}: it is clear that the genus, species or [difference]{g:differencia} of animal would not perish from that; and consequently not every animal would be Christ; from which, by [the topic from sufficient likeness]{g:locus-a-sufficienti-similitudine}, it follows that the Word, by assuming many [humanities]{g:humanitas}, would not be only one man.[^1] [The assumption]{g:assumptum} is clear in that the Word is not necessitated by the hypostatic [union]{g:unio} to corrupt anything positive; [210] and, since genus, species and difference are very much positive, it is clear that there is no need to extinguish them by the [assumption]{g:assumpcio}; and since there is no genus in act unless it has species and those supposits which are distinguished in species, it is clear from the case, possible for those whom I address, posited as fact: the [consequent]{g:consequens} is that there are many [individuals]{g:individuum} distinct in species; not every individual of the genus of living things, therefore, would be Christ; for there must be greater distinction in individuals than in common forms, since all lower things are united in their more common [ones].[^2] Nor can it be said that the genus animal remains with its dividing differences as before, since, indifferently, every man would be an ass, a lion, a goat, and so of the others, if Christ is not many animals by reason of many animalities hypostatically conjoined. Where, then, would the genus be, distinct from the species or the individual by [greater commonness]{g:communioritas}, if the same individual of every genus of animal is common to every animal? Where, too, would the difference then be between rational and irrational, able to bray and unable to bray, and so of the rest, if every rational animal is an irrational animal, the able-to-bray is the unable-to-bray, and so of all the other differences to be assigned?[^3] All the terms, therefore, that are [of themselves]{g:per-se} in the [category]{g:predicamentum} of substance would be predicated neither [*in quid*]{g:in-quid} nor of things distinct in species, since every animal would be every animal; nor is any form a sufficient [middle term]{g:medium} for distinguishing one supposit even naturally from another, in that the species are predicated of one another in a mixed way; and since species differ just as the individuals of a species do, it seems that, the species being [identified]{g:ydemptificare}, the individuals could be identified.[^4] Nor does it avail to say that by such an assumption the species are corrupted, because every species that was before has every singular [essence]{g:essencia} that it had before; and the Word is [not only]{g:nedum} the supposit of man but of ox, of lion, and of every animal. Since, therefore, it suffices for the existence [211] of a species that one supposit of that [nature]{g:natura} exist, it follows that every species remains that [was] before. For there is no [fallacy]{g:fallacia} of [accident]{g:accidens} here: "whatever was at the beginning of this hour is now; the species of lion is something that was at the beginning of that hour; therefore every such species is now."[^5] And since no things can differ now more and now less while remaining wholly unmoved, it follows that, as the natures differed in species before, so also now; and consequently species is now. This is confirmed from this, that every species is a nature; every nature of animal that was before is now; therefore every species that was before is now.[^6]

## ¶2 · 211.12–212.3

> [p. 211] Item Deus potest de omnibus hominibus per viam continuacionis facere unum compositum, sicut infinitum magnum et infinitum parvum potest Deus facere hominem. Quo posito⁹ videtur primo quod homo potest infinitum maiorari et alias infinitum minorari sine hoc quod quantitatem aliquam adquirat vel deperdat¹⁰; ut posito quod Verbum quotlibet humanitates seorsum assumat et omnes illas stante unione continuet, et¹¹ iterum discontinuet, secundum quod exigit conclusio probanda. Nam Christus fuit ante unionem homo medie quantitatis et post continuacionem erit monstrum grandissimum et deforme; et cum ex continuacione nulla quantitas secundum se totam adquiritur, sequitur propositum. Et idem contingit arguere de alteracione; ut potencia¹² naturali tam corporis quam anime tale quidem corpus multiplex sufficeret quantumlibet distanter sentire, et quantumlibet tarde¹³ moderando¹⁴ spacium longissimum¹⁵ in brevi¹⁴ describere. Cum ergo cuiuslibet partis illius corporis aliqua pars sit pars corporis Christi, sequitur quod hoc totum corpus sit corpus [p. 212] Christi; et per consequens Christus, postquam¹ fuit communis stature, iam est ita magnus sicut hoc corpus. Ex quo inferenda sunt alia.

Again, God can, by way of [continuation]{g:continuacio}, make one composite out of all men, just as God can make a man infinitely great and infinitely small. This being postulated, it seems, first, that a man can be infinitely increased and at another time infinitely diminished without acquiring or losing any quantity; as, supposing that the Word assume any number of humanities separately and, while the union stands, make them all continuous, and again discontinuous, according as the conclusion to be proved requires.[^7] For Christ was, before the union, a man of middling quantity, and after the continuation will be a most huge and misshapen monster; and since by continuation no quantity is acquired as a whole, the proposition follows. And the same can be argued of alteration: as, by the natural [power]{g:potencia} both of body and of soul, such a manifold body would suffice to sense however distantly, and, moderating however slowly, to describe a very long space in a short [time].[^8] Since, then, of each part of that body some part is a part of Christ's body, it follows that this whole body is the body [212] of Christ; and consequently Christ, after he was of common stature, is now as great as this body. From which other things are to be inferred.[^9]

## ¶3 · 212.4–213.14

> [p. 212] 2°. videtur sequi quod Christus sit quotlibet partes sue quantitative. Nam ante continuacionem Christus fuit compositum ex qualibet istarum animarum et suo corpore; ex quo sequitur quod Christus sit multe partes quantitative. Nec valet dicere quod natura humana, et non homo, sit pars hominis; quia partes quantitative corporis² sunt substancie per se in genere, quod negatur de humanitate; ergo sicut monstrum quod est homo geminus et a dyafragmate versus superius habet hominem eius partem, sic et Christus in casu posito. Non enim desinit esse completus homo secundum illam humanitatem propter continuacionem; sicut nec inciperet esse homo secundum istam humanitatem propter discontinuacionem. Concesso autem consequente videtur primo, si Christus sit quotlibet partes sue, quod multa quorum quodlibet³ est Christus; et cum Christus non sit aliud quam homo, sequitur quod multi homines sunt Christus et non propter continuacionem; ergo ante continuacionem fuit Christus multi homines; et non, si non ex assumpcione multarum humanitatum; ergo posito quod Christus multas humanitates assumat, consequitur ipsum esse multos homines, quod est intentum. Nec valet dicere quod Christus non habet partes huiusmodi, cum ex illis denominatur magnus, agens, beatus, et sic de formis ceteris que partibus suis insunt. Et si dicatur quod omnes sunt Christus et non pars eius, sed idem cum toto, tunc restat dicere secundum quam humanitatem Christus a Deo maioratur; cum non sit possibile humanitatem aggregari ex multis humanitatibus, nisi illa a qualibet illarum differat. Et patet quod secundum nullam illarum humatatum parcialium maioratur; ergo secundum illam totalem; et per consequens est nova humanitas sine correspondenti [p. 213] anima generata; quod est impossibile; cum nec corpus organicum correspondens tali humanitati fingi poterit, nec spiritus¹ implens materiam; nec corpus est maius propter iuxtaposicionem corporis quod non incidit in partem sui. Patet itaque logicis in casu posito quod ad omnem punctum Christi est non gradus augmentacionis, cum non sit motus nisi subiectum aliquod primo moveatur. Et hoc replicatur consequenter contra hoc Euclidis principium, ‘omne totum est maius sua parte quantitativa.’ Et posito² quod Christus assumat³ omnem naturam animalis; tunc post continuacionem omnium illorum factus monstrum foret alicubi non monstrum, et sic foret multe res, et per consequens multe substancie; quia aliter non foret pars sui, nisi pars et totum ponant in numerum.

Second, it seems to follow that Christ is any number of his [quantitative parts]{g:pars-quantitativa}. For before the continuation Christ was a composite of each of these souls and its body; from which it follows that Christ is many quantitative parts. Nor does it avail to say that the human nature, and not the man, is the part of a man; because the quantitative parts of a body are substances of themselves in a genus, which is denied of the humanity; therefore, as the monster that is a twin man, and from the diaphragm upward has a man [as] its part, so too Christ in the case supposed.[^10] For he does not cease to be a complete man as to that humanity on account of the continuation; just as neither would he begin to be a man as to this humanity on account of the discontinuation. And, the consequent being granted, it seems, first, if Christ is any number of his parts, that [he is] many [things] of which each is Christ; and since Christ is nothing other than a man, it follows that many men are Christ, and not on account of the continuation; therefore before the continuation Christ was many men; and not [so], if not from the assumption of many humanities; therefore, supposing that Christ assume many humanities, it follows that he is many men, which is the point intended.[^11] Nor does it avail to say that Christ does not have such parts, since from them he is [named]{g:denominare} great, acting, blessed, and so of the other forms which are in his parts. And if it be said that all are Christ and not a part of him, but the same with the whole, then it remains to say as to which humanity Christ is made greater by God; since it is not possible for a humanity to be aggregated from many humanities unless it differs from each of them. And it is clear that he is made greater as to none of those partial humanities; therefore as to that total [one]; and consequently there is a new humanity generated without a corresponding [213] soul; which is impossible; since neither could an organic body corresponding to such a humanity be feigned, nor a spirit filling the matter; nor is a body greater on account of the juxtaposition of a body that does not fall into its part.[^12] It is clear, therefore, to logicians, in the case supposed, that at every point of Christ there is no degree of [augmentation]{g:augmentacio}, since there is no motion unless some subject is first moved. And this is [rejoined]{g:replicatur} consequently against this principle of Euclid's: "every whole is greater than its quantitative part."[^13] And supposing that Christ assume every nature of animal: then, after the continuation of all of them, having been made a monster, he would somewhere not be a monster, and so would be many things, and consequently many substances; because otherwise he would not be a part of himself, unless part and whole make up a number.[^14]

## ¶4 · 213.15–213.28

> [p. 213] 3° potest poni casus de Dei omnipotencia quod infinitas naturas corporeas adaptet infinitis animabus, quarum prima sit dupla ad secundum,⁴ et sic in infinitum; et omnibus illis continuatis assumat quamlibet in ypostaticam unionem. Tunc videtur quod Christus sit infinite partes sue, et sic infinitum parvus, et per consequens maior seipso; et cum, ubicunque fuerit, est eque magnus racione multiplicacionis concomitancie, ut patet de eucaristia; sequitur quod in quotlibet locis est minor seipso. Ymo cum infinitas animas Deus potest producere et facere eas incommunicantes⁵ materias⁶ actuare, sequitur quod sit possibile⁷ quamlibet partem quantitativam Christi esse Christum; quod est impossibile, cum tunc non forent multe partes sui quantitative, ut tactum est superius proximo capitulo.

Third, a case can be posited from God's omnipotence: that he fit infinite bodily natures to infinite souls, of which the first is double the second, and so to infinity; and, all of them being made continuous, assume each into hypostatic union. Then it seems that Christ is infinitely [many of] his parts, and so infinitely small, and consequently greater than himself; and since, wherever he is, he is equally great by reason of the [multiplication of concomitance]{g:multiplicacio-concomitancie}, as is clear of the eucharist, it follows that in any number of places he is less than himself.[^15] Indeed, since God can produce infinite souls and make them actuate matters [that do not communicate]{g:incommunicans}, it follows that it is possible for each quantitative part of Christ to be Christ; which is impossible, since then there would not be many quantitative parts of him, as was touched on above in the preceding chapter.[^16]

## Notes

[^1]: **C/T.** "The topic from sufficient likeness": if the Word, joined to every animal, does not make every animal Christ, then by like reasoning many humanities do not make him only one man. "Preserved": *servatis*; B reads *sequitur*, and A first wrote *seiuat* (209.28).

[^2]: **C/T.** "The assumption": *assumptum*, the logician's premiss (that the union destroys nothing positive), not the assumption of a nature; *assumpcio* in the next clause is the assumption of the nature. A B C omit *non* (209.33), which the argument needs; O with the print. "Possible for those whom I address": the case is possible on the adversaries' terms; O reads *alloquitur* (210.5). A B C read *specificate* for *specifice* (210.4); O omits *distincta … individuum* (210.6–7).

[^3]: **C/T.** "By greater commonness": *secundum communioritatem*, the genus's being more common than its species. "Able to bray": *rudibile*, the stock difference of the ass; A B C read *hinibile* "able to neigh" for *irrudibile* (210.18), and omit *ceteris … de* (210.18–20) and *aliis* (210.20). A B read *tunc ergo*, O *insuper*, for *tunc* (210.17).

[^4]: **G/C/T.** *In quid*: predicated as what a thing is, as against *in quale*. "Even naturally": *vel naturaliter*; M reads *numeraliter* "numerically" (210.24), the print stands. "Species differ just as the individuals of a species do": *cum tam species differant quam individua*, or "as much as"; O reads *cum species plus differant* "since species differ more" (210.26), which would run against "greater distinction in individuals than in common forms" (210.8). The print stands.

[^5]: **C.** The syllogism shows the species persist through the assumption: the major is about things, the minor puts the species under it, and the conclusion is drawn without the fallacy of accident. B reads *speciei* for *nature* (211.1), A B C *existit* (211.1); O *illius* for *huius* (211.3), *eciam fuit* for *est* and omits *in principio* (211.4–5).

[^6]: **T.** O omits *natura omnis* (211.10); A B omit the conclusion *ergo … est* (211.11). C reads *tamen*, O *tunc*, for *cum* (211.6).

[^7]: **T.** "This being postulated": *petito*, with C O, against the print's *posito* "supposed" (211.15). A B C read *perdat* for *deperdat* (211.17) and *ut* for *et* (211.19). "Continuation": making the bodies one continuous body.

[^8]: **C/T/G.** *Potencia* (CONTEXTUAL) is "power" here, the natural capacity of body and soul. "Moderating however slowly … a very long space": the line is uncertain in the witnesses: A B C omit *tarde* (211.27), O omits *moderando … brevi* (211.27–28), and for *longissimum*, Harris's own word, A B C read *longis*, which does not construe; he thinks *longissimum* or *longum* necessary (211.28). The English follows the print. O reads *poñ* for *potencia* (211.25).

[^9]: **T.** A reads *preterquam* "except" for *postquam* (212.1).

[^10]: **C/T.** "Substances of themselves in a genus": a quantitative part (a hand, a half) is a substance in the category; a humanity is not, so the part of a man must be a man. The twin monster: one body from the diaphragm down, two men above, each a part of the whole. B omits *corporis* (212.9).

[^11]: **C/T.** "[He is] many [things] of which each is Christ": the clause has no verb; perhaps a word is lost. "Not [so], if not from": he was many men only by the assumption of many humanities.

[^12]: **C/T.** "Humanities": the print's *humatatum* (212.32–33) for *humanitatum*. A B C read *species* for *spiritus* (213.3). A body set beside another does not enlarge it unless it becomes its part.

[^13]: **S/C.** Euclid, *Elements* I, common notion 5 (8 in some numberings), "the whole is greater than the part"; Wyclif adds "quantitative" (LEDGER I12c-1, to be checked). "Rejoined": *replicatur*, raised against an answer; continuation would make the whole no greater than its part.

[^14]: **C/T.** "Make up a number": *ponant in numerum*, count as two. O reads *proposito* for *posito* (213.9) and *assumendo* for *assumat* (213.10).

[^15]: **C/T.** "Double the second": the parts diminish by halves to infinity, as the proportional parts of a continuum. B C read *duplam* (213.17). "Multiplication of concomitance": as Christ's whole body is under each part of the host. "Infinitely small": *infinitum parvus*, the print's paradox, as at 207.31.

[^16]: **S/T.** "The preceding chapter": chapter 11 (LEDGER I12c-2, to be checked). O reads *communicantes* "that communicate" for *incommunicantes* (213.24); B adds *creature* after *materias* (213.25); A reads *impossibile* for *possibile* (213.25).
